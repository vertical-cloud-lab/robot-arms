#### Claim 14: counterexamples for remote operation of a lab arm (2018-2026)

A helper agent searched; starred items I re-checked in the downloaded texts. The shared web-search budget

**Peer-reviewed, a lab arm, remote latency measured (but not its effect on outcomes)**

- **Li et al. 2020, *Adv. Sci.* 7, 1901957 (10.1002/advs.201901957).** The arm is a collaborative robot in a
  materials-synthesis lab, driven remotely in VR.
  - Main text \*: "the administrator can remotely operate the lab through VR and train MAOS for the whole
    experimental operation details"; Fig. 3a: "Remote users with VR glasses and gamepad operate the robot in
    a virtual lab."
  - SI Table S2 \*: ping 140 / 80 / 22 ms and 9 / 75 / 385 Mbps over 3G / 4G / 5G. SI §S5 \*: "22ms Ping also
    significantly enhanced the preciseness of remote control of robot cells". That effect is asserted, not
    measured.
- **Li et al. 2020, *Nat. Commun.* 11, 2046 (10.1038/s41467-020-15728-5), "intelligent cloud lab".**
  - Supplementary Table 3 \*: ping 39 / 75 / 19 ms over WLAN / 4G / 5G. Supplementary Method 2: "405Mbps data
    rate and 19ms Ping significantly enhanced the preciseness of remote control of experimental platform."
  - This is remote supervision of an autonomous platform (a Kinova arm moves samples), not teleoperation.
- **LIRA** (U1's own source, row 12) gives ~28/12 ms (wired) and ~45/15 ms (local 5G) for remote-inspection
  clients. That is LAN only, and no humans operate remotely.

**Preprints with remote-mode task outcomes**

- **TeleopLab, Chen et al., arXiv 2509.05547 (2025).** The closest analogue to BYU's plan. Remote students
  drive a UR5e to load a Mark-10 tensile tester \*.
  - Usage: "Six students participated in the remote tensile strength labs ... over three weeks, resulting in
    14 man-hours of testing. Participants connected to TeleopLab through cellular data, home WiFi, or company
    WiFi."
  - Outcome: "The 46.1% decrease in cycle time suggests that as students became more comfortable with the
    system, their efficiency improved".
  - Gaps: there is no local baseline. On-site staff handled preloading and unloading and resolved robot
    errors, but those interventions were not counted.
- **PUPPET, Vats et al., arXiv 2611.26350 (Nov 2026).** 30 participants moved an object with a TIAGo 7-DOF
  arm in person, by 2D teleoperation and by VR teleoperation \*.
  - Completion: "Participants completed the task in 68.66 s through PUPPET, in 94.96 s through 2D
    teleoperation, and in 72.10 s in person".
  - Interventions: "89.7% of VR trials ran without technician assistance, against 67.2% in 2D and 93.1% in
    person".
  - Latency: "The measured VR command-to-motion feedback latency had a median of 30.6 ms" (over a WiFi 6
    access point).
  - Gaps: it is a robotics testbed, not a science lab, and the operators were apparently on the local
    network rather than across the internet.
- **Wu et al. (LLNL), arXiv 2611.18496 (Nov 2026), "Toward Resilient Self-Driving Laboratories through
  Telerobotic Interventions".** A UR3e is teleoperated with a 3D mouse to fix SDL faults; 6 users, 86 trials.
  Result \*: "in the PI scenario, success improved from 71% to 86% under the decoupled mode". It does not
  show the operators were off-site, and it has no latency or local comparison.

**Weak cases**

- Synchrotron sample-changer robots:
  - Mueller et al. 2025, *J. Synchrotron Rad.* 32, 766: ">95% of all beamtimes ... remote".
  - Lazo et al. 2021, 28, 1649: 99.95% reliability over >35,000 samples.
  - Neither splits robot outcomes by remote versus on-site.
- Other cases, with no arm or with operators in the same room:
  - Fitzpatrick et al. 2018, *Angew.*: flow chemistry run across continents, no arm.
  - Soong et al. 2021, *J. Chem. Educ.*: servo titration with a ~0.5 s delay. Abstract only.
  - Trinitatova et al. 2022 and Lopez Pulgarin et al. 2022.

**Net:** U1 is wrong that no measured remote-specific numbers exist: latency has been measured for remote
control of lab arms since 2020, and remote task and intervention metrics exist in 2025-2026 preprints. As far
as this truncated search reached, what has not been published is a **peer-reviewed remote-versus-local
comparison on a science-lab arm, over the public internet, with interventions per operation and task
success**.

#### Corrections that matter for the lab's project choices

1. **An arm tending 3D printers in a closed loop is not unpublished.** U1 says it is in Part 4d, Rank 5 and
   Summary item 4; that is **wrong**.
   - BU's BEAR did it in 2020: a UR5e, five MakerGear printers, a scale and an Instron, run by Bayesian
     optimisation "without a human in the loop".
   - MAMA BEAR ran 25,387 such experiments in 2024.
   - IMDEA's 2023 *Digital Discovery* platform, from the same group as the preprint U1 cites, probably did it
     too, but it is unread.

   A BYU printer-tending project can't claim to be first. It can still be new in other ways:
   - a sub-$5k arm and consumer (Bambu) printers, including build-plate handling;
   - measured failure and intervention rates for that loop;
   - remote users;
   - the powder or furnace side.

   Use MAMA BEAR's lesson: let the bed cool before removal, because pulling parts off a warm bed deformed
   them.
2. **Fontenot's 98.5% is the *uncorrected* first-attempt rate**, and 100% is the rate after discard-and-replace
   (n = 130). U1 Rank 3 calls 98.5% the "corrected accuracy". So an open-loop versus corrected comparison
   already exists inside that paper, for one task. What is still open:

#### Corrections that matter

1. **A-Lab numbers are out of date (U2, table and §4).** Replace "41 of 58 targets (71%), 355 experiments" with "36 of 57 (63%), 353 experiments". The source is the Author Correction in *Nature* 650, E1 (published 2026-01-19, doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09992-y), which reclassified 4 results as inconclusive and dropped one target that was in the training data.
2. **LabRobFail severity (U3 §1 and the pre-registration plan).** "Four severity levels L1–L4" is wrong. L1–L3 are the benchmark's cognitive levels. Severity has four *named* levels: Minor / Recoverable / Critical / Catastrophic in the main text, but Risk / Dangerous / Fatal / Management Incident in Appendix Table 8. If the campaign codes severity with LabRobFail, say which naming it uses.
3. **RaC (U3 table and §2).** 61/100 vs 22/100 is a *simulation* result (Fig. 11). Cite RaC as IEEE T-RO 42:2843–2860 (2026), doi:10.1109/TRO.2026.3706552, not by its arXiv DOI with year 2025.
4. **Jovan / Pradhan sentences (U3 §5).** Both carry the wrong citation key (Paz 2020). The negative-binomial "extensions for overdispersion" claim is unsupported, Jovan's counts are human activity rather than interventions, and Pradhan is a weak source. Rewrite or drop these sentences.
5. **Von Rohr "quotes" (U2 §1 and §6) are Edison paraphrases presented as quotations.** Use the real sentences from the table above. Remove the Von Rohr citation from the flowability sentence.
6. **AlloyBot (U2 §4).** There are two documents, the Research Square preprint and Boonpetch's MS thesis, not one "Selvaraj/Boonpetch preprint". "<1 h/day maintenance" and "better than 1 at.%" are design objectives. 1 alloy/h and 24/day are schedule capacity, and validation covers only three alloys. It is also worth stating that AlloyBot deliberately avoids powder feedstock.
7. **Sirius (U3).** 0.32 ± 0.11, 2.80 interventions per trajectory and 15.28 timesteps per intervention exist only in the IJRR version. Only the mean and range of the ratio appear in verbatim snippets, so treat the SD and the per-intervention figures as unverified until someone reads the IJRR table. They describe the multi-operator dataset.
8. **HIL-SERL (U3 §2).** "Within 1–2.5 h on *nearly* all tasks" (the timing belt took 6 h). The arms are Franka, per the official code. "UR5" in U3's list of research-grade arms is unsupported.

#### Corrections that matter

**U4 (printer tending)**

1. **Heating vs cooling is inverted.** MAMA BEAR removed plastics from a heated bed until experiment 15,678. Height-QC failures showed this deformed the parts, and the fix was to **cool** PLA, PETG, nylon and TPU-3 to 30 °C before removal. U4 says bed heating was the fix. U4 is also wrong that BEAR 2020 gave no release temperature: Gongora et al. 2020 Methods say "the robotic arm retrieved the structure when the bed temperature was below 40°C" (PLA, printed on an 85/75 °C bed). Both papers point the same way for BYU's PLA: let the bed cool before the arm pulls a part. Neither used PEI. MAMA BEAR printed on polyimide tape on glass (SI §5.7), and BEAR 2020 does not name its bed surface, so U4's statement that cooling "substantially reduces adhesion on textured PEI" has no support in the sources it cites. The same lab's bed-temperature study (Snapp, Gongora & Brown 2021, PLA on glass and polyimide, abstract only) found adhesion peaks at 60–70 °C, that cooling below 40 °C adds little, and that hot removal is often easier. Hot removal is exactly what deformed parts in MAMA BEAR, so the release temperature for PLA on textured PEI is an open question for BYU to measure.
2. **FlexTrak never removed a real print automatically.** It is a WPI undergraduate MQP (not peer reviewed). The automated cycle ran on an empty bed, print removal through the bending track was shown only by hand, and Covid-19 stopped the planned tests. The scraper failures belong to the previous year's design. "Rated the most reliable" is one background sentence. U4's own last line ("lack of demonstrated autonomous sheet-flex removal") is the accurate one.
3. **Force arithmetic.** Breaking the Prusa MK3 sheet's magnetic hold took 15.48–16 N, which is above, not "well within", the PiPER's ~15 N grip, and about equal to its 1.5 kg (≈14.7 N) payload. The 6.76 N figure applies only after the edge is already lifted. All these values are for a Prusa MK3 sheet; the A1 mini's 180 mm plate was not measured. Kaščak's 53 N is PETG pulled perpendicular to a hot (75 °C) PEI bed, so it is not a PLA or a cold-removal number.
4. **Snapp et al. report no handling success rate.** The only yields are 13,250 valid out of 25,387 experiments (52%, all failure causes mixed), ~50 experiments per day and ~60% utilisation. U4 also omits the up-to-three re-grab rule.
5. **SmartLab.** The printer is named (Anet Pro). The 2.5× daily throughput comes from running 24 h against an 8 h human day; per specimen the robot is slower (65 vs 57 min). The text and Table 2 disagree (664 vs 633 per month). No success or failure counts are given.
6. **Minor:** the ATCAM 25.4 mm sphere is the coupon itself, not a separate striker. Two quotations are paraphrases in quotation marks (Kaščak's PLA sentence; FlexTrak's flex-plate sentence).
7. **Omitted systems** (found in the earlier U1 check): Aroca et al. 2017, *Rapid Prototyp. J.* (a 2-DOF manipulator pushes parts off the bed); ARE RoboFab (Buchanan et al. 2024, *Robotics* 13:157: an arm removes printed skeletons, "around 25 minutes to allow the print bed to cool"); and IMDEA's 2023 *Digital Discovery* paper on robotic pellet 3D printing and testing (not yet read in full). U4 says its IMDEA source handled only injection-moulded parts, which is true of the 2026 preprint but not of the group's 2023 work.
8. **Gap claims (row 6).** The print-farm gap holds only at farm scale. Peer-reviewed small-cell numbers exist, and U4 left out the most relevant one: Detect-Remove-Replace (Li et al. 2022, *IEEE RAM*), in which a robot removed prints in 20 of 21 tests and replaced the steel sheet "with a 100% success rate", with 85.11% defect detection (abstract only; no trial count given for the sheet swaps). The PLA-on-PEI gap holds provisionally; Pollák & Goryl 2025, which tested PLA on a PEI film and on a textured plate, could break it but could not be read in full.
9. **Camera-check caution.** U4 recommends MobileNetV2 at "≥95% accuracy with modest training data". The 97% (Aktepe 2026) is accuracy on a held-out split of one public Kaggle image set (1,912 images, five classes, no "spaghetti" class). It is not a test on a new printer or camera.

**U5 (agent-written code, safeguards, designed hardware)**

1. **Chemist Eye: 97% is wrong for the version U5 cites.** The peer-reviewed *Digital Discovery* abstract says 88% for hazard spotting (the arXiv preprint says 97%); 95% for decisions holds in both. Neither number is an aggregate. 97% is the best model–prompt pair for posture-based accident detection, the 95% averages the best prompt settings over 10-trial sets, and testing used recorded streams. The authors say it "is not yet ready for full-scale use as a safety system". U5's quotation "human judgment remains necessary" is not in the paper.
2. **LabscriptAI mixes versions.** 96.7% on 90 tasks is from v3 (Sep 2026), whose stricter StatePass is 73/90 (81%). "CV <5%, R² >0.999" and "318 GFP variants" are from v1/v2 (Oct–Nov 2025). v3 reports R² 0.985–0.999 and 854 GFP designs.

Mean time to success is 280 s (211 s of it reasoning) against 62 s for π0.5, with per-task means of 94–601 s. U5's "selected tasks 9/9, 94–208 s, total not reported" covers only four of the twelve rows.
4. **WetRobo.** The incubator-door times (2 h 40 min, then 3 min 27 s) are from Lab X; **the door task failed in Lab Y**. Cap lifting was 10/10 in both labs on repeated runs after adaptation, against the VLA's 0/10 in Lab Y, so a denominator is reported, and tokens are counted (41.4 M and 23.0 M). The kit has two PiPERs, but every run used the right arm.
5. **ACE-CaP-0 runs on two AgileX PiPER X arms**, the closest published precedent to BYU's hardware, which U5 does not mention. Development used human kinesthetic demonstrations on request and up to 10 human-assisted resets per task.
6. **The safeguard table leans on one survey and mixes simulation with hardware.** "li2026whentheworld" is Kaixin Li et al., arXiv 2611.22291 (Nov 2026), a survey preprint cited 13 times. The three numbers checked against the primary papers (NeSyRo, RoboGuard, SEAR) are correct. But only NeSyRo is a real arm. Code-as-Monitor, LEASH, DroneServer, SEAR and SafeVLA-Bench are simulated; RoboGuard (Jackal), VASO (Jackal/PX4) and Lang2LTL (Spot) are mobile robots or drones. 7. **SPINE** checks out (four PiPERs; Claude Code + Sonnet 5.0 human baseline; 38% and ~80%). But the human arm is 9 trials per scenario against 3 for SPINE, the P-HW1 human SD is about ±13 min, and the 100% vs 99% is a mean fraction of faults fixed, not trial success.
8. **Designed-hardware gap claim.** Keep the strict version and drop "no published evidence exists". Suggested wording: "We found no published study in which an LLM or other generative-AI system designed labware, fixtures or adapters for a laboratory-automation robot that were then fabricated and tested on it, and none reporting first-pass fit, iterations or human edits. The nearest precedents are outside lab automation: VLM-designed tools 3D-printed and used on a real xArm (RobotSmith, 2025 preprint, qualitative), diffusion-designed gripper fingers printed in PLA and tested on a real gripper (Xu et al., CoRL 2024), and LLM-co-designed hardware built by people (Stella et al. 2023; Makatura et al. 2023)." Re-run the search when budget allows, since lab-automation-specific and 2026 work was not swept.
9. **Paraphrases presented as quotations:** the two La Agente Óptima ledger passages (the facts are right) and Chemist Eye's "human judgment remains necessary" (not in the paper).
