### C1. Per-operation handling times with spread, human intervention rates, and DES validation in a materials/alloy development loop

**Verdict: Survives as written.**

The closest prior work is MATTERIX, a GPU-accelerated digital twin for robotics-assisted chemistry laboratory automation. MATTERIX validates simulated chemistry workflows against real-world robot execution, reporting, for example, a 75% pick-and-place success rate over 12 trials and a 90% liquid-pouring success rate over 10 experiments, with sim-to-real volume agreement of 59.6 ± 7.9 mL versus 61.3 ± 4.4 mL (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14). However, it does not report per-operation handling-time distributions (with spread), human intervention rates, or validate a discrete-event simulation against measured timing data (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 9-11, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 7-9). In pharmaceutical quality control, Lopes et al. (doi:10.1080/00207543.2019.1683250) built a DES-based digital twin using empirical processing-time data and reported aggregate time-in-system with standard deviations across simulation runs (lopes2020pharmaceuticalqualitycontrol pages 7-9, lopes2020pharmaceuticalqualitycontrol pages 9-13). However, this study did not report individual operation-level handling times with their statistical spread, human intervention rates, nor was it in a materials or alloy development context. The comprehensive SDL reviews by Tom et al. (doi:10.1021/acs.chemrev.4c00055) and Lee et al. (doi:10.1039/d5mh01984b) describe many SDL platforms but do not identify any study reporting the specific conjunction of operation-level timing distributions, intervention rates, and DES validation for a materials workflow.

**Recommendation:** The claim survives. Emphasize the conjunction: operation-level handling times *with spread*, human intervention rates, and DES validation against those measurements, specifically across a hands-on materials/alloy development loop (atomization, powder characterization, etc.).

---

### C2. Low-cost (<$5k) robot arm clearing consumer FDM printer build plates, with CI over many trials and camera false-clear rate

**Verdict: Survives as written.**

Andrews et al. (doi:10.1007/978-981-99-3330-3_27) demonstrated a 7-DOF KUKA arm with a ROBOTIQ gripper autonomously removing parts from an Ender 3 V2 printer, achieving 10/10 success in part removal and 10/10 in bed cleaning (andrews2023automated3dprintermaintenance pages 9-12). However, the KUKA arm far exceeds the $5,000 price point, no confidence interval was computed (n = 10), and "computer vision is mentioned only as a proposed future improvement" with no measured camera false-clear rate (andrews2023automated3dprintermaintenance pages 9-12). Wang et al. (doi:10.3390/app151810222) reported a digital-twin-driven sorting system for a 16-printer farm with 98.7% grasping success, but this used an industrial articulated robot, not a sub-$5k arm, and the camera-verification details were not quantified as a false-clear rate (wang2025digitaltwindrivensorting pages 18-19, wang2025digitaltwindrivensorting pages 14-16). Li et al. (doi:10.1109/MRA.2021.3103478) described a "Detect-Remove-Replace" robotic continuous 3D-printing system, but the full paper was unobtainable during this search. A thesis by ME (2020) reviewed scraper-based automated part removal with qualitative video analysis, noting prints frequently remained on the scraper, without statistical success metrics (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 23-28).

No study was found that jointly satisfies: (a) arm cost under $5,000, (b) many physical trials with a binomial confidence interval, and (c) a measured camera false-clear rate.

**Recommendation:** The claim survives. The novelty rests on the specific *conjunction* of a sub-$5k arm, a large trial set with CI, and a camera-based clear-check with a separately characterized false-clear rate.

### C3. Robot arm restocking consumables into a gantry liquid handler with measured success over hundreds of operations

**Verdict: Needs narrowing.**

Burger's doctoral thesis (doi:10.17638/03087073, non-peer-reviewed) describes a KUKA mobile robot that transferred vial racks into and out of a GC headspace-sampler station, reporting "464 successful transfers of racks from the robot into the station and 464 successful transfers from the station back onto the robot, with no reported failures" over 71 iterations in a two-hour robustness test (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 62-69). The same platform handled vials and Quantos powder-dispensing cartridges, though detailed vial-level success rates were not separately reported (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 79-83). Carruthers (2025, non-peer-reviewed thesis) described a similar mobile robot for autonomous solar-fuels research that loads/unloads vial racks at multiple workstations, stating the system supports "more than a week of unattended operation," but provides no numerical success-rate metric (carruthers2025amobilerobotic pages 27-32). Ochiai et al. (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2501.05789, preprint/later peer-reviewed) proposed the self-maintainability (SeM) concept treating restocking as "care" but reported no primary experimental data (ochiai2025automatingcareby pages 9-12, ochiai2025automatingcareby pages 12-14).

**Recommendation:** The claim as written — "or similar laboratory instrument" — is partially undermined by Burger's GC-station result, which demonstrates robotic restocking of vial racks into a lab instrument with hundreds of measured operations. Narrow the claim to restocking *replaceable consumables into the deck positions of a gantry liquid handler* (e.g., OT-2 or CubXL tip racks, plates, or vial carriers), where no published precedent was found.

### C4. ISO 9283-style characterization for the AgileX PiPER or SO-100/SO-101 arms

**Verdict: Survives as written.**

No published study was found that performs an ISO 9283-style independent characterization of pose repeatability or accuracy for the AgileX PiPER, SO-100, or SO-101 arms. Wojtkowski (doi:10.32920/32288082, thesis, non-peer-reviewed) used the AgileX PiPER for pick-and-place experiments and cited a manufacturer-specified repeatability of ±0.1 mm, but performed no independent metrology (wojtkowski2026developmentofa pages 27-31). The PiPER and SO-100/SO-101 appear extensively in the robotics literature as platforms for imitation learning and VLA evaluation, but always with task success as the metric, never with traceable pose measurements. Chajón et al. (doi:10.3390/robotics15050086) performed photogrammetric characterization following selected ISO 9283 elements (30 repetitions at 5 poses), achieving sub-millimeter repeatability—but on a UR3, not any of the claimed arms (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 1-3, chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 8-10). Oghalai et al. (doi:10.1016/j.mfglet.2026.08.019) calibrated a low-cost Annin AR4 MK3 arm using camera-based measurements validated with dial indicators, reducing position error from 51 mm to 6.0 mm, but this is a different platform (oghalai2026anoncontactlow pages 1-2).

**Recommendation:** The claim survives. It is straightforwardly novel because these specific arms are very new and have not received formal metrology. Specify the ISO 9283 test protocol (number of poses, approach directions, full workspace coverage, measurement uncertainty) and ensure traceable ground truth.

### C5. LLM agent designing a 3D-printed lab fixture validated on a real robot, with first-pass fit rates compared to human-designed parts

**Verdict: Needs narrowing to "laboratory fixture."**

RoboBuilder (arXiv:2611.23855, preprint) is the closest published system. An LLM coding agent designs robot mechanisms including custom 3D-printed parts (e.g., worm-driven whegs, cam wheels, hoops), physically fabricates them, and validates them on real obstacle courses. 135 robots were built and tested; initially 0 of 15 simulated-pass designs crossed the real course (0% first-pass success in 44 attempts), rising to 73% after simulator learning and to 93% after agents redesigned based on failure videos (nehme2026robobuilderagenticrobot pages 7-8, nehme2026robobuilderagenticrobot pages 1-2, nehme2026robobuilderagenticrobot pages 3-5). However, these are robot locomotion mechanisms, not laboratory fixtures, holders, or instrument adapters. Ocker et al. (arXiv:2503.04417, preprint) demonstrated an LLM-driven multi-agent CAD system for 3D-printable models, but without physical robot validation. Yoshikawa et al. (doi:10.1007/s10514-023-10136-2) used LLMs to generate chemistry-robot task plans executed on a real robot, but did not design physical fixtures.

No published study was found in which an LLM agent designed a *laboratory fixture, holder, or adapter* that was 3D-printed and validated on a laboratory robot with first-pass fit/function rates compared against human-designed counterparts.

**Recommendation:** Narrow the claim to explicitly state "laboratory-specific fixture, holder, or instrument adapter" and "compared against human-designed baselines on the same fit/function criteria." The broader concept of LLM-designed, 3D-printed, physically validated robot hardware is now demonstrated by RoboBuilder.

### C6. Human takeovers during scripted lab robot operation used as training data for a learned recovery policy, with intervention rate tracked over the campaign

**Verdict: False as written — needs major narrowing.**

Multiple published studies use human teleoperated corrections during autonomous robot policy execution as training data for learned recovery or correction policies. RaC (Hu et al., IEEE Transactions on Robotics, doi:10.48550/arxiv.2509.07953) explicitly collects human takeovers during policy rollouts, structuring each intervention into recovery and correction segments that are used to train a learned recovery policy for long-horizon bimanual manipulation tasks including shirt hanging and lid sealing (hu2026racrobotlearning pages 6-8, hu2026racrobotlearning pages 2-4). TRANSIC (Jiang et al., arXiv:2405.10315) records teleoperated intervention data with a binary intervention indicator and trains a gated residual correction policy for sim-to-real transfer on contact-rich tasks including furniture assembly; "increasing the correction dataset from 25% to 75% of the full dataset improves TRANSIC's average performance by 42%" (jiang2024transicsimtorealpolicy pages 1-3, jiang2024transicsimtorealpolicy pages 6-8, jiang2024transicsimtorealpolicy pages 3-4). HACTS (Xu et al., arXiv:2503.24070, preprint) enables "bilateral, real-time joint synchronization" that allows the human to "intervene seamlessly while collecting action-correction data for future learning" (xu2025hactsahumanascopilot pages 1-2). Mandlekar et al. (arXiv:2012.06733, preprint) demonstrated iterative human-in-the-loop imitation learning where a remote operator takes control when the policy fails (mandlekar2020humanintheloopimitationlearning pages 1-2).

However, critically: (a) none of these works operate in a *scientific laboratory* workflow context (they address household manipulation, furniture assembly, or generic manipulation benchmarks); (b) none use *scripted or fiducial-guided* operation as the baseline interrupted by takeovers; and (c) none explicitly *track the intervention rate over the campaign* (i.e., show it declining as a time series or over rounds of data collection).

**Recommendation:** Rewrite the claim as: the first demonstration in a *scientific laboratory workflow* where takeovers interrupt an otherwise scripted or fiducial-guided arm, train a recovery/correction policy, and the intervention rate — with denominator and uncertainty — is tracked longitudinally across the experimental campaign. The broad concept of learning from teleoperated takeovers is well-established prior art and cannot be claimed as novel.
