# Q4: Unattended Operation, Safety, and the Control Plane for a Cloud-Operated SDL

## 1. Standards, Regulations, and What Actually Binds a University Lab

### 1.1 The Standards Landscape

The relevant standards form a hierarchy. **ISO 10218-1/-2 (2025 revision)** addresses industrial robot safety at the manufacturer and integrator level, requiring a cell-level risk assessment that covers safeguarded, restricted, operational, and collaborative spaces, validated protective stops, emergency stops, and integration documentation (ahmed2025humanrobotinteractiondynamic pages 19-23, marino2022frameworkmultilivelloper pages 17-21). **ISO/TS 15066** supplements ISO 10218 with guidance on the four collaborative operation modes: safety-rated monitored stop (SRMS), hand guiding, speed-and-separation monitoring (SSM), and power-and-force limiting (PFL), with body-region-specific transient and quasi-static contact force and pressure limits (ahmed2025humanrobotinteractiondynamic pages 19-23, marino2022frameworkmultilivelloper pages 17-21). Example collaborative cobot limits cited are 0.23 m/s speed, 149 N force, and 79 W dynamic power (ahmed2025humanrobotinteractiondynamic pages 19-23).

**ISO 13849-1** provides the Performance Level (PL a–e) framework for safety-related parts of control systems, requiring each safety function to be designed to an architecture category with appropriate component reliability, diagnostic coverage, and common-cause failure resistance. **IEC 62061** provides a parallel SIL claim limit (SILCL) assessment. In the humanoid robot feasibility study, the external detection and evaluation subsystems each achieved PL e / SILCL 3, but the robot-side reaction chain remained uncertifiable because the PiPER—like any uncertified low-cost arm—lacks a characterized probability of dangerous failure per hour (PFHD) for its stopping behavior (ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 6-8, ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 4-5, ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 2-3). **IEC 61508** is the parent generic functional safety lifecycle standard; it is not normally applied directly to machinery but may be invoked where ISO 13849-1 or IEC 62061 do not adequately cover a function (wahid2026regulatoryandsafety pages 4-5).

A critical point: PL and SIL are assigned **per safety function**, not to the laboratory as a whole. A PL e-rated safety relay cannot retroactively make an uncertified robot reaction path PL e; the entire chain from sensor through logic to final element must satisfy the claimed integrity (ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 6-8, ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 1-2).

### 1.2 The US Regulatory and Institutional Reality

In the United States, the ISO standards are voluntary consensus standards. They become effectively binding through several mechanisms: (a) OSHA's General Duty Clause (OSH Act §5(a)(1)) requires employers to provide a workplace free from recognized serious hazards, and consensus standards serve as evidence of recognized practice; (b) **ANSI/RIA R15.06** is the US national robot safety consensus standard, historically harmonized with ISO 10218 (ahmed2025humanrobotinteractiondynamic pages 19-23); (c) university EHS committees and institutional review processes typically require documented risk assessments and may impose specific standards as conditions of approval; and (d) insurers may require compliance with specific standards as conditions of coverage.

**What published SDLs have actually done:** No published self-driving laboratory located in this review has documented formal third-party ISO 10218 certification or a completed ISO 13849-1 PL validation for its robot safety functions (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 2-4, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 21-23). The most comprehensive published safety architecture is **Safe-SDL**, which proposes a defense-in-depth framework with Operational Design Domains (ODDs), Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), and a CRUTD transactional safety protocol, but explicitly frames these as architectural proposals rather than certified implementations (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 14-16, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 1-2, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 8-9). This is a significant gap in the field: the SDL community has published extensively on workflow orchestration and optimization but very little on formal safety engineering.

### 1.3 Risk Assessment Methodology

For an SDL that combines software-controlled robots, furnaces, reactive powders, and remote supervision, **STPA (Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis)** is the most appropriate primary hazard analysis method. STPA models the system as a hierarchical control structure and examines unsafe control actions (UCAs) and causal scenarios across controllers, software, humans, and the environment (qi2026towardssaferlearningenabled pages 32-38, glish2023astampbasedapproach pages 4-7, glish2023astampbasedapproach pages 1-4). Unlike FMEA, which focuses on individual component failures, STPA can address system-level interactions, feedback loops, software errors, and hazards that arise without any component failing—precisely the kind of scenario where an autonomous retry loop or a stale MQTT message causes harm (qi2026towardssaferlearningenabled pages 52-56). HAZOP is valuable for the furnace and powder-handling process nodes but requires adaptation for software-intensive systems (qi2026towardssaferlearningenabled pages 52-56). The recommended approach is: **ISO 12100** as the overall risk assessment framework, supplemented by **STPA** for the control structure, **HAZOP** for the furnace/powder process, and **FMEA/FMECA** for hardware component faults.

For 10 μm aluminium powder, the maximum explosion pressure was 0.64 MPa and the deflagration index (Kst) was approximately 144 bar·m/s, measured at 200 g/m³ in a 20 L sphere (chen2022investigationonthe pages 4-6). For 20 μm aluminium, Kst dropped to approximately 43 bar·m/s (chen2022investigationonthe pages 4-6). Silicon dust has a reported Kst of approximately 120 bar·m/s at optimum concentration (kaya2026calculationsforhazardous pages 41-45). These values are strongly particle-size dependent and must be obtained through lot-specific testing rather than handbook lookup (kaya2026calculationsforhazardous pages 41-45). AlSi10Mg alloy-specific data were not found in the reviewed literature and must be tested independently.

**Static from a printed enclosure or plastic funnel is a real ignition source.** The MIE of fine aluminium powder is typically in the range of 10–50 mJ (extrapolation flagged—obtain lot-specific data), which is well within the energy range of electrostatic discharges from insulating plastic surfaces. FDM-printed enclosures (PLA, PETG, ABS) are insulators that can accumulate static charge during powder transfer. A powder-contact funnel or chute printed on a Bambu Lab printer would be an ungrounded insulating surface capable of generating brush discharges with energy sufficient to ignite fine aluminium dust. All conductive powder-handling equipment must be bonded and grounded with verified continuity. Non-conductive components must be replaced with static-dissipative or conductive alternatives, or eliminated from the powder-contact path.

**Enclosure and exhaust design:** The fume hood provides containment and local exhaust ventilation, but the exhaust system must be designed for combustible metal dust. Aluminium dust must not be routed into a general-purpose dust collector or a wet scrubber where water contact with fine aluminium could generate hydrogen. A dedicated, NFPA 484/660-compliant collection system with spark-resistant ductwork and appropriate isolation is required where adopted codes apply.

**Class D fire suppression:** Burning aluminium reacts violently with water (steam explosion risk and hydrogen generation) and with CO₂ (aluminium reduces CO₂ to carbon and aluminium oxide, sustaining combustion). Standard ABC extinguishers, water sprinklers, and CO₂ systems are all contraindicated. A Class D extinguishing agent (e.g., Met-L-X, sodium chloride-based, or dry sand) must be provided, and all on-site personnel must be trained to recognize that applying water to a metal fire will make it catastrophically worse.

### 2.2 Furnace Hazards

The critical furnace scenario is: the arm faults mid-insert with the furnace door open. If the safety system unconditionally removes arm power, the arm may drop a hot crucible, creating a spill of hot material and potentially a powder ignition event. The safe-state design must account for this: a mid-insert fault should trigger a controlled arm retraction or a hold-in-place before power removal, with the furnace door closing only after the arm is confirmed clear of the aperture. This requires a cause-and-effect matrix that addresses the arm, furnace, and their interaction states. Safe-SDL identifies thermal runaway and exothermic reactions as domain-specific hazards requiring real-time monitoring, forward prediction against a safe set, and automated shutdown (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 16-18, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 18-21).

### 2.3 The Arm Itself

The AgileX PiPER is a 6-DOF arm with 1.5 kg payload and 626 mm reach, costing approximately $3k. At this price point, it almost certainly does not have certified safety-rated monitored stop, certified safe torque off, or validated stopping-time characterization with a published PFHD. This means the arm **cannot independently claim any Performance Level under ISO 13849-1** for its stopping behavior (ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 6-8, ding2608towardcertifiedfunctional pages 1-2). An external safety relay, hardwired e-stop chain, and guard interlocking are not merely advisable—they are the only mechanism by which a defensible safety function can be constructed. The e-stop chain must use positive-opening contacts, dual-channel monitoring, manual reset with restart inhibit, and must not pass through MQTT, Tailscale, a Raspberry Pi, or any ordinary computing element.

At 1.5 kg payload and modest speed, the pinch and crush hazard is moderate but not negligible. A 1.5 kg payload at 1 m/s generates approximately 0.75 J of kinetic energy; the hazard is dominated by trapping geometry (e.g., crushing a finger between a crucible and a fixture) rather than free-space impact. The polycarbonate enclosure with interlocked access door is the primary mitigation for unexpected entry.

## 3. Fault Detection, Containment, and Safe-State Design

### 3.1 Spacecraft FDIR as the Transferable Model

The spacecraft Fault Detection, Isolation, and Recovery (FDIR) architecture provides the most directly transferable guard architecture for an SDL. In the Masat-1 CubeSat, FDIR is organized hierarchically: housekeeping collectors and safety functions detect component-level problems; a safety monitor correlates events with actions; and the flight planner makes the highest-level FDIR decisions (latachi2020reusableandreliable pages 24-26). Recovery is attempted through command retries, parameter defaulting, watchdog-triggered resets, and receiver reinitialization. External EPS watchdogs periodically ping the onboard computer; after five failed pings they reset it. For unresolvable faults, the system transitions to a fail-safe mode: non-vital units are powered down and the spacecraft adopts a thermally and energetically safe configuration until ground contact (latachi2020reusableandreliable pages 24-26, latachi2020reusableandreliable pages 18-21).

For an SDL, this translates to:
- **Level 0 (device):** Current/torque limits, encoder watchdogs, temperature monitors on each device.
- **Level 1 (subsystem):** Workspace envelope violation, balance disagreement, camera loss, stale heartbeat → local stop and retry (bounded).
- **Level 2 (orchestrator):** Mass-balance audit failure, protocol-sequence violation, consumed/remaining inventory mismatch → HOLD state and alert.
- **Level 3 (supervisor):** Escalation to human, system-wide safe state, furnace shutdown, ventilation assurance.

### 3.2 VLM Supervision: Useful but Not Safety-Critical

The LabRobFail benchmark evaluated vision-language models for robotic failure detection in chemical SDLs. The specialized LabRobFail-VLM achieves 90.83% failure-detection accuracy on seen environments, 77.21% temporal localization, and 83.18% severity assessment (wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 5-6, wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 1-2). However, under distribution shift (unseen objects and scenes), detection accuracy drops to 71.02%, and temporal localization falls to 41.41% (wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 6-7, wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 7-8). These results are explicitly insufficient for safety-critical gating: a 29% miss rate on unseen conditions is far below the >99% reliability needed for a safety interlock (wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 6-7). A VLM supervisor is appropriately used as a **supplementary anomaly-detection channel** that can trigger a HOLD state and page a human, but it must not be the sole gate on a hazardous action.

### 3.3 Safe-SDL's CRUTD Protocol

Safe-SDL proposes a transactional safety protocol: Create a request → Read/lock resources and capture state → simulate the transition → Test constraints → execute under monitoring → confirm the achieved state → abort/rollback on failure (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 8-9, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 4-8). This directly addresses the Q1 provenance failure where insert time was conflated with measurement time: the protocol separates requested, validated, executed, and confirmed states with distinct timestamps.

### 3.4 Recovery vs. Halt: The Autonomous Retry Problem

The critical design question is: when should the system retry, and when should it halt and page a human? The spacecraft pattern is clear: bounded local recovery at the lowest level, with escalation on failure (latachi2020reusableandreliable pages 6-9). The SDL equivalent is:
- **Retry:** A balance reading that disagrees by <1% on first attempt → re-weigh once. A gripper position error within repeatability → re-approach once.
- **Halt:** A balance disagreement >5%, a dropped object, a current spike, an unrecognized object in the workspace, any thermal anomaly, any loss of communication with safety-critical devices, or **any second consecutive failure of the same type**.

The autonomous retry loop that converts a recoverable fault into a catastrophic one is the most dangerous failure mode. The design rule is: **finite retry budget (typically 1–2), monotonically decreasing scope with each retry, automatic escalation to HOLD after budget exhaustion, and human-cleared recovery from HOLD** (colagrossi2026adigitaltwin pages 13-15, colagrossi2026adigitaltwin pages 10-11).

## 3b. Remote Supervision

### Latency, Bandwidth, and Video Quality

Remote vehicle operation studies indicate that communication latency above approximately 50 ms makes displayed imagery outdated for useful interaction; 5G networks may reduce latency below 10 ms (mutzenich2022situationawarenessin pages 35-36). Streaming video from four HD cameras requires at least 50 Mbit/s (mutzenich2022situationawarenessin pages 35-36). A latency spike of 3–4 seconds caused a remote operator to miss that a vehicle was approaching warehouse racks, creating crash risk (mutzenich2022situationawarenessin pages 190-193). For an SDL, this means the Tailscale VPN must provide consistent sub-100 ms round-trip latency, and the system must automatically transition to HOLD if video latency exceeds a defined threshold.

### The Out-of-the-Loop Problem

When a remote human is only summoned on exception, recovery time depends critically on the specificity of the alert. A specified failure (e.g., "balance timeout on station 3") took approximately 29 seconds to understand, whereas an unspecified problem took approximately 162 seconds (mutzenich2022situationawarenessin pages 35-36). This is a factor of 5.6×, which has direct implications for alarm design: every alert must include the failure type, affected station, current system state, available actions, and recommended response. HMD-360 presentation produced faster decisions than screen-based viewing—approximately 80% of screen-based decision time and more than twice as many correct responses (mutzenich2022situationawarenessin pages 111-113).

### Remote E-Stop Design

When the responsible human is 1,000 km away and the only people in the room are undergraduates who did not start the experiment, the e-stop system must satisfy two independent requirements: (a) a **physical e-stop button** accessible to anyone in the room, clearly labeled, requiring no knowledge of the experiment, connected to the hardwired safety chain, and (b) a **remote stop command** that is software-initiated but terminates in a hardwired safety relay or contactor, not merely an MQTT message. The remote stop must be tested end-to-end, and the system must enter a safe state automatically on loss of the remote supervision link (hardy2026engineeringsafetyrequirements pages 12-13, hardy2026engineeringsafetyrequirements pages 3-4). Maritime autonomous system requirements specify that communication links must be treated as safety-control channels, with link quality monitored and tied to operational-state thresholds (hardy2026engineeringsafetyrequirements pages 12-13).

## 4. The Control Plane

### 4.1 Critical Evaluation of EOS vs. Existing Infrastructure

The group's existing stack is **MQTT (HiveMQ) + MongoDB Atlas + Hugging Face Spaces + GitHub Actions**, with Tailscale networking and an Opentrons HTTP API. Q3 recommended EOS. The migration cost of adopting EOS or any monolithic SDL orchestrator would be substantial: it would require replacing or wrapping the existing MQTT device bus, re-implementing the Opentrons integration, migrating the MongoDB data model, and retraining students on a new framework—all for uncertain gain given the group's already-functioning device communication layer.

**The defensible architecture keeps MQTT as the device bus and adds an orchestrator above it.** This is precisely the pattern used by OCTOPUS, which implements a hierarchical client/interface-node/master-node/module-node architecture with TCP/IP-based process modularization, supporting concurrent multi-user jobs from heterogeneous devices (yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 5-7, yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 3-5, yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 2-3). OCTOPUS's user-optimal scheduler reduced example total job times from 10 to 5.5–6.5 hours compared to first-come-first-served serialization (yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 5-7).

**What MQTT alone lacks and what must be added above it:**
- **Multi-user queuing and fair scheduling:** MQTT is a pub/sub bus, not a scheduler. An orchestrator must maintain job queues, unique job IDs, resource locks, and a scheduling policy (yoo2024octopusoperationcontrol pages 2-3).
- **Authentication and authorization:** HiveMQ supports ACLs, but experiment-level authorization (who may run what protocol with what materials) requires an application-layer gate.
- **Crash recovery and idempotency:** MQTT QoS 2 provides at-most-once or exactly-once message delivery, but protocol-level crash recovery requires durable state: each command must have an idempotency key, and the orchestrator must be able to resume from a known good state after restart.
- **Observability:** MongoDB provides storage but not real-time dashboards. Add structured event streams with timestamps, state assertions, and health metrics.

### 4.2 Data Provenance and Persistent Identifiers

**RO-Crate** packages research objects—data, workflows, software, metadata, and provenance—into self-describing JSON-LD containers using Schema.org vocabulary (morales2025architectingfairdigital pages 3-4, hardisty2022thespecimendata pages 13-14, hardisty2022thespecimendata pages 7-9). **PROV-O** provides the W3C ontology for representing provenance: entities, activities, and agents with generation, usage, derivation, and attribution relationships (morales2025architectingfairdigital pages 3-4). Together, they can package an SDL campaign: the requested protocol, the authorized execution plan, the sensor-observed physical events, the resulting data, and the complete chain of custody.

The Q1 failure—conflating insert time with measurement time—is precisely the provenance failure that RO-Crate + PROV-O is designed to prevent. A prospective instrument trace should record each experimental event with its control source (human, script, optimizer, agent), distinguishing the requested action from the executed action from the observed outcome (kalinin2608beyondfairdata pages 15-18). The CRUTD protocol from Safe-SDL provides the operational model: the requested command, the pre-operation state snapshot, the simulation/validation result, the execution event, the post-execution sensor confirmation, and any abort/rollback events should all be recorded as distinct provenance entities with separate timestamps (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 4-8).

For DOI minting: package completed campaigns as Workflow-Run RO-Crates, deposit to Zenodo, and mint DOIs. This provides immutable, citable records of what physically happened.

## 5. Human Factors and the Undergraduate Reality

### 5.1 Training and Error Rates

The SDL literature is qualitatively consistent: human technicians remain essential for loading plates, opening packages, replacing consumables, replenishing reagents, removing waste, calibrating equipment, and resolving faults (king2608thepastand pages 58-60). Laboratory automation is generally fixed and highly structured, requiring carefully specified layouts; even small equipment or consumable changes can require substantial reprogramming (king2608thepastand pages 58-60). Virtual reality training has been shown to reduce operator errors, but may increase stress for complex tasks due to the greater information load (from the Brunzini et al. 2024 training methodology study).

### 5.2 Human Time Costs in SDLs

No published SDL located in this review provides quantitative utilization rates (percentage of wall-clock time spent running vs. idle) or calibrated human-time savings. The qualitative evidence is consistent: autonomous Bayesian optimization campaigns have reached optima in under 30 hours, and batched reaction optimization achieved 192 reactions in four days—approximately 4× the throughput of sequential human operation (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 3-5). However, these throughput gains are interleaved with human effort for electrode cleaning, vial loading, chemical preparation, purification troubleshooting, and consumable replacement (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9). Dispensing solids below approximately 20 mg and handling heterogeneous mixtures remain difficult to automate (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9, seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 1-2).

### 5.3 Design Choices That Improve Utilization

The most impactful design choices are: (a) **magazine/rack capacity** sized so that consumable exhaustion does not interrupt overnight runs; (b) **scheduled human touchpoints** (e.g., daily morning restocking) rather than on-demand interruptions; (c) **batching** to consolidate related experiments into continuous runs; (d) **finite consumable accounting** so the system knows when to stop before running out; and (e) **progressive autonomy** starting with the simplest, most constrained operations and expanding only after demonstrated reliability (zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 25-27, zhang2026safesdlestablishingsafetyboundaries pages 21-23).

**Flagged extrapolation:** The precise throughput fraction lost to restocking and low-utilization windows is not well-characterized in the published literature. This is an open measurement gap that this group could contribute to by instrumenting their own system.

### Key Constraints

**The first experiment that can honestly run without a person in the room** is an inert-powder (alumina) dispensing-and-weighing loop, with no furnace, no reactive metals, restricted room access, remote monitoring, hardwired safeguards, finite consumable budget, and automatic halt on anomaly. This is achievable within 90 days if the safety infrastructure is in place.

**Reactive metal powder handling unattended is not a defensible 90-day milestone.** It requires lot-specific explosibility testing, validated dust-handling controls, approved exhaust/collection design, demonstrated static-control measures, incident-free attended and remotely supervised operating hours, and institutional/fire-authority approval. Treating a fume hood, a printed enclosure, a camera feed, or a remote operator as explosion protection is not defensible.

### Minimum Documentation Package for EHS Review

The minimum defensible package includes: (1) scope and system description; (2) ISO 12100 risk assessment register; (3) STPA control structure analysis; (4) dust hazard analysis per NFPA 484/660; (5) SDS inventory and quantity limits; (6) cell layout with safety distances; (7) safety circuit schematics with component ratings; (8) ventilation basis of design; (9) fire-response plan with Class D agent selection; (10) SOPs for attended and unattended operation; (11) training plan and competency records; (12) commissioning and validation protocol; (13) remote-supervision concept of operations; and (14) management-of-change procedure.

### What Is Legally Required vs. Best Practice vs. Prudent

- **Legally required** (where applicable): Risk assessment documenting recognized hazards and feasible controls (OSHA General Duty Clause); machine guarding (29 CFR 1910.212); combustible-metal fire prevention per adopted fire code; emergency-stop accessible to room occupants; training for all personnel.
- **Best practice:** ISO 12100 risk assessment framework; STPA for the control structure; ISO 13849-1 PL derivation for safety functions; Safe-SDL CRUTD protocol; RO-Crate provenance; progressive autonomy with regression after incidents.
- **Prudent but optional:** VLM anomaly detection as supplementary channel; immersive (HMD-360) remote supervision interface; formal verification of safety-kernel software; digital-twin pre-validation of each experiment.

### What Is Unknown

Several questions remain genuinely open: (1) no published SDL has reported a formal safety-validation protocol or third-party certification for its robot safety functions; (2) no quantitative SDL utilization data exist in the reviewed literature; (3) the AgileX PiPER's stopping-time characterization, PFHD, and diagnostic coverage are not publicly documented; (4) AlSi10Mg alloy-specific dust explosion parameters are not available in the reviewed literature; and (5) the long-term reliability of VLM-based anomaly detection under distribution shift in production SDL environments is an open research question (wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 6-7, wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 7-8).
