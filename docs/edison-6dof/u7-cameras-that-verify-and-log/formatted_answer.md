**LIRA (Zhou et al. 2025; peer-reviewed).** The most directly relevant system for self-driving laboratories is LIRA, which uses a fine-tuned vision-language model on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin (64 GB) edge computing module. Across 200 manually introduced placement errors in two autonomous laboratory workflows (solid-state and synthesis), the fine-tuned VLM achieved 97.9% overall accuracy for determining whether placement was correct, 96.8% for classifying misalignment direction, and 98.9% for predicting whether an error was recoverable (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 5-7). Five failures occurred out of 200 tests (2 PXRD-plate and 3 NMR-rack errors), yielding a 97.5% error-inspection success rate (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8, cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 9-10). By comparison, a conventional YOLOv11 classifier reached only 86.3%, and a zero-shot LLaVA-OneVision model achieved 42.9% (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 5-7). The system used an Intel RealSense D435i camera on the robot end effector and fixed webcams on instruments (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1.

**SuccessVQA / Flamingo (Du et al. 2023; preprint).** Fine-tuned Flamingo-3B evaluated on Panda gear-manipulation tasks achieved in-domain balanced accuracies of 82.1–96.0% for insertion and removal tasks, with approximately 50,000–60,000 episodes evaluated per task and 2,076 held-out episodes (du2023visionlanguagemodelsas pages 9-12). Critically, changing the viewpoint from the front basket camera (used for training) to the rear camera reduced balanced accuracy by up to 7.5 percentage points (from 96.0% to 89.7% for large-gear insertion; from 87.2% to 79.4% for large-gear removal) (du2023visionlanguagemodelsas pages 12-14). Adding distractor objects caused minimal degradation. This demonstrates that viewpoint is a significant factor even for fine-tuned models. arXiv: 2303.07280.

**Guardian (Pacaud et al. 2025; preprint).** Guardian-8B correctly recognized 91% of successful executions and 76% of failed executions, with a 9% false-positive rate on a UR5/RG6 multi-camera setup (pacaud2025guardiandetectingrobotic pages 9-11). On benchmark datasets, it achieved execution accuracy of 0.86 on RoboFail, 0.77 on UR5-Fail, and 0.85 on RoboVQA (pacaud2025guardiandetectingrobotic pages 5-6). arXiv: 2512.01946.

**Robometer (Liang et al. 2026; preprint).** On 100 Franka Panda DROID trajectories (30 successes, 70 failures, seven tasks), Robometer achieved a mean F1 of 0.81, outperforming RoboReward-4B (0.74), GPT-5-mini (0.33), and VLAC (0.16) (liang2026robometerscalinggeneralpurpose pages 9-10). arXiv: 2603.02115.

**Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 (GR Team 2025; preprint).** The system was evaluated for binary video success detection across real-time (inference at 5 Hz) and offline settings, with both multiview and singleview inputs (team2025geminirobotics1.5 pages 14-15). The text emphasizes that stale predictions due to inference latency can render real-time judgments unreliable during dynamic interactions. The specific 82.4% figure mentioned in the user's context could not be confirmed from the retrieved text; Figure 13 of the paper plots values approximately ranging from the high-0.4s to about 0.8 depending on setting and model (team2025geminirobotics1.5 pages 14-15). arXiv: 2510.03342.

**FailBench (Navasardyan et al. 2026; preprint).** This cross-source benchmark evaluated 13 VLM-based detectors across 2,197 manipulation attempts from 14 independent sources (1,176 failures, 1,021 successes; 73% real-world). The strongest detector achieved 0.77 mean balanced accuracy. Critically, "no model exceeds 0.60 balanced accuracy on contact-rich assembly" (navasardyan2026failbenchhowreliable pages 2-4, navasardyan2026failbenchhowreliable pages 1-2). Purpose-built failure detectors generally underperformed general-purpose VLMs (navasardyan2026failbenchhowreliable pages 9-11). arXiv: 2609.03611.

**SAFE (Gu et al. 2025; preprint).** SAFE extracts VLA internal features and predicts failure likelihood. On real-world data, SAFE-MLP achieved ROC-AUC of 86.76–89.11 on seen tasks and 88.42 on unseen WidowX tasks (gu2026safemultitaskfailure pages 9-11). It uses functional conformal prediction to calibrate time-varying detection thresholds, with significance level α ≈ 0.15 providing a tradeoff between balanced accuracy and detection latency (gu2026safemultitaskfailure pages 19-21). arXiv: 2506.09937.

**Edge deployment.** Among the systems reviewed, only LIRA demonstrated deployment on edge hardware suitable for a small cloud lab (Jetson AGX Orin) (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9, cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 10-13). No system reported deployment on a Raspberry Pi 5 for success/failure detection. The user's context mentions TOPReward; this system (arXiv: 2602.19313) uses token probabilities from VLMs as zero-shot rewards for robotics, evaluated on LeRobot datasets, but the retrieved text did not contain precision/recall metrics directly comparable to the other detectors.

### 2. Automatic Detection of Human Interventions and Presence

**Laboratory-specific systems.** Chemist Eye (Munguia-Galeano et al. 2026; peer-reviewed) achieved 97% accuracy for detecting potential safety hazards and 95% decision-making success in a self-driving laboratory with three mobile robots (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 1-2). For PPE compliance (lab coat detection), accuracy ranged from 67.5% to 97.5% depending on the prompt format, with LLaVA-Phi3 achieving the highest rates (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 5-6). Dangerous posture detection (lying, sitting, crawling) reached 97% accuracy (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 6-7). However, the authors explicitly stated the system was "not yet trustworthy for autonomous safety-related decisions" and retained human judgment as necessary (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 7-8). DOI: 10.1039/d6dd00062b.

PREVENT (Veeramani et al. 2025; preprint) used a fine-tuned ResNet-18 for visual hazard classification, achieving deployment accuracies of 92.0–96.7% across navigation, vial pickup, and vial placement tasks. A fine-tuned ViT-L/14 for hazard-type classification achieved 90.2–98.0% (veeramani2025preventproactiverisk pages 14-17). arXiv: 2510.21438.

**Edge hardware for hand/pose detection.** Ivačko et al. (2026; peer-reviewed) benchmarked a complete hand-tracking pipeline (MediaPipe Hands + Kalman smoothing + YOLO + LSTM) on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano and a Raspberry Pi 5 with Hailo AI accelerator. End-to-end latency (frame acquisition to robot command) was approximately 160 ms on the Jetson and 280 ms on the Raspberry Pi (ivacko2026edgecomputingapproach pages 22-24), corresponding to approximately 6 FPS and 3.5 FPS respectively. DOI: 10.3390/computers15040241.

**Camera-based safety under ISO standards.** Under ISO 10218, when the distance between a robot and a person falls below a defined safety level, the robot must slow down and stop (ozkahraman2021designandvalidation pages 1-2). Camera-based safety systems have achieved response times of 0.08–0.1 seconds and can operate at a minimum illuminance of 120 lux (versus 400 lux for commercial systems), with at least 1080p resolution recommended (ozkahraman2021designandvalidation pages 15-18). DOI: 10.3390/app112411679.

However, **no published camera-based safety system using deep learning has demonstrated certification to ISO 13849 Performance Level d or e, or compliance with IEC 61496 as electro-sensitive protective equipment.** The retrieved literature discusses these standards in context but does not present a system that has achieved formal safety certification for camera-based human detection (ozkahraman2021designandvalidation pages 13-15, ozkahraman2021designandvalidation pages 18-20). Camera-based intrusion detection for robot safety remains a research topic rather than a certified solution. For safety-critical functions, commercial systems (e.g., Pilz SafetyEye using three cameras with a certified safety controller) provide certified solutions, but research literature on their internal detection accuracy and false-alarm rates is sparse (lettera2024amultimodalworkspace pages 54-59). ISO 10218:2025 (the latest revision) may address camera-based monitoring more explicitly, but the retrieved sources primarily reference the 2011 edition.

**Implication for the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab.** Camera-based hand detection can serve for logging human interventions (a non-safety-critical function) with sub-300 ms latency on a Raspberry Pi 5 with accelerator. For safety-rated stopping, a separate certified system would be required under current standards.

### 3. Camera Verification of Placement and Insertion for Labware

LIRA provides the most detailed published evidence. The fine-tuned VLM classified placement correctness at 97.9%, misalignment direction at 96.8%, and recoverability at 98.9% across 200 tests involving Chemspeed vial racks, PXRD plates, NMR racks, and LCMS racks (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 5-7). In a separate evaluation, 600 vial pickup-and-insertion manipulations achieved 100% success rate (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 6-9). The system used a wrist-mounted RealSense D435i camera on a KUKA KMR iiwa for post-placement inspection (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). Failures were attributed to "visually ambiguous conditions, such as similar rack/holder colors or cluttered backgrounds" (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 9-10). DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1.

**Smallest detectable offsets.** No published work was found that specifically quantifies the smallest positional offset (in mm) detectable by a vision system for labware placement verification. LIRA's misalignment classification distinguishes translational offsets (left, right, forward, backward) from rotational errors, treating translational errors as recoverable and rotational errors as requiring human intervention (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 6-9), but it does not report minimum detectable displacement thresholds. **This remains a gap in the published literature** relevant to the user's vial-seating and stub-insertion verification needs.

**Fontenot et al. 2026** was not found in the literature search. No publication matching this author and date was retrievable. If it exists, it may be too recent to have been indexed.

### 4. Calibration Maintenance and Drift Detection

**AnchorHEC (Hwang et al. 2026; preprint)** is a targetless hand-eye calibration framework for jointly calibrating wrist and fixed cameras using RGB images, forward kinematics, and known camera intrinsics, without calibration targets. In simulation, AnchorHEC reduced translation calibration error by 86.4% and rotation error by 71.6% compared to reconstruction-based baselines (hwang2026anchorhectargetlesshandeye pages 1-2). On a physical robot, wrist-camera calibration achieved 1.22–1.35 cm translation error and 1.00–1.48° rotation error at 15 wrist poses (hwang2026anchorhectargetlesshandeye pages 5-6, hwang2026anchorhectargetlesshandeye pages 7-8). Fixed-camera translation errors averaged approximately 1.29 cm for accepted registrations (hwang2026anchorhectargetlesshandeye pages 7-8). The method supports recalibration from a single scene acquisition without dedicated targets. arXiv: 2611.02884.

**Self-calibration using contact.** Limoyo et al. (2018; peer-reviewed) demonstrated targetless self-calibration for mobile manipulators using point-cloud registration, achieving centimeter-level accuracy for end-effector positioning under visual guidance. DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.1803.06406.

**Drift detection.** No published work was found that specifically addresses automated detection of extrinsic calibration drift during long campaigns. The standard approach in robotics is periodic re-verification against known fiducial markers—which aligns with the user's current AprilTag infrastructure. A practical self-check would compare the detected tag poses against their known fixture positions at regular intervals; if reprojection error exceeds a threshold, recalibration is triggered. **No published study reports how often recalibration is needed for low-cost cameras like the IMX477**, nor whether the IMX477 exhibits greater thermal or mechanical drift than industrial cameras. This is an open gap.

### 5. Measuring and Bounding False-Clear / False-Success Rates for Vision Gates

**Conformal prediction** provides the most principled framework for bounding false-negative rates (missed failures) of vision-based decision gates. Luo et al. (2023; peer-reviewed) demonstrated that conformal prediction can tune warning systems to provably achieve a false-negative rate ≤ ε using as few as 1/ε unsafe calibration examples—approximately 20 examples for a 5% FNR target (luo2024sampleefficientsafetyassurances pages 8-9, luo2024sampleefficientsafetyassurances pages 7-8, luo2024sampleefficientsafetyassurances pages 1-2). The key tradeoff is that demanding very low FNR imposes a lower bound on false-positive rate: FPR ≥ 1 − (1+T)·ε, where T is the training-set size (luo2024sampleefficientsafetyassurances pages 8-9). DOI: 10.1177/02783649231221580.

**PAC-Bayes bounds.** Farid et al. (2022; preprint) achieved misclassification bounds of 0.128 for standard navigation and 0.154 for occluded-obstacle settings using PAC-Bayes analysis of a vision-based failure predictor. In 15 physical trials per setting, no false negatives (collisions) occurred when the predictor was used (farid2202failurepredictionwith pages 7-8). arXiv: 2202.05894.

**SAFE's functional conformal prediction.** SAFE uses a time-varying conformal prediction band calibrated on successful rollouts, with significance level α (typically 0.15) controlling the accuracy-latency tradeoff. A failure is flagged when the score exceeds the threshold (gu2026safemultitaskfailure pages 4-6, gu2026safemultitaskfailure pages 19-21). This framework is directly applicable to gating decisions like "is the plate clear?"

**Practical implication for the plate-clear check.** No published study reports the false-clear rate of a specific vision gate for 3D-printer plate clearing. To measure this, one would: (a) collect labeled examples of clear and not-clear plates, (b) run the classifier on a held-out set, (c) apply conformal prediction to bound the false-clear (false-negative) rate at a user-specified level (e.g., ε = 0.05), requiring approximately 20 deliberately not-clear images. The conformal guarantee holds marginally under exchangeability; distribution shifts (lighting changes, camera drift) can violate this assumption.

### 6. Privacy, Governance, and Anonymization Effects

**Effect of face blurring on downstream detection.** Ren et al. (2018; peer-reviewed) found that Gaussian face blurring reduced spatial action detection mAP on the DALY dataset from 56.85% to 42.39–51.97% depending on blur severity (a 5–14.5 point drop), while masking caused the largest degradation to 38.86% (ren2018learningtoanonymize pages 9-11). Their adversarially learned anonymizer maintained mAP at 52.32%, outperforming all hand-crafted methods. arXiv: 1803.11556.

Lee & You (2024; peer-reviewed) found that face blurring reduced YOLOv8x bbox mAP by 2.6 points and RT-DETR-L by 7.5 points on COCO, but person-class detection was relatively robust due to the strong representation of persons in training data (lee2024balancingprivacyand pages 8-10). DOI: 10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3352146.

Abdulaziz & Bondarev (2026; peer-reviewed) showed that for video anomaly detection, blurring caused only minor AUC degradation (0.2–0.5 points for robust models like UR-DMU and PEL4VAD), while masking could paradoxically improve some models but at the cost of extreme false-alarm rates (99.65% for BN-WVAD) (abdulaziz2026unmaskingperformancegaps pages 6-9, abdulaziz2026unmaskingperformancegaps pages 9-13). DOI: 10.1007/978-3-032-07343-3_14.

**Practical guidance for the BYU lab.** At 720p and 5 FPS with on-device face blurring, the user's setup should expect modest degradation (2–8 points mAP) for person and object detection. For hand-entry detection, hands are typically not blurred, so hand-detection accuracy should be unaffected by face anonymization. Activity recognition for face-centric actions (e.g., drinking) would be more impacted, but laboratory manipulations are hand-centric and thus less sensitive.

**Governance.** No published standard specific to laboratory video governance was found. General guidance from GDPR and institutional review board frameworks applies: face de-identification should occur at the point of capture (as the user is already doing), retention policies should be defined and enforced, and access controls should limit who can view raw (un-blurred) footage. The key unresolved question is whether blurred video constitutes personal data under applicable regulations if re-identification is technically possible through de-blurring attacks, as noted in the privacy literature (abdulaziz2026unmaskingperformancegaps pages 1-3).
