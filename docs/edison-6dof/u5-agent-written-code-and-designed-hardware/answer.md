# Agent-Written Robot Code, Safeguards, AI-Designed Hardware, Provenance, and Evaluation for Laboratory Automation: A Literature Synthesis (2023–2026)

## 1. Agent-Written Robot Code on Physical Laboratory Hardware

The period 2023–2026 has seen a rapid expansion of systems executing language-model-generated code or plans on real laboratory robots, though quantitative reporting of success rates, failure modes, latency, cost, and human intervention remains inconsistent across the literature. **Coscientist** (Boiko et al., 2023) demonstrated autonomous design and execution of Suzuki–Miyaura and Sonogashira cross-coupling reactions on an Opentrons OT-2 liquid handler. The system "autonomously designed, plans and performs complex experiments" and successfully verified target products by GC–MS analysis (boiko2023autonomouschemicalresearch pages 5-6, boiko2023autonomouschemicalresearch pages 1-2). It initially hallucinated an incorrect heater–shaker API method but self-corrected after consulting documentation (boiko2023autonomouschemicalresearch pages 5-6). Safety checking blocked hazardous suggestions including sodium azide as an additive and 220°C in ethanol (ezeh2026acomprehensivereview pages 33-37). No aggregate success rate or trial count was reported; plates were moved manually (boiko2023autonomouschemicalresearch pages 5-6).

**CLAIRify** (Yoshikawa et al., 2023) translated natural-language chemistry instructions into structured plans executed via constrained task-and-motion planning on a Franka robot. Constrained TAMP achieved 97% success with an 8-DoF robot versus 84% for 7-DoF; pouring relative errors were 8.1% for water and 8.8% for salt (yoshikawa2023largelanguagemodels pages 2-4). Failure modes included motion-planning failures, spillage, incorrect plan syntax, and perception errors (yoshikawa2023largelanguagemodels pages 2-4).

**MATTERIX** (Darvish et al., 2025) provided a GPU-accelerated digital-twin framework for chemistry laboratories. Physical pick-and-place achieved 75% success (9/12 trials) with failures from pose estimation and calibration errors; liquid pouring achieved 90% (9/10) (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14). The system validates workflows in a full simulated laboratory before physical execution (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 2-5, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 9-11).

**LabscriptAI** (Gao et al., 2026; *bioRxiv preprint*) achieved a 96.7% simulation-pass rate on a 90-task liquid-handling benchmark—"the highest among the evaluated LLM-based and commercial baselines" (gao2025autonomousliquidhandlingrobotics pages 4-7). It supported four liquid-handling platforms (Opentrons OT-2, OT-Flex, Hamilton, Tecan Fluent) and demonstrated cross-platform reproducibility with CV below 5% and R² above 0.999 (gao2025autonomousliquidhandlingrobotics pages 11-15).

**WetRobo** (Oikawa et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) provided a reproducible robot kit for coding agents using PiPER arms. Cap removal was successfully adapted across two laboratories despite different grippers and camera placements; the initial adaptation took 2 h 40 min but repeat execution took only 3 min 27 s, illustrating the efficiency of code reuse (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 7-9, oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 1-2). An alternative VLA approach failed to transfer between sites (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 1-2).

**ACE-CaP-0** (2026; *arXiv preprint*) achieved 92.1% overall success (221/240 trials) across eight real-world manipulation tasks using frozen agent-developed programs, with no human or coding agent during evaluation. Mean physical execution time ranged from 0.97 to 2.27 minutes per task (team2026acecap0realworldcodeaspolicies pages 9-12).

**AMP1** (Bagherieh et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) achieved 82.8–85.6% on RoboLab-120 and 95.2% on LIBERO-PRO in simulation. Selected real-world tasks achieved 9/9 success with total times of 94–208 seconds (bagherieh2026onagenticmanipulation pages 4-5, bagherieh2026onagenticmanipulation pages 3-4). Failure modes included grasp failures from object geometry/mass and "flaky verifiers rejecting valid end states" (bagherieh2026onagenticmanipulation pages 3-4).

**SPINE** (Ham et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) achieved 100% fault resolution across five PiPER scenarios (versus 99% for human operators using Claude Code) and reduced mean time-to-teleoperation by 38% (12 min 30 s to 7 min 48 s) (ham2026spinebridgingthe pages 2-3, ham2026spinebridgingthe pages 6-7). The strongest result was an 80% reduction in recovery time for disconnected camera and CAN links (ham2026spinebridgingthe pages 6-7).

**Common failure modes** across systems include: hallucinated or deprecated API calls (inagaki2023llmscangenerate pages 1-3, boiko2023autonomouschemicalresearch pages 5-6); perception and pose-estimation errors (yoshikawa2023largelanguagemodels pages 2-4, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14); grasp failures from object geometry (fu2026capxaframework pages 56-58, bagherieh2026onagenticmanipulation pages 3-4); wrong coordinate frames or units (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14); prompt brittleness causing substantial performance variation (johnson2025multiagentsystemsfor pages 12-15); LLM "sleepwalking"—deviating from instructions without notification (johnson2025multiagentsystemsfor pages 12-15); and silent failures where VLM verifiers accepted failed states (li2026whentheworld pages 36-37).

**Notable gap.** Per-task latency and monetary cost are almost universally unreported, as are human-intervention rates. Only ACE-CaP-0 and AMP1 report execution times systematically.

### Key Findings

**Static and formal checks** represent the most rigorous pre-execution layer. NeSyRo improved real-robot success from 10.6% (Code as Policies) to 57.5% and reduced irreversible actions from 53 to 7 by validating generated code with an SMT solver and checking skill preconditions with a PDDL planner (li2026whentheworld pages 16-18). VASO achieved 96.8% specification compliance by model-checking plans against temporal-logic safety specifications (li2026whentheworld pages 44-46).

**Simulation dry runs** are the most widely used safeguard. MATTERIX validates entire chemistry workflows—including robotic manipulation, chemical kinetics, and heat transfer—in a digital twin before physical execution (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 2-5). LabscriptAI uses platform-specific simulators with iterative targeted patching rather than full regeneration (gao2025autonomousliquidhandlingrobotics pages 4-7). ReSkill demonstrated that simulation screening catches many errors but leaves a residual gap: 72/80 simulated successes versus 62/80 real successes (li2026whentheworld pages 36-37).

**Runtime safety filters** can dramatically reduce unsafe behavior. RoboGuard reduced unsafe execution under worst-case jailbreaks from over 92% to below 3% (li2026whentheworld pages 36-37). LEASH used separate task, action, and gripper/pouring gates to reduce harm from 11.23% to 0.00% for one model on 92 RoboRisk tasks (li2026whentheworld pages 39-40). However, VLM-based runtime verifiers can be unreliable: one fixed VLM "accepted 50.0% of remaining hover failures" and a learned verifier "was wrong on 91% of 699 later rollouts" (li2026whentheworld pages 36-37).

**Vision-language safety monitors** show promise but are not yet reliable for autonomous decisions. Chemist Eye achieved 97% aggregate hazard spotting and 95% decision-making performance in a self-driving chemistry laboratory (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 1-2), with PPE detection accuracy ranging from 67.5% to 97.5% depending on query format (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 5-6). LabSafety Bench found that no evaluated model—across 19 LLMs and VLMs—exceeded 70% accuracy on realistic hazard identification, "highlighting significant safety challenges" (zhou2026benchmarkinglargelanguage pages 9-12, zhou2026benchmarkinglargelanguage pages 1-2).

**Human approval gates** remain essential. La Agente Óptima implements authenticated API keys, operator certification, and an append-only SHA-256 hash-chained audit ledger (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 54-55). SPINE requires human operators for physical actions and validates recovery through end-to-end teleoperation checks (ham2026spinebridgingthe pages 2-3). The emerging consensus is that "human judgment remains necessary" for safety-critical decisions (munguiagaleano2026chemisteyea pages 7-8).

**Frozen versus regenerated code.** No controlled randomized comparison exists, but indirect evidence favors freezing. ACE-CaP-0 achieved 92.1% with frozen programs and no agent during evaluation (team2026acecap0realworldcodeaspolicies pages 9-12). WetRobo showed that reusing generated code reduced execution from 2 h 40 min to 3 min 27 s (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 7-9). RHO deploys single-turn frozen programs at test time (li2026whentheworld pages 16-18). However, online revision can be "much more successful than one-shot execution in some settings, so freezing is not universally more reliable" (li2026whentheworld pages 16-18). The practical recommendation is to freeze code once validated and implement regression testing when the library changes (li2026whentheworld pages 44-46, li2026whentheworld pages 57-59).

**Missing evidence.** Catch rates and false-alarm rates for most individual safeguard layers are unreported. No study systematically measures the precision and recall of human approval gates (cheng2026towardshumanledagentdriven pages 8-11, li2026whentheworld pages 57-59).

## 3. Agent- or AI-Designed Physical Hardware for Laboratory Robots

**This is the area with the largest evidence gap.** No published study was found in which a language model or generative-AI system designed a laboratory fixture, holder, gripper, adapter, or docking feature that was then 3D-printed and tested on real laboratory robot hardware, with reported first-pass fit rates, iterations to success, or human edits needed.

The closest evidence comes from **text-to-CAD generation** systems evaluated computationally:

- **CADSmith** (Barkley et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) used five agents and two nested correction loops (inner for code errors, outer for geometric validation using OpenCASCADE measurements and VLM judgment). It achieved 100% execution rate on a 100-prompt benchmark and improved median IoU from 0.8085 to 0.9629 and reduced mean Chamfer Distance from 28.37 to 0.74 (barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 1-2, barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 6-7). Failure modes included "structurally incorrect yet geometrically valid solids" and subtle gaps missed by fixed viewing angles (barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 7-8). No physical fabrication or testing was reported (barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 1-2).

- **IterCAD** (Hu et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) achieved 0.64% invalid ratio (~99.36% code validity) and low median Chamfer distance of 0.10 on Text2CAD benchmarks (hu2026itercadaniterative pages 8-10). Its iterative multimodal correction averaged 2.34 turns per design. No physical validation was reported.

- **AgentsCAD** (George et al., 2026; *arXiv preprint*) specifically targeted design-for-manufacturing of FDM parts using multi-agent LLM reasoning, but was evaluated computationally.

**3D-printed laboratory components** are widely documented in the self-driving-lab literature. Doloi et al. (2025) reviewed advances in low-cost 3D printing for laboratory automation, including "automatic liquid handlers, robotic arms, automated" components. However, these designs were human-engineered rather than AI-generated.

**Implication for the BYU proposal.** The proposal to have an agent design connecting hardware (sample holders, docking plates, camera mounts, grippers) would represent genuinely novel work. The closest precedent is CADSmith's computational CAD generation pipeline, which could be extended to lab-fixture design with physical validation. The expected workflow would involve: LLM generates CadQuery/OpenSCAD code → geometric validation loop → 3D printing → physical fit testing → iteration. Based on CADSmith's computational results, one might expect high code-execution rates but significant iteration for dimensional accuracy and functional fit, requiring human measurement feedback.

## 4. Provenance and Auditability for Agent-Operated Laboratories

### Demonstrated Approaches

**Git-based provenance.** WetRobo packages the robot's base stack, skill instructions (AGENTS.md), and agent-generated programs in a Git repository, with trial snapshots retained as branches. This creates a "record linking the starting software, agent-generated modifications, and individual attempts" (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 1-2). However, it does not include signed commits, immutable logs, or formal linkage from commits to measured physical outcomes (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 1-2).

**Hash-chained audit ledger.** La Agente Óptima implements the most rigorous provenance: "all robot state transitions pass through one funnel that atomically persists state and appends a line-delimited JSON entry to an append-only, hash-chained audit ledger. Each entry records the actor, action, structured payload, UTC timestamp, predecessor hash, and SHA-256 hashes of the complete state before and after the transition" (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 54-55). This enables reconstruction of "the requesting person, technician certification of the physical deck, autonomous actions between request and measurement, and whether the record was altered" (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 54-55).

**Community database deposition.** LabscriptAI deposits experimental data with metadata to FPBase (excitation/emission spectra, quantum yields) and EnzymeML (raw kinetics data), providing "traceability and reproducibility at the protocol/data level" (gao2025autonomousliquidhandlingrobotics pages 15-18).

**Digital-twin synchronization.** MATTERIX synchronizes the simulated and real robots via FoundationPose-based pose estimation, enabling comparison of predicted and actual outcomes (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 9-11). This provides a form of "what was planned versus what happened" comparison, though quantitative discrepancy metrics were not systematically reported.

### Cross-Site Reproducibility

WetRobo demonstrated cross-site reproducibility for cap removal across two laboratories with different grippers and camera placements; the coding-agent approach succeeded while a VLA baseline failed to transfer (oikawa2026wetroboareproducible pages 1-2). LabscriptAI demonstrated cross-platform protocol translation with calibration CV below 5% across four liquid-handling platforms (gao2025autonomousliquidhandlingrobotics pages 11-15). These represent the strongest published demonstrations, though both involve limited task sets.

### Standards

**No retrieved publication specifically discusses ISO 10218:2025 or its cybersecurity clauses for networked robots in the context of agent-operated laboratories.** The 2025 revision of ISO 10218 (Parts 1 and 2) added requirements for cybersecurity of networked industrial robot systems, but the agent-lab literature has not yet engaged with these requirements. Cheng et al. (2026) call generally for "cybersecurity and AI-security practices" but name no specific standard (cheng2026towardshumanledagentdriven pages 11-13). The proposed design principles—authenticated identity, state provenance, human certification, and tamper-evident audit trails (muller2026laagente\optimatowards pages 54-55)—align with the spirit of ISO 10218:2025 but have not been evaluated against its specific clauses.

## 5. Defensible Evaluation Design

Based on the surveyed literature, a defensible evaluation of "an agent designs the fixture and writes the workflow, and the arm runs it" should include the following elements:

### Outcome Metrics

Evaluations should report binary task success, quantitative partial progress through task-defined subgoals, safety violations, execution cost (model calls, tokens, GPU time, robot time), wall-clock time, and uncertainty intervals over repeated trials (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52). Subgoals should be "implementation-independent, count repeated achievements only once, and specify whether an achieved state must remain satisfied" (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52). For the fixture-design component, relevant metrics include code-execution success, geometric accuracy (IoU, Chamfer distance), first-pass physical fit rate, iterations to successful function, and human edits required (barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 1-2, barkley2603cadsmithmultiagentcad pages 6-7).

### Baselines

Comparisons should include at minimum three conditions: (1) a human expert designing the fixture and writing the workflow from scratch; (2) a scripted or deterministic workflow baseline using pre-existing fixtures; and (3) the agent system (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52, li2026whentheworld pages 49-51, wang2025thedawnof pages 21-23). CaP-X explicitly compared CaP-Agent0 against human-written programs and found the agent matched or exceeded humans on 4 of 7 tasks (fu2026capxaframework pages 7-8). VABAR uses "human-specified workflow or graph baselines" alongside agent results (li2026whentheworld pages 49-51).

### Sample Sizes

Current practice varies widely: ACE-CaP-0 used 30 trials per task (240 total) (team2026acecap0realworldcodeaspolicies pages 9-12); CaP-Bench used 25 hardware trials per task (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52); VoxPoser used 5 real tasks with 10 trials each; BUMBLE used 70 trials across three buildings (wang2026asurveyof pages 11-12). The literature recommends that "sample sizes should be large enough to support uncertainty estimates rather than relying on single runs" and should use Wilson intervals or Bayesian methods (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52, li2026whentheworld pages 54-55). For the proposed evaluation, **≥25 trials per task per condition** would align with current best practice, with reporting of confidence intervals.

### Expected Failure Rates

The literature suggests the following baseline expectations:
- **Best manipulation systems (frozen code):** 83–100% per-task success, 92% aggregate (ACE-CaP-0) (team2026acecap0realworldcodeaspolicies pages 9-12)
- **Code-as-policy with perturbations:** 12–26% under position/instruction perturbations (CaP-Agent0 on LIBERO-PRO) (fu2026capxaframework pages 56-58)
- **Chemistry laboratory manipulation:** 75–90% for pick-and-place and pouring (MATTERIX) (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14)
- **Sim-to-real gap:** simulation screening catches many failures but typically 10–15% of simulated successes fail on real hardware (li2026whentheworld pages 36-37)
- **Safety violations:** policies exceeding 90% task success were unsafe in 18–28% of episodes; 38–56% of successful rollouts in one benchmark violated a safety clause (li2026whentheworld pages 37-39)

### Evaluation Architecture

A defensible benchmark should: (1) use sealed or independently verifiable judges, separating diagnostic feedback from final evaluation (li2026whentheworld pages 51-52); (2) deliberately inject execution-layer and sample-identity faults, scoring detection, localization, safe abort, recovery, and provenance preservation (cheng2026towardshumanledagentdriven pages 11-13); (3) audit evaluator errors against references and report false acceptances and rejections separately (li2026whentheworld pages 55-57); (4) freeze or explicitly measure any learning during testing (li2026whentheworld pages 54-55); (5) test both simulation and physical embodiments with paired trials (li2026whentheworld pages 49-51); and (6) disclose all human involvement including setup, resets, and reward code (wang2026asurveyof pages 11-12).

### For the Specific BYU Proposal

An evaluation of "agent designs fixture + writes workflow + arm runs it" is unprecedented in the literature. The closest analogs combine CADSmith-style computational CAD validation with ACE-CaP-0-style physical execution testing. A defensible protocol would:

1. **Fixture design:** Agent generates CadQuery code for each connecting part → geometric validation (IoU, Chamfer) → 3D print → measure dimensional accuracy → test fit on robot → score first-pass success, iterations, and human edits.
2. **Workflow writing:** Agent generates motion/manipulation code → simulation dry run → physical execution with ≥25 trials → score task success, safety violations, and latency.
3. **End-to-end:** Score the fraction of parts that fit, workflows that execute, and complete alloy-development loops that run without human handling, compared against human-designed fixtures and human-written workflows under matched conditions.
4. **Provenance:** Log every agent session, generated artifact, commit, and physical outcome in a hash-chained audit trail, then verify that the full chain can be reconstructed.

The literature suggests one should expect 75–92% physical success rates for well-developed manipulation tasks, significant iteration (2–5 cycles) for first-pass fixture fit, and that human intervention will be necessary for physical setup, safety checks, and debugging hardware faults that agents cannot diagnose remotely (ham2026spinebridgingthe pages 2-3, darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14).

## Summary of Evidence Gaps

- **Per-task monetary cost** is essentially unreported across all systems.
- **Human-intervention frequency** is rarely quantified.
- **False-alarm rates** for individual safeguard layers are almost never measured.
- **AI-designed physical lab hardware** fabricated and tested on real robots: **no published evidence exists.**
- **ISO 10218:2025 cybersecurity clauses** have not been analyzed in the agent-lab literature.
- **Controlled comparison of frozen versus regenerated code** has not been published.
- **End-to-end agent-designed-fixture-plus-workflow evaluation** has no published precedent.
