# Verification Testing and Documentation for a Low-Cost Robot Arm in a University Cloud Lab

## 1. Emergency-Stop and Protective-Stop Test Procedures and Acceptance Criteria

### 1.1 Stop Categories per IEC 60204-1

IEC 60204-1 defines three stop categories that govern how robot motion ceases. Stop Category 0 is an uncontrolled stop: "power is removed from the actuators" immediately, prioritizing the fastest cessation without controlling the deceleration trajectory (pqac-00000001). Stop Category 1 is a controlled stop: "actuator power is maintained while the robot decelerates to a complete stop, after which power is removed and brakes are applied" (pqac-00000001). Stop Category 2 is a controlled stop where "actuator power remains available and the brakes are not applied after stopping" (pqac-00000001). The following table summarizes these categories and their implications for the PiPER arm:

### 1.2 ISO 13850, ISO 10218, and ISO 13849-2 Requirements

ISO 13850 permits either Category 0 or Category 1 for emergency stops, selected on the basis of risk assessment. ISO 10218-1:2011 requires that in collaborative mode 1 (safety-rated monitored stop), any unintended motion or failure of braking deceleration must produce a Category 0 stop; in modes 2–4, violations of speed or position limits must produce a protective stop of at least Category 0 or Category 1 (pqac-00000000). The emergency-stop function should meet at least Performance Level d (PLd) with Category 3 architecture under ISO 13849-1 (pqac-00000000). ISO 13849-2 provides validation procedures including fault exclusion analysis for different component technologies; the VTT guidelines note that "fault exclusions presented in ISO 13849-2 for different" technology types should be evaluated during cell verification (pqac-00000025).

### 1.3 Measuring Stopping Time and Distance

ISO 10218-1 Annex B provides provisions for evaluating stopping time (T_S) and braking distance (B), which "should be obtained through traceable measurements tied to the robot system under test" (pqac-00000049). These are not fixed constants: they vary with the robot's initial velocity, payload, pose, brake wear, and configuration (pqac-00000048). Marvel and Norcross describe a practical approach: trigger the safety system with a controlled event (e.g., a laser beam interrupted by the robot), run the robot along a repeatable trajectory, record its stop position, and repeat at multiple speeds (pqac-00000048). External tracking systems can independently verify the robot's reported velocity; however, position noise of only a few millimeters can significantly distort velocity estimates (pqac-00000050). For industrial robots, earlier systems at ~3 m/s had emergency-stop distances of approximately 40 cm and servo-stop distances of ~90 cm; a current ABB IRB 4600 at 2500 mm/s reportedly has a 65 cm servo-stop distance (pqac-00000054).

### 1.4 Number of Repetitions

The CEN/CENELEC COVR guidelines for safety testing procedures in human-robot collaboration specify that "each test case is to be run at least three times" (pqac-00000002). The COVR protocol specifies using "an accurate distance-measuring device, such as a laser meter with an error below 5%" or alternatively "another distance sensor or a motion-tracking system" (pqac-00000002). Zimmermann et al. used 5 repetitions per pendulum test setting and 30 repetitions for one representative condition to establish repeatability, finding that maximum-force standard deviation was below 1% at 30 trials (pqac-00000043).

### 1.5 Published Examples on Cobots

Jocelyn et al. assessed three collaborative robots and found that robots 1 and 2 generated Category 0 protective stops for modes 2–4, while robot 3 could generate Category 0 or 1 depending on configuration (pqac-00000000). Svarny et al. tested emergency stop on a UR10e (Stop 0) and KUKA iiwa (Stop 1) in the context of collision testing with protective soft skins. Mariano et al. tested UR5e and UR10e end-effectors in a self-driving laboratory, performing "repeated measurements...under the most restrictive manufacturer settings" to verify safety function response (pqac-00000039). **No published stop-test data exist for the AgileX PiPER or similar sub-$3k hobby-class arms.**

## 2. Arms Without Brakes: Power-Loss Behavior and Mitigations

### 2.1 The Fail-Passive Problem

For back-drivable arms without mechanical brakes, de-energizing does not automatically produce a safe state. Ding et al. formalize this as the "fail-passive gap": for a system whose safe condition requires active control (or at minimum gravitational stability), "classical Stop Category 0 power removal is not automatically safe" because gravity can back-drive the joints (pqac-00000007). Back-drivable actuators permit "forces applied at the arm output to move the actuator input," and the degree of back-drivability depends on gear ratio, friction, and motor cogging (pqac-00000005). Non-back-drivable mechanisms such as lead screws inherently resist passive movement but are uncommon on low-cost arms designed for compliance and safety during contact (pqac-00000005).

### 2.2 Published Measurements

**No published quantitative measurements of drop distance, descent speed, or settling behavior for the PiPER, SO-100/SO-101, myCobot, or similar low-cost brakeless arms were found in the peer-reviewed literature.** This is a significant gap. The closest analogue is cable-driven robot work by Fanton et al., who showed that "merely holding a pose normally requires continued motor input specifically to prevent cable backdriving" (pqac-00000004). For the PiPER, which uses servo motors with harmonic or planetary reducers, the back-drive resistance depends on the specific reducer's efficiency and friction coefficient, which is typically not published for hobby-grade actuators.

### 2.3 Mitigations

Several mitigation strategies are described in the literature:

- **Mechanical brakes:** Fanton et al. developed a Bi-Directional Self-Locking Clutch (BDSLC) that "requires no input power" and allows "motors can be shut down while the robot remains secured" (pqac-00000004). Electromagnetic brakes with fail-safe features that "maintain actuator position during power failures or system faults" are described by Ullah et al. (pqac-00000003).
- **Controlled stop before de-energizing (Category 1 approach):** The arm commands a gravity-stable or supported park pose before power removal, as the IEC 60204-1 Category 1 concept envisions: controlled deceleration followed by power removal (pqac-00000001). For brakeless arms, the park pose must be one where gravity produces minimal or no torque on any joint, or where the arm rests on a physical support.
- **Rest/park poses:** Commanding the arm to a folded, gravity-stable configuration before de-energizing is a practical mitigation for the PiPER. The worst case for gravity drop is the fully extended horizontal pose.
- **UPS-backed controllers:** Not specifically discussed in the retrieved literature for small arms, but conceptually straightforward: a UPS on the controller and motor supply provides time for a Category 1 controlled stop sequence before supply exhaustion.
- **Physical supports/cradles:** A mechanical cradle at the park position catches the arm if it sags after de-energizing. This is analogous to the "supported park positions" concept and addresses the fundamental problem that brakeless arms cannot hold arbitrary poses unpowered.

### 2.4 First-Principles Analysis

For a 6-DOF arm with 500 mm reach and 1.5 kg payload held horizontally, a simple worst-case free-fall estimate gives: at the TCP, gravitational acceleration of ~9.8 m/s² produces ~70 mm of drop in the first 120 ms. However, actual behavior will be significantly slower due to motor cogging torque, gear-train friction, and viscous damping in the actuators. **This must be measured empirically for the specific PiPER arm** because the dominant terms—reducer back-drive friction and motor electromagnetic braking—are not publicly specified.

## 3. Collision Testing with Inexpensive Instruments

### 3.1 ISO/TS 15066 Body-Region Limits

ISO/TS 15066:2016 specifies biomechanical force and pressure limits for 29 body locations within 12 body regions. These limits originate from pain-onset studies using approximately 100 test subjects who generated about 9,000 analyzable measurements (pqac-00000011). Quasi-static force and pressure limits are specified directly; transient-impact thresholds "can generally be calculated by multiplying the corresponding values by two" (pqac-00000009). For example, the transient force limit for the back of the hand (body location 25) is 280 N (pqac-00000040).

### 3.2 Commercial Biofidelic Devices

The standard measurement approach uses "biofidelic, biomechanically humanlike instruments and sensors with body-like features" (pqac-00000009). Commercial devices such as the GTE CBSF-75 and CoboSafe include interchangeable spring constants (e.g., 75 N/mm for hand, 25 N/mm for sternum, 10 N/mm for abdomen) and damping elements (Shore hardness SH70 or SH10) to simulate different body regions (pqac-00000041). Force sensors operate at ≥1 kHz, with signals filtered using a 100 Hz Butterworth low-pass filter (pqac-00000047). Pressure measurement is typically via Fujifilm Prescale films, which are scanned, calibrated, and evaluated in software (pqac-00000015).

### 3.3 Interlaboratory Variability

Scibilia et al. conducted an interlaboratory comparison across four European research centers (CNR, Fraunhofer IFF, DTI, CEA), performing 1,456 total tests on UR10/UR10e robots. Force generally increased linearly with velocity but included a nonzero force offset, deviating from the simple inelastic model predicted by ISO/TS 15066. Results were influenced by the robot controller, sensor fixture, and distance from the robot base (pqac-00000042). Pressure measurements were found "difficult for end users to reproduce reliably because small robot or alignment differences and the cylindrical tool's edges generated high local pressure" (pqac-00000040).

Zimmermann et al. compared three commercial biofidelic devices and found average peak-force differences of approximately 5% under identical conditions, with differences reaching up to 10% under matched bench conditions. Using 30 repeated measurements at one condition, peak-force standard deviation was below 1% (pqac-00000043). The five-trial average standard deviations across the three devices were 1.4%, 0.4%, and 0.6% respectively (pqac-00000043).

A critical finding from Hartmann et al.'s systematic review is that "low-cost resistive sensors" lost signal linearity completely above approximately 150 N, with "correlation to a certified reference load cell collapsing because of uncompensated dynamic effects" (pqac-00000008). This means inexpensive force-sensing resistors are unsuitable for validating compliance with ISO/TS 15066 transient limits, which often exceed 150 N.

A more defensible low-cost approach is a calibrated piezoelectric or strain-gauge load cell (≥1 kHz sampling) mounted behind a body-region-appropriate spring and elastomer pad, with Fuji Prescale film for pressure. This replicates the architecture of commercial PFMDs. However, ISO/TS 15066 "does not precisely define the measurement setup or measurement uncertainties," so "different instruments can produce different results under identical loads" (pqac-00000012).

### 3.5 Measured Collision Forces for Low-Cost Arms

**No published collision-force measurements were found for the AgileX PiPER, SO-100/SO-101, myCobot, or other sub-$3k arms.** For the UR10 at speeds up to 400 mm/s, Ponikelský et al. measured forces up to 446 N near the robot base (pqac-00000015). For the BYU PiPER with its 1.5 kg payload and lower mass, collision forces are expected to be substantially lower due to the smaller effective mass. The arm's low mass (likely <5 kg total moving mass) may place most operating configurations well below ISO/TS 15066 transient limits even at moderate speeds, but this must be verified empirically.

## 4. Risk Assessment and Documentation for Robots in Academic Research Labs

### 4.1 Applicable Standards Framework

Robot cells in academic laboratories are subject to the same fundamental safety standards as industrial installations. ISO 12100 provides the general risk-assessment process; ISO 10218-1 and ISO 10218-2 address robot and robot-system safety respectively (the latter is "particularly directed to system integrators"); ISO/TS 15066 provides collaborative-robot-specific guidance; and ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 addresses "safe design, installation, operation, risk assessment, safety systems, training, and maintenance" (pqac-00000023, pqac-00000024). The integrator—which in a university lab is typically the research group—assumes responsibility for the completed cell because it incorporates guarding, protective devices, and other equipment (pqac-00000025).

### 4.2 Self-Driving Laboratory Safety

Mariano et al. present the most directly relevant published framework for robot safety in self-driving/cloud laboratories. Their methodology uses an iterative six-step process: "define the system, identify hazards, analyze scenarios, estimate risk, reduce risk, and validate the resulting safeguards" (pqac-00000017). In their Swiss case study (CAT+ West Hub), sixteen hazards were identified; robot–human collisions and chemical exposure were considered most critical. They emphasize that "collaborative robots should not be presumed safe and require rigorous validation, including experimental force and pressure measurements under realistic operating conditions" (pqac-00000017). Safety laser scanners were validated to detect personnel and trigger an immediate stop, and "safety-zone dimensions were checked against minimum separation distances in ISO 13857:2020" (pqac-00000035). **This is a peer-reviewed paper** (SLAS Technology, 2026; DOI: 10.1016/j.slast.2026.100463).

### 4.3 Attended vs. Unattended Operation

Zhang et al. propose a six-level autonomy framework for SDLs: Level 0 (manual) through Level 5 (full autonomy). Level 2 allows "AI execution under continuous human monitoring," while Level 3 permits "unattended or largely autonomous operation only within strictly defined operational design domains (ODDs), with a human available on standby." Advancement requires "typically hundreds of incident-free operations" rather than arbitrary schedules, and safety incidents trigger "automatic regression to a lower autonomy level" (pqac-00000056). **This is a preprint** (arXiv, Feb 2026; DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2602.15061).

Tobias and Wahab recommend that "knowledgeable humans review and approve every experimental plan and executable code before an autonomous experiment runs" and that "a human supervisor should remain available and empowered to pause or terminate an experiment, including through a 'kill switch'" (pqac-00000058).

Leong et al. recommend that "personnel should receive specialized training for human-robot shared spaces, safe maintenance, emergency procedures, and safety-rated monitored stops" and advocate retaining "detailed operational data, decision-making records, environmental parameters, and AI/ML logs, analogous to aviation black boxes, to enable post-incident analysis" (pqac-00000057). **This is a preprint** (ChemRxiv, Dec 2024; DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv-2024-2qx28).

### 4.4 Training and Signage

ANSI/RIA R15.06-2012 requires that the risk assessment identify hazards, estimate likelihood and severity, and determine protective measures. Training should include "robot programming, troubleshooting, and emergency-response procedures." Safeguards should include "emergency-stop buttons, safety interlocks, and protective barriers" (pqac-00000023). The VTT guidelines specify that the risk assessment should establish "intended use, operating modes, training, exposure, space and time limits, environmental conditions, and other relevant constraints" (pqac-00000030).

### 4.5 Incident Reports

**No published incident reports specifically involving low-cost or educational robot arms in university laboratories were found in the peer-reviewed literature.** Leong et al. cite industrial evidence that "inadequate safeguarding and insufficient warnings" are leading causes of robot injuries, and that "injuries may rise during the first two years of robotics adoption." Non-routine activities—"including troubleshooting, maintenance, repair, inspection, and cleaning—are highlighted as especially vulnerable" (pqac-00000062). **University-specific EHS policies for robot arms are largely unpublished internal documents;** no peer-reviewed compilation was found.

### 4.6 Periodic Re-Verification

No single cited standard mandates a fixed re-verification interval. The general principle from ISO 12100 and related standards is that "risk assessment and validation should be repeated after mitigation and when tasks, environment or operators change; ongoing monitoring should include close calls, incidents and feedback" (pqac-00000029, pqac-00000030). Zhang et al. recommend re-verification "after incidents, constraint violations, corrective changes, or evidence of model or sensor degradation" rather than on a fixed calendar (pqac-00000032).

### 5.1 Recommended Test Sequence

**Phase 1 (Before any operation near people):**
1. **V-01: E-stop functional test.** Verify the magnetic-switch E-stop actually removes power independently of software. This has never been tested. Use a multimeter or oscilloscope to confirm supply drops to zero within milliseconds of actuation. Test at minimum 3 times per condition (empty and loaded, multiple poses) (pqac-00000002).
2. **V-02: Gravity-drop characterization.** With the E-stop confirmed working, deliberately cut power in multiple poses and payloads and measure the resulting sag with calibrated video. This characterizes the actual hazard envelope.
3. **V-03: Controlled park test.** Implement and test a software-commanded park-to-supported-pose routine that completes before the IoT relay opens.
4. **V-04: Pi reboot test.** Deliberately reboot the Pi during motion and verify the arm does not produce hazardous motion on restart.

**Phase 2 (Before attended operation near people):**
5. **V-05: Collision force screening.** Using a calibrated load cell behind appropriate padding, measure peak contact forces at the worst-case pose and speed. Even screening data provides a defensible basis for speed limits.
6. **V-07: SOP walkthrough and training.** Every authorized user must complete training including emergency-stop actuation, spill response, and energy isolation.

**Phase 3 (Before considering unattended operation):**
7. **V-06: Perimeter safeguarding test** (if safety scanners or interlocks are added).
8. **V-08: Establish periodic re-verification schedule.**

### 5.2 Records

For each test, the minimum defensible record should include: test protocol with acceptance criteria defined before testing; date and personnel; hardware/software configuration and versions; raw data (video, traces, logs); results and comparison to acceptance criteria; anomalies and corrective actions; and reviewer signature (pqac-00000027, pqac-00000030). The VTT guidelines state that the responsible entity must "perform and document a risk assessment, address all applicable safety requirements and directives...compile and maintain a technical file" (pqac-00000025).

### 5.3 Key Gaps and Limitations

Several important limitations should be noted:

- **No published safety data exist for the PiPER arm or similar sub-$3k arms.** All stop-test, drop-test, and collision-force measurements must be generated locally. This is original characterization work, not verification against manufacturer specifications.
- **The PiPER's magnetic-switch E-stop on the supply line implements a de facto Stop Category 0,** but on a brakeless arm this may not achieve a mechanically safe state. A controlled-park-then-de-energize sequence (approximating Category 1) is strongly recommended as the normal shutdown path.
- **Low-cost resistive force sensors are unsuitable for ISO/TS 15066 validation** above approximately 150 N due to complete loss of linearity (pqac-00000008). A calibrated strain-gauge or piezoelectric load cell at ≥1 kHz is the minimum defensible instrument.
- **University EHS robot-specific policies are largely unpublished.** The lab should work with BYU's EHS office to establish local requirements, using the ISO 12100 / ISO 10218-2 framework and the SDL safety literature as a basis.
- **Unattended operation should not be attempted until Phase 1 and Phase 2 are complete,** all results are documented and reviewed, and the risk assessment explicitly addresses unattended failure modes. The Safe-SDL framework suggests requiring "hundreds of incident-free operations" before advancing autonomy levels (pqac-00000056).

This verification plan is designed to be achievable at university-lab cost using instruments likely available in an engineering department (oscilloscope, calibrated video, load cell, multimeter) while producing documentation that meets the intent of ISO 10218-2's verification requirements and the emerging SDL safety literature.
