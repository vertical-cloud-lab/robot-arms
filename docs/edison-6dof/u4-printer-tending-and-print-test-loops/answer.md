**BEAR / MAMA BEAR (Boston University).** The Bayesian Experimental Autonomous Researcher (BEAR), first reported in Gongora et al. 2020 (*Science Advances*, DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.aaz1708), uses five dual-extruder MakerGear M3 FDM printers arranged within the working radius of a six-axis Universal Robots UR5e arm, a Sartorius CP225D scale, and an Instron 5965 universal testing machine (gongora2020abayesianexperimental pages 1-2, gongora2020abayesianexperimental pages 2-3). The expanded campaign (Snapp et al. 2024, *Nature Communications*, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-48534-4) details the removal method: before each print, an arm-mounted camera checks whether the bed is clear; if cleaning is needed, the arm uses a scraper with up to ten retry attempts, taking a verification photo each time; if the bed remains dirty the printer is deactivated and an operator is notified (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 28-33). After printing, the bed is heated for TPE and TPU-1/2 (100 °C) to facilitate removal, or allowed to cool to 30 °C for PLA, PETG, Nylon, and TPU-3 (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 23-26). Failure modes include dirty beds, unsuccessful component grabs, misorientation, mass/height deviations >5%, severe print defects, insufficient strain data, and periodic need to replace worn polyimide tape and load new filament rolls (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 28-33). No explicit handling success-rate percentage was reported.

**FlexTrak automated sheet-flexing system (thesis, 2020).** This non-peer-reviewed student project describes a modular system for the Prusa i3 MK3 that elevates the magnetic spring-steel sheet off the bed (requiring approximately 15.48 N normal force to break magnetic retention, and 6.76 N horizontal pull), feeds it through a curved bending track using powered rollers, and uses passive scrapers to remove remaining debris (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 60-70, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 56-60). A decision-matrix comparison rated sheet flexing higher than disposable roll media (3.75 vs. 3.1) for reliability, modularity, and maintainability (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 52-56). Scraper-only removal was tested and found unreliable: it damaged small parts, retained parts on the blade, was limited to about 38% of the bed area (150 cm² maximum on a 400 cm² bed), required favorable part orientation and rafts, and produced visible scrape marks after 250 cycles (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 28-33, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 23-28). Print-head collision/ejection was also described, wherein modified G-code causes the print head to ram the part off; this requires no hardware modification but needs parts approximately 1 inch tall and becomes harder as contact area increases (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 28-33). Overnight, weekend, and full-spool reliability tests were planned but not quantitatively reported (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 1-7, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 7-9).

**ATCAM (Mazhari et al. 2021, *Journal of Materials Engineering and Performance*, DOI: 10.1007/s11665-021-06042-2).** This peer-reviewed system prints dynamic coupons on a Prusa MK3S and ejects them using an in-situ actuator (ISA) printed on the build surface; the toolhead pulls back the ISA, which releases stored energy through a 25.4 mm sphere to impact-eject each coupon toward a load cell (mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 1-2, mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 2-4, mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 2-2). The actuator generates approximately 2 N of force, and the coupon base is designed with reduced contact area to lower adhesion. Each ISA was reused for 100 nondestructive ejection cycles, after which manual replacement was required (mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 2-4). A total of 3,000 coupons were generated across three PLA feedstocks (mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 4-5). No explicit ejection success rate was reported, but automated ejection was described as the most difficult engineering challenge (mazhari2021automatedtestingand pages 1-2).

**SmartLab (Yun et al. 2025, *Journal of Computational Design and Engineering*, DOI: 10.1093/jcde/qwaf034).** This peer-reviewed system uses a San Robotics M1013 arm on a Syscon Scorpion A-100 mobile base. After printing, the robot removes the entire printer bed with a suction gripper, measures the specimen with a wrist camera, changes to a finger gripper via a pneumatic gripper changer, detaches the specimen, and places it on a rack for downstream measurement and testing (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 6-7, yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 5-6). No bed temperature or cool-down protocol was specified in the retrieved evidence.

**Commercial systems.** Prusa Research's automated farm system (Prusa Pro AFS) and other commercial plate-swapping solutions (e.g., Bambu Lab conveyor concepts) exist as trade products, but **no peer-reviewed trial counts, success rates, failure-mode studies, or comparative cost analyses were located.** These should be treated as commercial/trade sources only.

### (2) Closed-Loop Design-Print-Test Systems with Robotic Specimen Handling

**BEAR (2020) and MAMA BEAR / expanded campaign (2024)** are the most extensively documented systems. BEAR demonstrated fully autonomous closed-loop operation: Bayesian optimization selected a structure design, OpenSCAD generated the STL, Slic3r produced G-code, OctoPrint managed the printer, the UR5e retrieved the part, weighed it, loaded it into the Instron 5965, and the compression test result fed back into Gaussian-process regression to guide the next experiment (gongora2020abayesianexperimental pages 5-6). In the original campaign, 600 designs were tested in triplicate without human intervention; 32 experiments in 12 hours matched a 1,800-experiment grid campaign, and 64 experiments in 24 hours exceeded it (gongora2020abayesianexperimental pages 4-5). The expanded campaign reported 25,387 experiments across seven materials (TPE, TPU-1/NinjaFlex, TPU-2/Cheetah, TPU-3/Armadillo, Nylon, PETG, PLA), with experiments running approximately 60% of wall-clock time and each mechanical test taking approximately 10 minutes (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 28-33, snapp2024superlativemechanicalenergy pages 2-3, snapp2024superlativemechanicalenergy pages 7-8). This directly contradicts any claim that no publication shows an arm unloading 3D printers inside a closed experimental loop. Human interventions included periodic manual bed cleaning when automated scraping failed after ten attempts, filament replacement, and polyimide tape maintenance (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 28-33). The authors state limitations including manufacturing variability (mass CV 4.6%, toughness CV 12.8%) and the need for human monitoring to modify campaign parameters during the multi-year operation (gongora2020abayesianexperimental pages 2-3, snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 33-39).

**SmartLab (2025)** demonstrated a closed loop for tensile testing of ASTM D638 Type I specimens, varying raster angle and line distance across 90 specimens manufactured and tested without human specimen handling. The per-specimen handling cycle was 17 minutes (versus 9 minutes for a human researcher), but 24-hour continuous operation yielded a projected throughput of 21.1 specimens/day and 633/month versus 8.42/day and 252.6/month for human researchers working 8-hour shifts (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 10-12, yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 12-13). The robotic researcher positioned specimens on the UTM with average errors of 268 μm and 0.19°, compared to 0.74° angular error for human researchers (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 8-9). The main limitation was battery life (eight experimental cycles before manual recharging) and scheduling of multiple parallel tasks (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 10-12, yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 13-13).

**Perception-driven polymer laboratory (Hernández-del-Valle et al. 2026, ChemRxiv preprint, DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv.15007960/v1).** This non-peer-reviewed preprint describes a TurtleBot 4 mobile robot with a UR10 cobot workcell processing injection-molded Charpy specimens (not FFF-printed). It processed 190 specimens in 274 minutes (1 min 26 s average per specimen), with a recoverable-error rate of 1.05% (one grasp failure and one incomplete cut detected and corrected) and two additional undetected incomplete cuts (1.05%) (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12). Telegram-based alerts and event-driven recovery reduced supervision to brief, occasional interventions, supporting a one-researcher-per-laboratory model rather than one-operator-per-instrument (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12, hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 12-14).

Key findings: Spaghetti/stringing detection using DeepLabV3 with tailored ASPP modules reached 95% accuracy at optimal dilation rate (marin20243dprintingerrors pages 2-4). Multi-class defect classification (cracking, layer shift, off-platform, stringing, warping) with MobileNetV2 transfer learning achieved 97% overall accuracy, while EfficientNetB0 reached 87.7%, with confusion primarily between cracking and layer shifting (aktepe2026deeplearningapplication pages 3-6). Extrusion flow-rate error detection using ResNet-18 with a global-shutter camera achieved 96.1% classification accuracy at both low and high print speeds. The DaR3D thesis system detected print slippage at 89.6% accuracy using webcam images with controlled lighting. The commercial Obico/Spaghetti Detective system uses YOLOv2 but no independently validated accuracy metric was located in peer-reviewed literature (charia2025realtimestringingdetection pages 2-4, eisenhour2022thedar3dsystema pages 46-51, marin20243dprintingerrors pages 1-2).

For **plate-clear verification**, the BEAR system uses an arm-mounted camera to check whether the bed is clear before each print, followed by robotic scraper cleaning with photographic verification after each attempt, up to ten retries (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 28-33). No published false-clear rate or classification accuracy for this verification step was found.

**No peer-reviewed study was found that specifically addresses automatic dimensional measurement of printed specimens before testing using low-cost cameras**, although SmartLab uses a wrist camera for dimension measurement with a reported 268 μm positional accuracy at the UTM (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 8-9), and the perception-driven polymer lab uses YOLOv11 segmentation and ArUco-based pose estimation for specimen localization (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12, hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 4-5).

### (4) Print Farms with Robotic Tending

**No peer-reviewed publication was found that reports quantitative throughput, uptime, human-hours-per-print, or cost-per-part for a robotically tended FFF print farm in comparison to a manual farm.** The FlexTrak thesis describes a modular system designed to attach one removal mechanism per printer, enabling queued production through OctoPrint on Raspberry Pis, with the goal of continuous operation requiring human intervention only for filament spool replacement every few days (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 1-7, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 7-9). However, the planned reliability tests (overnight, weekend, and full-spool runs) were proposed rather than reported (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 7-9). Budzik et al. (2024) describe a conceptual robotic MEX cell in which a collaborative arm transfers platforms and parts but provide no measured farm-level metrics (budzik2024possibilitiesofautomating pages 3-5, budzik2024possibilitiesofautomating pages 1-2). Post-processing is estimated to add approximately 17–100% to printing time and roughly 30% of manufacturing cost, with labor costs of approximately USD 30–100/hour (dizon2021postprocessingof3dprinted pages 28-29). These figures suggest substantial economic incentive for automation but remain indirect comparisons rather than controlled farm studies. **This represents a clear gap in the published literature.**

### (5) Remote or Cloud Users Submitting Design-Print-Test Jobs

**SmartLab Tanzania pilot (2025):** Twenty-one students at Arusha Technical College in Tanzania remotely conducted material-testing experiments on equipment hosted at Seoul National University in Korea. Students controlled the system via a GUI and monitored it through real-time 360° VR streaming; temporary network disconnections did not halt experiments, as recorded VR video could be replayed (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 10-12, yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 9-10). Two full cycles of material testing were successfully completed remotely (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 9-10). The system tolerated unstable network conditions without significant interruption to experimental procedures (yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 10-12, yun2025smartlabflexibleand pages 13-13). No numerical job-completion rate or turnaround-time distribution was reported.

**BEAR external-user pilot:** No measured outcomes (completion rate, turnaround, interventions) for external users submitting jobs to the BEAR system were found in the accessible published literature. The BEAR data are reportedly available through kablab.org/data (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 33-39), but no publication describing external-user pilot results was located.

**No other published cloud-lab design-print-test system with measured user outcomes was identified.** This area remains largely undocumented in the peer-reviewed literature.

### (6) Feasibility Analysis for the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab

**Adhesion forces.** Kaščak et al. (2024, *Applied Sciences*, DOI: 10.3390/app14020945) measured PETG tear-off force on a PEI-coated spring-steel build plate at 53.48 ± 7.95 N (mean ± SD over 12 trials), compared with 33.09 ± 5.52 N on uncoated aluminum (kascak2024adhesiontestingdevice pages 8-9). PLA was explicitly excluded from that study because it "generally adheres sufficiently to commonly used uncontaminated surfaces" (kascak2024adhesiontestingdevice pages 6-8). **No peer-reviewed measurement of PLA adhesion force on textured PEI was located**, representing a data gap. Higher bed temperature generally increases adhesion, with temperatures slightly above the glass-transition temperature recommended for first-layer bonding (kascak2024adhesiontestingdevice pages 2-4). PETG showed higher shear resistance than PLA on aluminum substrates: 5.94 MPa vs. 3.92 MPa at their respective optimal conditions (camposjurado2025manufacturingofpolymer–metal pages 12-14).

**Sheet-flexing forces.** The FlexTrak system measured approximately 15.48 N normal force to break Prusa MK3 magnetic bed retention and 6.76 N horizontal pull after the edge was lifted (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 60-70, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 56-60). This is well within the PiPER arm's 15 N gripper force specification, but the arm's J5 ±70° wrist limitation would make straight-down grasps near the table infeasible. Sheet flexing was rated the most reliable removal method, requiring the least effort and posing minimal risk of part damage in a qualitative evaluation (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 19-23, me2020increasing3dprinter pages 37-42).

**Feasibility for the PiPER arm on A1 mini bed-slingers.** The A1 mini is a bed-slinger (Y-axis moves the bed), which means the magnetic spring-steel sheet can in principle be slid off the bed by the arm. With approximately 15 N of gripping force, the PiPER could grip the sheet edge and pull it, since horizontal pull requires only about 6.76 N (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 60-70). However, the 53 N tear-off force for PETG on PEI significantly exceeds the arm's force budget, so direct scraping or perpendicular prying of well-adhered parts would likely fail. The recommended strategy for small PLA toys is: (a) allow cooling to room temperature (snapp2023autonomousdiscoveryof pages 23-26), which substantially reduces adhesion on textured PEI; (b) slide the magnetic sheet off the bed (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 60-70); and (c) flex the sheet to pop parts free, as commercial flex plates "require the least effort" and are "reliable" with "minimal risk of part damage" (me2020increasing3dprinter pages 19-23). The ±70° J5 limitation means the arm should approach the bed edge laterally rather than from directly above.

**Grasping irregular printed parts.** No study specifically examining parallel-gripper grasping of irregular 3D-printed parts was found. General robotic grasping literature reports that underactuated grippers achieve 90–98% success rates on mixed-object benchmarks, and CNN-based grasp planners on parallel grippers can handle unknown objects including those with irregular shapes. For the specific case of small printed toys, compliant or soft-padded parallel jaw inserts would improve grasp stability. PETG inserts with 30% hexagonal infill showed distinct friction and wear behavior compared to bulk polymers, suggesting that printed gripper jaw liners could be customized for the application. The 1.5 kg payload of the PiPER arm is well above the mass of small PLA toys (typically <50 g) but would limit handling of multiple parts simultaneously or large fixtures.

**Practical recommendations for the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab:** (1) For the December A1 mini toy batch, flexing the magnetic PEI sheet after cooling to room temperature is the most feasible and best-documented approach within the arm's force budget. (2) For later mechanical testing (tensegrity compression/drop tests), the BEAR paradigm of robot-arm transfer between printer, scale, and testing machine is directly applicable and proven at >25,000-experiment scale. (3) Camera-based bed-clear verification should be implemented following the BEAR protocol (arm-mounted camera check with retry logic), and spaghetti/failure detection using MobileNetV2 or similar lightweight models can achieve ≥95% accuracy with modest training data.
