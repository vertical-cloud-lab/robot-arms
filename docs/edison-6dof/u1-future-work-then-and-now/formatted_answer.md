#### Key Quoted Limitations by Theme

**Grasping and Grippers.** Maffettone et al. (2023) state that "a universal gripper that would exhibit the dexterity of a human hand" remains impractical, recommending instead "3D printed parts and/or use tool exchangers" and task-specific fingers (maffettoneUnknownyearwhatismissing pages 7-8). Zwirnmann et al. (2023) demonstrated monolithic dual-extrusion 3D-printed compliant fingers at approximately €3 per finger, but noted that "comprehensive optimization of all finger dimensions was outside the publication's scope" and that the benchmark "could not handle a Petri dish" (zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 5-7). Harazono et al. (2024, preprint) found that "commercially available grippers often lacked sufficient width to grasp microplates," necessitating custom 3D-printed fingers, and that repeatability was direction-dependent with lid-placement failure occurring sharply beyond a 2.190 mm y-axis offset (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 11-15). Jiang et al. (2023) reported that their dual-arm system "cannot dispense sub-milligram quantities" and struggled with "compressible powders such as calcium carbonate and highly hygroscopic or deliquescent solids" (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11).

**Perception and Calibration.** Zhou et al. (2025, LIRA) identified that "robot manipulation in most SDL workflows operates in an open-loop manner, lacking real-time error detection and error correction" (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). ORGANA (Darvish et al. 2025) noted that "robust, model-free perception of transparent objects remains unresolved because such objects can be textureless and reflective" (darvish2025organaarobotic pages 14-15). Sasaki et al. (2025) stated that their system "reproduces demonstrated motions without adapting to changed target-object or equipment positions" and that "motion-capture position-estimation errors can substantially reduce task success" (sasaki2025roboticsystemfor pages 23-25).

**Error Detection and Recovery.** Seifrid et al. (2022) proposed "online computer-vision process control to monitor every operation and detect failures early" as future work (seifrid2022autonomouschemicalexperiments pages 8-9). Zhou et al. (2025) achieved 97.9% inspection accuracy but noted that "recovery still used hard-coded interpretation logic" and that the "complete closed-loop pipeline from reaction execution through PXRD analysis" had not been demonstrated (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). The ADePT framework (Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia 2026) identified that systems "require human intervention to modify task logic or recover failures" and called for "standardized reporting of errors, recovery, and reproducibility" (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 6-7, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 13-13).

**Reliability and Uptime.** Burger et al. (2020) reported that the robot "was charged but idle for approximately 32% of the experiment, primarily while waiting for the GC" over an 8-day, 688-experiment campaign, with operators alerted when "workflow failures occurred" and "most errors could be reset remotely" (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). Dai et al. (2024) emphasized that "concatenating many hardware and software modules creates a demanding reliability requirement: each module must have a very low failure rate" and that "the reported workflow required over a year of development and debugging before achieving stable operation" (dai2024autonomousmobilerobots pages 7-8). Moran et al. (2026) concluded that "complete SDL autonomy remains limited because human intervention is generally required between experimental campaigns for maintenance" and that "this appears to apply to nearly every SDL example published in the literature to date" (moran2026theriseof pages 9-11).

**Throughput.** Burger et al. (2020) identified GC analysis as the throughput bottleneck and suggested that "idle time could be used for additional experiments in parallel" (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). Maffettone et al. (2023) called for "synchronization of data, multi-fidelity and multimodal algorithms, distributed orchestration" to scale throughput (maffettoneUnknownyearwhatismissing pages 9-10).

**Multi-Station Integration and Scheduling.** ORGANA noted that "PDDL-domain definitions" required for planning "may be difficult for chemists without planning expertise" and that "joint task-and-motion planning and scheduling currently add overhead" (darvish2025organaarobotic pages 14-15). Maffettone et al. (2023) stated that "scaling beyond a single laboratory requires connecting distinct hardware modules and distributed SDLs, potentially through fixed transport systems or mobile robots" (maffettoneUnknownyearwhatismissing pages 10-11).

**Human Oversight and Remote Operation.** ORGANA users "supported retaining humans in the autonomy loop, and half were uncertain about fully autonomous, start-to-finish operation" (darvish2025organaarobotic pages 14-15). Burger et al. (2020) used "continuous CCTV coverage" for remote monitoring with remote error reset capability (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12).

**Safety.** Leong et al. (2024, preprint) proposed that future systems should impose "conservative or dynamically adjusted constraints on end-effector orientation, velocity, and acceleration" and deploy vision for detecting "spills, improper storage, equipment malfunctions, procedural deviations, manipulation failures, human presence, and PPE noncompliance" (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 20-23).

**Cost.** Maffettone et al. (2023) called for "engineering and feasibility analyses addressing capital and operational costs, including instrumentation, robotics, space, materials, power, maintenance, replacement, and architectural integration" (maffettoneUnknownyearwhatismissing pages 9-10). Jiang et al. (2023) noted their dual-arm robot was "significantly cheaper than most commercially available solid dispensing platforms" (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11).

### Part 2: Which Later Papers (2023–2026) Address These Items, with Measured Results

**Vision-based error detection and calibration.** Zhou et al. (2025, LIRA; DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1) directly addressed the open-loop limitation. Using ArUco markers and VLMs on edge hardware, LIRA achieved localization accuracy of 0.92 mm (cube-based) to 2.42 mm (20 mm ArUco, unfiltered) over 100 repeated calibrations, and 97.5% error-inspection success across 200 deliberately introduced placement errors (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 3-6, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9).

**Vision-corrected manipulation of labware.** Fontenot et al. (2026; DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00541h, peer-reviewed) demonstrated closed-loop manipulation of transparent glass substrates using deep-learning micro-error correction. Over 130 independent reload cycles, the system achieved 98.5% first-attempt placement accuracy; both misplacements were automatically detected and corrected, yielding 100% success by the second attempt. Classification confidence ranged from 97–100% across small, medium, and large errors (fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 1-3, fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 8-10, fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 10-12).

**3D-printed grippers and compliant fingers.** Zwirnmann et al. (2023; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2302.03644) demonstrated taxonomy-based, 3D-printed compliant fingers at ~€3/finger that handled diverse biolaboratory containers (zwirnmann2023towardsflexiblebiolaboratory pages 5-7). Harazono et al. (2024, preprint; DOI: 10.1101/2023.12.29.573685) provided quantitative microplate-handling benchmarks showing direction-dependent repeatability and sharp failure thresholds (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 11-15).

**Digital twin for pre-deployment validation.** MATTERIX (Darvish et al. 2025; DOI: 10.1038/s43588-025-00924-4) demonstrated sim-to-real transfer for chemistry lab automation with collision-free trajectory optimization and workflow precondition checking, though chemical simulation remained "semi-quantitative" (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 17-18).

**Perception-driven polymer SDL with measured error rates.** Hernández-del-Valle et al. (2026, preprint; DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv.15007960/v1) reported processing 190 polymer specimens with 1.05% recoverable errors (1 pick failure and 1 incomplete cut detected and retried) and 1.05% undetected errors across a cobot/mobile-robot workflow integrating extrusion, injection molding, and mechanical testing (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12).

**Remote laboratory operation.** Hamm et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1002/aisy.202300566) demonstrated internet-based remote control of a robotic arm for cell culture experiments conducted from Paris (France) on equipment in Seoul (Korea), plus educational remote experiments from Indonesia. Robot positioning repeatability was reported as 0.02 mm. The automated transduction process produced a statistically significantly higher number of infected cells than the manual process (p = 0.038). However, no quantitative remote-specific performance metrics (latency, error rates, task completion rates) were reported (hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 7-8, hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 8-9).

**Constrained task and motion planning.** Yoshikawa et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1007/s10514-023-10136-2) and later work demonstrated LLM-guided planning with spillage and collision constraints for chemistry, but did not report campaign-level reliability statistics (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 6-7).

**Autonomous error recovery with replanning.** Skreta et al. (2024; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2401.04157, preprint) proposed RePLan for VLM-based online replanning when plans fail, but results were on general robotics benchmarks rather than SDL campaigns.

### Part 3: Unaddressed Future-Work Items as of Late 2026, Ranked

The following items recur across multiple papers and remain substantively unaddressed. They are ranked by (i) recurrence across the literature and (ii) feasibility for a lab with a sub-$5k arm (AgileX PiPER), Raspberry Pi cameras, 3D-printed fixtures, and remote users.

**Rank 1 (highest recurrence, high feasibility): Standardized uptime and intervention-rate reporting.** Cited by Burger 2020, Dai 2024, Szymanski 2023, Moran 2026, ADePT 2026, and Maffettone 2023. No SDL has published a denominator-based intervention rate (interventions per operation or per hour). A BYU lab running closed-loop campaigns could contribute this metric with minimal additional hardware—simply by logging every human touch (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12, moran2026theriseof pages 9-11, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 13-13).

**Rank 2 (high recurrence, moderate feasibility): Autonomous error recovery beyond simple retry.** Cited by Zhou 2025, Darvish 2025, Seifrid 2022, Sasaki 2025, ADePT 2026. LIRA's recovery still uses hard-coded logic (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). True adaptive recovery—diagnosing failure mode, selecting corrective action, verifying correction—remains undemonstrated. A Pi-camera-based VLM inspection loop is feasible on a PiPER arm but would require substantial software development.

**Rank 3 (high recurrence, moderate feasibility): Vision-corrected manipulation primitives with explicit open-loop baseline.** Cited by Zhou 2025, Fontenot 2026, Seifrid 2022, Sasaki 2025. Fontenot et al. achieved 98.5% corrected accuracy but did not publish an explicit open-loop baseline for comparison (fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 3-4). A controlled study comparing open-loop vs. AprilTag/camera-corrected pick-and-place on labware with a sub-$5k arm is directly feasible at BYU.

**Rank 4 (moderate recurrence, high feasibility): Independent accuracy/repeatability characterization of sub-$5k arms.** Cited by Harazono 2024, ADePT 2026, Maffettone 2023. No ISO 9283-style independent characterization of the AgileX PiPER or similar sub-$5k arms exists in the peer-reviewed literature. Zhang (2026) validated the PiPER only in simulation; YOR (Anjaria et al. 2026) reported cost (US$2,500) but no measured accuracy (anjaria2026yoryourown pages 5-8, zhang2026openeaiplatformanopensource pages 8-9). A dial-gauge or camera-based repeatability study at BYU would fill this gap.

**Rank 5 (moderate recurrence, moderate feasibility): Robot arm loading/unloading 3D printers or powder-processing equipment in a closed loop.** No publication was found demonstrating an arm autonomously loading and unloading a 3D printer (e.g., Bambu Lab) or powder-processing equipment inside a closed experimental loop. Hernández-del-Valle et al. (2026) demonstrated cobot load/unload of an injection molder but not a 3D printer (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12). This is directly relevant to BYU's tensegrity and alloy workflows and is feasible with 3D-printed fixtures and AprilTag localization.

**Rank 6 (moderate recurrence, low-moderate feasibility): Robust autonomous solid dispensing with sub-$5k arms.** Cited by Jiang 2023, Seifrid 2022, Maffettone 2023. Jiang's dual-arm system achieved 2 mg accuracy but struggled with specific material classes (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11). Replicating this with SO-101 or PiPER arms and 3D-printed spatulas is feasible but materials-dependent.

**Rank 7 (moderate recurrence, low feasibility): Certified safety for unattended remote operation.** Cited by Leong 2024, ADePT 2026, ORGANA 2025. The PiPER has no certified safety functions; implementing SIL-rated safety on a sub-$5k arm is not straightforward. The BYU relay-switched E-stop is a pragmatic workaround but not a certified solution (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 20-23).

**Rank 8 (lower recurrence, high feasibility): Remote user supervision with measured outcomes.** Cited by Burger 2020, Hamm 2024, ORGANA 2025. Hamm et al. demonstrated Seoul-to-Paris remote operation but reported no quantitative remote-specific metrics (hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 7-8). A study measuring task completion rates, latency, and intervention frequency for remote users running experiments on a PiPER via web interface is straightforward at BYU.

### Part 4: Specific Post-January-2026 Publications

**(a) Measured interventions or failures per operation for an arm in a real SDL campaign.**
The closest publication is Hernández-del-Valle et al. (August 2026, ChemRxiv preprint; DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv.15007960/v1), which reports processing 190 polymer specimens with 2 recoverable errors (1.05%) and 2 undetected errors (1.05%) in a cobot/mobile-robot workflow (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12). However, this reports error rates rather than a standardized human-intervention rate per operation. Moran et al. (May 2026; DOI: 10.1038/s44182-026-00094-z) note that intervention between campaigns appears universal but provide no quantified rates (moran2026theriseof pages 9-11). **No SDL has published a standardized interventions-per-operation metric as of late 2026.**

**(b) Independent accuracy or repeatability characterization of the AgileX PiPER or another sub-$5k 6-DOF arm.**
Zhang (2026; DOI: 10.32920/32253717) integrated the AgileX PiPER in an NVIDIA Isaac Sim simulation framework with AprilTag detection and MoveIt2, validating autonomous pick-and-place in simulation but not reporting physical accuracy or repeatability measurements. Anjaria et al. (February 2026, preprint; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2602.11150) selected the PiPER for YOR at US$2,500 per arm, 4.2 kg weight, but provided no ISO 9283-style characterization (anjaria2026yoryourown pages 5-8). OpenEAI-Platform (Zhang et al. 2026, preprint; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2606.03392) benchmarked the PiPER on manipulation tasks, finding average success rates of 0.64 across four tasks, with failures "concentrated in grasp initiation and precise placement" attributed to its "distinct kinematic and actuation design" (zhang2026openeaiplatformanopensource pages 8-9). **No peer-reviewed independent accuracy/repeatability characterization of the PiPER or comparable sub-$5k arm exists as of late 2026.** Harazono et al. (2024 preprint; DOI: 10.1101/2023.12.29.573685) provide the closest methodology—a dial-gauge-based repeatability platform for a low-cost arm—but tested a different robot (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 11-15).

**(c) Vision-corrected manipulation primitives compared open-loop versus corrected on labware.**
Fontenot et al. (2026; DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00541h, peer-reviewed) demonstrated closed-loop vision-corrected manipulation of transparent glass substrates with deep-learning error detection, achieving 98.5% first-attempt success (128/130) and 100% after automatic correction. The system used a 5-DOF xArm with 0.1 mm stated repeatability, fused geometric and CNN models with 97–100% classification confidence, and automatically corrected all 10 pre-programmed failures on the second attempt (fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 1-3, fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 8-10, fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 10-12, fontenot2026closedlooproboticmanipulation pages 3-4). However, the paper does not report a separate open-loop baseline; instead it characterizes the closed-loop system's performance. LIRA (Zhou et al. 2025; DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1) achieved localization accuracy from 0.92 mm (cube-based calibration) to 1.51 mm (filtered ArUco) and 97.5% inspection success across 200 test cases, but likewise did not provide a controlled open-loop comparison (cooper2025liralocalizationinspection pages 3-6). **A direct, controlled open-loop vs. vision-corrected comparison on standard labware has not been published.**

**(d) Arm loading and unloading 3D printers or powder-processing equipment inside a closed experimental loop.**
**No published evidence was found.** Hernández-del-Valle et al. (2026 preprint) demonstrated cobot load/unload of an injection molder within a polymer SDL, involving "8 load/unload operations by the robot arms" (hernandezdelvalle2026perceptiondrivenexperimentalworkflows pages 10-12), but this was for injection molding, not 3D printing. Dezaki et al. (2026 preprint; DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv.15010111/v1) demonstrated autonomous robotic loading of a furnace at 1600°C for solid-state synthesis, but this involved a furnace rather than a 3D printer. No publication demonstrates an arm autonomously loading build plates into or removing parts from a 3D printer (e.g., Bambu Lab, Prusa) or powder-processing equipment (e.g., ball mill, sieve, gas atomizer) as part of a closed experimental loop.

**(e) Remote users supervising or teleoperating a laboratory arm over the internet, with measured outcomes.**
Hamm et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1002/aisy.202300566, peer-reviewed) demonstrated remote operation of a modular robotic platform for cell culture, with experiments "successfully performed on the robot platform located in Seoul, Korea, by users in Paris, France" and educational remote experiments from Yogyakarta, Indonesia (hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 7-8). The automated transduction process produced significantly more infected cells than manual operation (p = 0.038) (hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 8-9). Robot positioning repeatability was 0.02 mm, and TCP/IP communication was used for control (hamm2024amodularrobotic pages 10-10). However, no remote-specific quantitative metrics (latency, task completion rate, error rate, throughput comparison) were reported. Burger et al. (2020) established remote error reset via CCTV as a practical capability (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). LIRA (Zhou et al. 2025) supports "remote vision inspection and seamless multi-platform communication" (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 8-9). **No publication reports quantitative measured outcomes specifically attributable to remote (vs. local) supervision or teleoperation of a laboratory arm, such as remote-specific success rates, latency impacts, or intervention frequencies.**

---

### Summary of Key Gaps for the BYU Vertical Cloud Lab

The most actionable and feasible contributions from a lab with a sub-$5k AgileX PiPER arm, Raspberry Pi cameras, 3D-printed fixtures, and remote users would be:

1. **Publish standardized intervention and failure metrics** for real SDL campaigns (interventions per operation, per hour, per experiment)—this is the single most-cited gap across the literature and requires only systematic logging.

2. **Conduct an independent ISO 9283-style accuracy and repeatability characterization** of the AgileX PiPER using cameras or dial gauges—no such characterization exists in the peer-reviewed literature.

3. **Perform a controlled open-loop vs. vision-corrected comparison** for pick-and-place of labware (vials, crucibles, SEM stubs) using AprilTags and Pi cameras on the PiPER arm.

4. **Demonstrate arm loading/unloading of Bambu Lab 3D printers** and powder-processing equipment in a closed design-print-test loop—this has not been published for any 3D printer.

5. **Report measured outcomes for remote users** running experiments via web interface, including task success rates, latency, and intervention frequency compared with local operation.

These gaps are well-aligned with the lab's existing hardware and workflows (aluminium-alloy design, tensegrity structures, gas atomization, powder characterization) and represent contributions that the broader SDL community has explicitly called for but not yet delivered.
