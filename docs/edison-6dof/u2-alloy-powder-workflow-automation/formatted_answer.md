Question: Context. We run a small cloud lab at BYU (the Vertical Cloud Lab) with an AgileX PiPER 6-DOF arm (about $2.5k, 1.5 kg payload, parallel gripper, about 15 N available at the flange), SO-101 leader/follower arms for teleoperation and imitation learning, Raspberry Pi 5 wrist and fixed cameras, AprilTags and 3D-printed fixtures. Our own schedule estimates 10 to 15 person-hours of hands-on work per atomizer run, mostly after the run, and none of it is timed yet. Aluminium powder is a combustible metal (NFPA 484, now NFPA 660); operators wear full-face respirators for open-powder work; and the arm will stay out of the atomizer chamber. Peer efforts we know of include CMU's Materials Innovation Cloud Lab (also a rePOWDER), Texas A&M's metals self-driving lab, robotic metallography efforts, and printer-based SEM powder preparation (Milsted et al. 2025, EMSBot).

(3) Solid-specimen steps: removing parts from LPBF build plates, sectioning, mounting, grinding and polishing (robotic metallography), automated Archimedes density, automated Vickers or micro-hardness, and robotic loading of tensile specimens. Give reported success rates, cycle times and failure modes.
(4) Charge preparation: robotic weighing and kitting of alloy charges from pieces or powders, and crucible loading.
(5) Safety: what NFPA 484/660, ATEX or published guidance requires of robots and electronics that handle open or closed combustible metal powders (ignition sources, grounding, dust-tight enclosures, inerting), any incident reports, and how autonomous metal-powder labs (including A-Lab follow-ups under inert atmosphere) handle this.

### (1) Post-Atomization Powder Workup

**No peer-reviewed publication was found that describes a robotic or automated system for the full post-atomization workup chain—powder collection, loading sieve stacks, six-fraction classification, riffling/splitting, mass balance, jarring, labelling, or sealing O/N/H aliquots—with measured throughput, success rate, recovery, or cross-contamination control between alloys.** This represents a significant gap in the literature. Von Rohr (2024) notes that "automated grinding, sieving, and pelletizing" have "partially available commercial solutions" but that "these systems may require adaptation for the strictly controlled, inert conditions needed in autonomous materials laboratories" (rohr2024thechanginglandscape pages 5-6). The closest relevant systems are mass-balance and container-transport operations demonstrated by the A-Lab and AlloyBot (discussed under charge preparation), which validate that robot-to-balance transfer of powders and solids is technically mature (selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 4-7, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-7).

**PiPER feasibility:** The arm can serve as a lightweight courier for capped containers, individual sieve pans, and jars. However, a loaded six-sieve stack with 250–300 g powder and collection pans approaches or exceeds the 1.5 kg payload. Sieve loading/unloading, splitting, and O/N/H sample sealing (crimping) each require purpose-built fixtures or tools.

---

### (2) Powder Characterization Sample Preparation

**SEM stub preparation.** The EMSBot system (Milsted et al. 2025, DOI: 10.1039/d5dd00116a) is the state of the art, using a modified Creality Ender3 Pro 3D printer with a vacuum-pickup robot and electrostatic deposition to disperse powder onto carbon-tape-mounted SEM stubs. It operates at "10 kV, 10 mA, and a 5-second hold time" and demonstrated "more uniform dispersion and greater particle separation" than manual preparation, with reduced agglomeration (milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 2-3, milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 5-6). It retries stub pickup up to three times before requesting human intervention (milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 3-5). No aggregate throughput or quantitative success rate was reported. The system is designed for integration with autonomous laboratory workflows via a "documented API, GUI, Python backend" (milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 6-8). **Caution:** The electrostatic deposition method has not been validated for combustible aluminium powder, and open high-voltage dispersion near Al fines would require a formal ignition-risk assessment.

**Automated SEM-EDS analysis.** The AutoEMX framework (Giunto et al. 2025 preprint, DOI: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-7837297/v1; published 2026 in Nature Communications, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-76633-x) provides fully automated particle localization, EDS spot selection, spectral fitting, filtering, and unsupervised k-means clustering for phase-resolved compositional analysis. It "typically acquires about 100 spectra" per sample "in 20–45 minutes" and "processes tens of samples per day" (giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 4-6, giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 3-5). Validated across 74 powder samples and 38 elements (nitrogen to bismuth), it "consistently estimates compositions within 5–10% of their expected values" and requires only "one experimental standard per element" (giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 1-4, giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 14-15).

**Laser diffraction, flowability, and apparent/tap density.** No peer-reviewed robotic end-to-end workflow was found for any of these tests. Published powder-flowability studies compare standardized Hall/Carney funnels and rheometers but do not describe autonomous sample loading, cleaning, or recovery (rohr2024thechanginglandscape pages 5-6). These tests remain predominantly manual.

### (3) Solid-Specimen Steps

**Robotic metallography.** The most advanced published system is RASI (Robot-Assisted Automated Serial-Sectioning and Imaging), developed at BAM (Bundesanstalt für Materialforschung und -prüfung). It uses a "UR5e robotic arm with parallel grippers and a wrist camera, a modified Qpol250 BOT grinding/polishing unit, an etching station, an optical microscope, and a control workstation" (moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 3-4). RASI achieves "up to 150 slices per specimen per day" in "largely unattended, continuous 24/7 operation," with "XY resolution approaching 225 nm" and z-spacing "as fine as approximately 170 nm" for specimen volumes up to "approximately 15 × 15 × 15 mm³" (moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 4-4, moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 2-2). The system uses spray-based on-demand etching consuming "approximately 1–4 mL of etchant per slice, compared with roughly 60–200 mL for immersion baths" (moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 3-3). Demonstrated applications include AM 316L stainless steel melt pools, sintered steel porosity, and cast iron graphite (moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 1-2). RASI was first commissioned in 2021 and uses a UR5e (5 kg payload, ±0.03 mm repeatability) with a Python interface (lemiasheuski2023developmentofan pages 7-10). The Čermák et al. 2024 paper in *Practical Metallography* (DOI: 10.1515/pm-2024-0055, unobtainable) also addresses robotic metallographic sample preparation for precision and safety, but was not accessible for review.

**Automated hardness testing.** Commercial hardness testers with automated XY stages, autofocus, and indent recognition exist, but no peer-reviewed paper was found describing a robot-loaded hardness-testing loop for AM alloy development campaigns with measured loading success rate or cycle time.

**Archimedes density.** No peer-reviewed robotic Archimedes density workflow was located. Published AM studies universally report manual density measurements.

**Tensile specimen loading.** No peer-reviewed flexible robotic loading system for ASTM E8 sub-size tensile bars with measured success was located. Collins (2026) notes that "automated robots must be capable of handling arbitrary and widely varying complex geometries" and identifies multi-fingered, pneumatic, electro-adhesive, and soft grippers as solvable engineering problems (collins2026additivemanufacturingenabled pages 12-13).

**Part removal from build plates and sectioning.** These steps involve cutting forces, coolant, and fixturing well beyond a benchtop arm's capabilities. No alloy-development-loop paper with general robotic success metrics was located.

**PiPER feasibility:** The arm can shuttle standardized metallographic pucks and small coupons between stations but cannot supply polishing force, cutting force, or pressing force. The grinder/polisher, saw, and mounting press must operate independently.

### (4) Charge Preparation

**AlloyBot** (Selvaraj, Boonpetch et al. 2026, DOI: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-9761885/v1; **preprint**) is the most directly relevant system. It combines "a six-axis robotic arm mounted on a horizontal seventh axis, an automatic weighing module, and an automatic arc-melting module" with 12 element channels (expandable to 24) using paired pellet dispensers and wire feeders for coarse and fine mass control (selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 4-7, boonpetch2026alloybotsynthesisof pages 13-18). Stated performance targets are "one alloy per hour (>150 alloys per week)" with delivery "within three hours of a request" and "less than one hour of daily human maintenance" (boonpetch2026alloybotsynthesisof pages 8-13, selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 1-4). Compositional accuracy is "better than 1 at.%" with demonstrated agreement for Ti-6Al-4V, a refractory HEA, and a metallic glass (boonpetch2026alloybotsynthesisof pages 41-45). The system handles volumes up to 25 cm³ (>100 g Ti-6Al-4V) at approximately $100/5 cm³ driven primarily by feedstock cost (selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 1-4). No aggregate synthesis success rate was reported.

**Biomimetic solid dispensing** (Jiang et al. 2023, DOI: 10.1039/d3dd00075c, peer-reviewed) used a dual-arm ABB YuMi robot with interchangeable 3D-printed spatulas, an analytical balance, and fuzzy-logic closed-loop control. For non-challenging solids at 200 mg, "the dual-arm robot achieved an average dispensing error of 0.07% (standard deviation 0.56%)" versus −0.41% for Quantos (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10). However, the aggregate failure rate across all 13 tested solids was 23.1% for the robot, 26.9% for Quantos, and 26.5% for Chemspeed (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11). Dispensing times were substantially longer: "552, 517, 381, and 239 seconds at 1000, 500, 200, and 20 mg targets" for the robot vs. 40–285 s for Quantos (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10).

**A-Lab** (Szymanski et al. 2023, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-023-06734-w, peer-reviewed, Nature) uses "a Mitsubishi robot arm" and "an automatic Quantos dispenser balance" for precursor powder dosing, followed by mixing "with ethanol and ten 5-mm ZrO₂ balls for 9 minutes in a dual asymmetric centrifuge" and transfer into alumina crucibles (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-7). Over "17 days of continuous operation," 41 of 58 targets were synthesized, a 71% success rate (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 2-3, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 4-5).

**PiPER feasibility for charge prep:** The arm can move cups, pellets, wire segments, and small master-alloy pieces well within 1.5 kg. For loose aluminium powder, an enclosed gravimetric dispenser (not freehand pouring) is essential for both accuracy and safety.

### (5) Safety: Combustible Metal Powders and Autonomous Robotics

**No peer-reviewed publication was found that specifically addresses NFPA 484 (now NFPA 660), ATEX/IECEx, or other combustible-dust standards as applied to autonomous robotic systems handling open aluminium or other combustible metal powders.** This is a critical gap.

The most relevant SDL safety publication is Leong et al. (2024, DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv-2024-2qx28, **preprint**), which recommends the "RAMP process—recognizing hazards, assessing risks, minimizing risks, and preparing for emergencies—and the hierarchy of controls" (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 1-4). It advocates "multimodal sensing for early detection of violent bubbling, excessive heat generation, and rapid pressure buildup," constrained motion planning, emergency stops, human-in-the-loop authorization, and systematic near-miss reporting (leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 33-36, leong2025steeringtowardssafe pages 7-11). However, this paper does not mention NFPA, ATEX, combustible dust classification, dust-tight enclosures, bonding/grounding, inerting, or explosion protection.

The A-Lab deliberately restricted its campaigns to air-stable oxide and phosphate targets and operated in open air—not under inert atmosphere (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 2-3, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 1-2). AlloyBot operates under high-purity argon in its arc-melting chamber with a load-lock (selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 1-4), but the safety rationale is metallurgical (oxidation prevention) rather than explosion prevention, and the weighing module appears to operate in air.

**What NFPA 660 (formerly 484) requires in general terms** (based on the standard itself, not from a peer-reviewed paper): a combustible-dust hazard analysis for the specific material; classification of electrical equipment in areas where ignitable concentrations may exist; bonding and grounding of all conductive equipment handling powder; dust-tight enclosures or ventilation/collection for dust-generating operations; housekeeping to prevent accumulation; and explosion protection (venting, suppression, or inerting) for enclosed volumes exceeding minimum explosive concentration. Standard consumer-grade electronics, including Raspberry Pi boards and unrated motor drives, are not rated for classified areas. **No published incident report involving an autonomous metal-powder laboratory was identified**, but absence of reports is not evidence of safety.

### (6) Time-Per-Sample and Person-Hour Breakdowns

**No published time-and-motion study or person-hour breakdown was found for the specific atomization-to-characterization workflow described in the FLAIME program** (ultrasonic atomization → collection → six-fraction sieving → splitting → jarring → O/N/H sampling → SEM stubs → flowability → printing → density/hardness/metallography/tensile testing). The lab's estimate of 10–15 person-hours per atomizer run therefore remains externally unvalidated.

The closest published comparisons come from adjacent workflows. McCalla (2023, DOI: 10.1021/acsengineeringau.3c00037, peer-reviewed) reports that traditional solid-state powder synthesis yields "approximately 5–10 samples per researcher over three days" and identifies "structural characterization" (XRD at ~1 hour/sample) as "the dominant throughput bottleneck" (mccalla2023semiautomatedexperimentsto pages 3-4). Von Rohr (2024, DOI: 10.2533/chimia.2024.855, peer-reviewed) identifies both sample preparation and characterization as persistent bottlenecks and states: "if analysis remains human-operated, it can remain the principal bottleneck" (rohr2024thechanginglandscape pages 4-5). AlloyBot reduces synthesis labor to "less than one hour of daily human maintenance" for ~24 alloys/day throughput (selvaraj2026alloybotondemandsynthesis pages 1-4).

These comparisons support the intuition that post-run powder workup and characterization preparation—not the atomization run itself—dominate hands-on time.

### (7) Feasibility Assessment for the PiPER Arm (1.5 kg Payload, ~15 N, Parallel Gripper, Raspberry Pi Cameras)

The evidence from published autonomous-lab implementations consistently shows that low-payload collaborative robots are most effective as **sample couriers between purpose-built processing modules**, rather than as general-purpose manipulators replacing industrial equipment. The A-Lab uses UR5e arms (5 kg payload) exclusively for crucible and labware transport between stations (szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 7-8, szymanski2023anautonomouslaboratory pages 8-12). RASI similarly uses a UR5e to shuttle specimens between a grinder/polisher, etching station, and microscope (moschetti2025robot‐assistedautomatedserial‐sectioning pages 3-4). EMSBot uses a modified 3D printer for the same courier function (milsted2025automatedelectronmicroscopy pages 2-3).

**Steps realistically within reach of the PiPER:**
- Sealed-container logistics: moving capped jars, vials, SEM-stub trays, and metallographic pucks between stations
- Weighing operations: placing cups on balances, triggering tare/read cycles
- Identity verification: reading AprilTags, barcodes, and labels with wrist camera
- SEM stub exchange: picking/placing lightweight stubs from trays to SEM-compatible holders
- Instrument tending: loading coupons into hardness tester nests, automated saw fixtures, or other instruments with self-clamping mechanisms
- Simple coupon transport for tensile bars and density specimens

**Steps requiring industrial or dedicated equipment (beyond the PiPER):**
- Sieve-stack assembly/disassembly with loaded powder (weight, clamping force)
- Press-fitting 6063 cup plugs (>>15 N, typically tonnes)
- Part removal from LPBF build plates (cutting forces)
- Compression/hot mounting (kN forces, 150–180°C)
- Grinding and polishing (sustained contact force, abrasive environment)
- Operations in classified combustible-dust areas (the PiPER and Raspberry Pi are not rated for such environments)
