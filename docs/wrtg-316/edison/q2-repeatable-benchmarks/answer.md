## Repeatable Manipulation Benchmarks for Comparing Low-Cost and Industrial/Research Robot Arms

This report identifies published manipulation benchmarks with quantitative results obtained on industrial or research arms that could be rerun on a low-cost 6-DOF arm (such as AgileX PiPER or SO-101) equipped with a parallel gripper and RGB cameras. For each benchmark, the protocol, trial count, success criterion, measuring tool, arm model, and key quantitative result are described. The following table provides a comprehensive summary of all identified benchmarks.

### 1. ISO 9283 Pose Repeatability and Accuracy

**Protocol.** The ISO 9283:1998 standard prescribes approaching each of five target poses arranged in an ISO cube configuration 30 times from the same direction. The end-effector position is recorded at each arrival, and positional repeatability is computed as the mean three-dimensional radial deviation plus three standard deviations (RPₗ = l̄ + 3σ) (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 7-10, nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 1-4). Testing is performed unloaded and with a specified payload. Pose accuracy is the deviation between the commanded and measured mean positions.

**Published results.** On an RTA ARTIC6-1215-6kg, the contact-based unloaded test yielded 0.0260 mm repeatability over 30 cycles using three orthogonal digital dial indicators; the vision-based test with a 4 kg payload yielded 0.0406 mm using a calibrated OptiTrack multi-camera system (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 24-26). At the five ISO cube points with payload, repeatability ranged from 0.02691 mm to 0.26279 mm (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 24-26). Kirschner et al. extended the ISO 9283 framework to force-sensing and safety benchmarking on a Franka Emika Panda, UR5e, and UR10e (DOI: 10.1109/IROS51168.2021.9636329), though positional repeatability in millimeters was not separately reported in the retrieved text (kirschner2021towardsareference pages 2-3). Pollák et al. (2020) reported unidirectional pose accuracy and repeatability of the collaborative UR5 per ISO 9283 (DOI: 10.1177/1687814020972893), but the full text was unobtainable (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 44-45).

**Replication on low-cost arms.** This test requires only a rigid end-effector tool, a measuring instrument (dial indicators or an external camera system such as OptiTrack or even ArUco/AprilTag tracking with a calibrated external camera), and programmed motion to fixed targets. A low-cost arm can run the same 30-cycle protocol. **No published ISO 9283 result exists for the PiPER or SO-101.**

### 2. Box and Blocks Test (BBT)

**Protocol.** Morgan et al. (2020) adapted the clinical Box and Blocks Test into three robot protocols using the YCB apparatus (objects #68 and #69). The Modified-BBT requires transferring 16 aligned blocks over a barrier. The Targeted-BBT adds ordered placement accuracy. The Standard-BBT transfers as many randomly configured blocks as possible, with a timed (60 s) and untimed variant. Each protocol is run consecutively at least five times; a failed execution scores zero (morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 2-3, morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 4-5).

**Published results on Franka Panda.** Modified-BBT: average score 14.40, total time 247.94 s. Targeted-BBT: score 13.00, total time 307.10 s. Timed Standard-BBT: 2.80 blocks in 60 s. Untimed Standard-BBT: 7.60 blocks in 248.73 s. The Panda used an extended parallel gripper (140 mm 3D-printed fingertips) and an Intel RealSense D435i eye-in-hand sensor (morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 5-6). A Barrett WAM with a Yale Openhand T42 gripper outperformed the Panda, transferring an average of 4.8 ± 0.4 blocks in the untimed variant (morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 7-8). DOI: 10.1109/LRA.2019.2961053 (peer-reviewed, IEEE RA-L).

**Replication.** The BBT apparatus is commercially available and 3D-printable. A low-cost arm with a parallel gripper and wrist camera can directly replicate this benchmark, measuring blocks transferred and time.

### 3. NIST Assembly Task Board — Peg-in-Hole and Connector Insertion

**Protocol.** Kimble et al. (2020) defined a standardized benchmarking protocol using NIST Assembly Task Boards. Task Board 1 includes four round pegs (4, 8, 12, 16 mm diameter), four rectangular pegs, four connectors (USB, RJ45, waterproof, DSUB), gears, and fasteners. In the assembly mode, a fully disassembled board and parts kit are placed within the robot's work volume; the robot autonomously grasps each part and assembles it. Performance is evaluated using completion time, board completion percentage, and reliability (probability of successful sub-task completion). Mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval are required over trials (kimble2020benchmarkingprotocolsfor pages 6-9, kimble2020benchmarkingprotocolsfor pages 3-5). DOI: 10.1109/LRA.2020.2965869 (peer-reviewed, IEEE RA-L, 157 citations).

**Published results.** Lian et al. (2021) benchmarked off-the-shelf solutions on a UR5e using a subset of NIST Task Board 1. With a 3D-vision system (two LMI Gocator 3506 sensors), 150 trials per task were conducted: all peg tasks and most connectors achieved 100% completion except for four RJ45 failures (lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions pages 4-5). End-to-end connector insertion results: USB 100.0% / 29.8 s mean cycle time; RJ45 96.0% / 33.6 s; waterproof 98.0% / 30.1 s; DSUB 100.0% / 28.0 s (lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions pages 7-7, lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions media c9397f47). DOI: 10.1109/IROS51168.2021.9636586 (peer-reviewed, IROS 2021).

**Replication.** The NIST task board designs use standard off-the-shelf components and are publicly documented. A low-cost arm can attempt the same peg insertions and connector tasks, though tight-clearance connectors may exceed the positional accuracy of budget hardware.

### 4. Peg-in-Hole with Controlled Positional Error

**Protocol.** Van Wyk et al. (2018) defined a comparative peg-in-hole benchmark using 3D-printable pegs and holes with a dimensionless clearance of c = (D − d)/D = 0.02. Each test involves 60 attempted insertions with deliberate Gaussian positional offsets (σ = 1 mm and 2 mm). Completion time (CT) measures only the alignment-to-insertion phase, excluding peg acquisition and transport. Probability of success (PS) is calculated with 95% confidence (wyk2018comparativepeginholetesting pages 3-5). DOI: 10.1109/TRO.2018.2791591 (peer-reviewed, IEEE Transactions on Robotics, 138 citations).

**Published results.** At σ₁ = 1 mm offset: System 1 (conventional gripper) achieved PS = 85.4%, mean CT = 11.70 s; System 2 Quasi-Random (force-controlled hand) achieved PS = 95.2%, mean CT = 3.11 s. At σ₂ = 2 mm offset: System 1 achieved PS = 87.6%, mean CT = 18.31 s; System 2 Quasi-Random achieved PS = 95.2%, mean CT = 8.2 s (wyk2018comparativepeginholetesting media eff4b610, wyk2018comparativepeginholetesting media 139c342d).

**Replication.** The 3D-printable test artifacts make this highly accessible. A low-cost arm can run the same 60-insertion protocol with known offsets, measuring success and insertion time.

### 5. QBIT — Quality-Aware Benchmarking for Robotic Insertion Tasks

**Protocol.** Schempp et al. (2025) introduced QBIT, evaluating position-controlled, force-controlled, and learning-based (InsertionNet) insertion approaches on a UR5e with a six-axis force/torque sensor. Peg-in-hole scenarios used 1 mm and 0.1 mm clearances plus USB-plug insertion. Metrics include success rate, force energy, force smoothness, and completion time, calculated from 100 repetitions per approach (schempp2025qbitqualityawarecloudbased pages 4-5). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2503.07479 (**preprint**, arXiv 2025).

**Replication.** The protocol requires a force/torque sensor for full quality metrics, but success rate and completion time alone can be measured with a low-cost arm and video scoring.

### 6. YCB Object Pick-and-Place — SceneReplica

**Protocol.** Khargonkar et al. (2024) created 20 reproducible tabletop scenes, each containing five objects from a set of 16 YCB objects. The robot attempts grasp, lift, rotate, transport, and drop-off. Both grasping success and complete pick-and-place success are scored separately over 100 object attempts per pipeline (khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 9-11, khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 3-5).

**Published results on Fetch robot.** Success rates ranged from 36% to 66% depending on the perception and grasp-planning pipeline. The best non-oracle result was GDRNPP + GraspIt! top-down grasping at 66/100 (66%). Ground-truth-pose variants reached 78–82/100 (khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 9-11, khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 6-7, khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 5-6). DOI: 10.1109/ICRA57147.2024.10610180 (peer-reviewed, ICRA 2024).

**Replication.** The YCB object set is commercially available. Scene layouts are specified with stencils. A low-cost arm with an RGB camera can replicate this, substituting its own perception pipeline.

### 7. ACRV Picking Benchmark

**Protocol.** Leitner et al. (2017) designed a reproducible shelf-picking benchmark with 42 common objects, a widely available shelf, and exact arrangement stencils. Four setups of increasing difficulty are each run three consecutive times. Success rate = successful picks / attempted picks (leitner2017theacrvpicking pages 7-7). DOI: 10.1109/ICRA.2017.7989545 (peer-reviewed, ICRA 2017, 110 citations).

**Published results on Baxter.** Setup 1: 75% (9/12); Setup 2: 33.33% (4/12); Setup 3: 8.33% (1/12); Setup 4: 0% (0/18) (leitner2017theacrvpicking pages 7-7).

**Replication.** The shelf and object arrangement are reproducible by design. A parallel gripper can substitute for the vacuum end-effector used on Baxter, though success rates will differ.

### 8. FMB — Functional Manipulation Benchmark

**Protocol.** Luo et al. (2024) proposed FMB using a Franka Panda with torque control. It evaluates grasping (5 trials × 10 objects = 50), single-object long-horizon manipulation (30 trials), and multi-object assembly (10 trials) using 66 reproducible 3D-printed objects. Observations include multi-camera RGB-D, kinematics, and force/torque (luo2025fmbafunctional pages 5-7, luo2025fmbafunctional pages 9-11).

**Published results.** Grasping: Transformer policy 28/50 (56%), ResNet 27/50 (54%). Single-object assembly: best 19/30. Multi-object assembly: best 7/10 (luo2025fmbafunctional pages 14-15, luo2025fmbafunctional pages 9-11). DOI: 10.1177/02783649241276017 (peer-reviewed, IJRR 2024, 161 citations).

**Replication.** The 3D-printed objects and CAD files are publicly available. A low-cost arm can attempt the grasping and single-object assembly tasks; multi-object assembly may require force sensing not available on budget platforms.

### 9. FurnitureBench

**Protocol.** Heo et al. (2025) evaluated long-horizon furniture assembly on a Franka Panda with the default parallel gripper and three Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D cameras. Eight 3D-printable furniture models test grasping, insertion, screwing, and pushing. Individual skills are evaluated over 10 trials under low/medium initialization randomness; termination occurs on completion, 5 s inactivity, unsafe motion, or step limits (heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 3-4, heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 6-7). DOI: 10.1177/02783649241304789 (peer-reviewed, IJRR 2025, 241 citations).

**Published results.** Individual skill success rates vary by part and randomness. Full-assembly learning methods averaged at most 2 of 12 subtasks. Rebuilt environments achieved 84% of original one-leg-task phase performance (heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 3-4, heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 1-2).

**Replication.** The 3D-printable models are available. Single-skill tasks (grasp, insert) are feasible on low-cost arms, though full assembly may exceed their capabilities.

### 10. Low-Cost Arm Benchmarks: SO-100/SO-101 with LeRobot

**SmolVLA on SO-100.** Shukor et al. (2025) evaluated ACT, SmolVLA, and π0 on three SO-100 tasks (pick-place, stacking, sorting) using 50 demonstrations per task. ACT achieved 70%/50%/25% (mean 48.3%); SmolVLA achieved 75%/90%/70% (mean 78.3%); π0 achieved 100%/40%/45% (mean 61.7%) (shukor2025smolvlaavisionlanguageaction pages 10-12, shukor2025smolvlaavisionlanguageaction pages 12-14). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2506.01844 (**preprint**, arXiv 2025, 655 citations).

**Cowling SO-101 scaling study.** Cowling (2026) compared ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA, and π0.5 on an SO-101 green-ball pick-and-place task with two distractors, using nested demonstration sets of 12–200. Each evaluation block ran 10 fixed placements with a 30 s timeout. Over 1,340 scored episodes, ACT improved from 1/40 at 12 demonstrations to 37/40 at 100; π0.5 achieved 16/40 at 12 and ~70% at higher counts; SmolVLA and Diffusion Policy peaked near 50% (cowling2026from12to pages 1-3, cowling2026from12to pages 7-9, cowling2026from12to pages 3-5). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2610.37170 (**preprint**, arXiv 2026).

**SO-101 Bench.** Hickok (2026) defined a language-conditioned spatial manipulation benchmark with Bin, Next-to, Between, and Move tasks across 916 simulated episodes and 60 paired physical episodes. Learned policies ranged from 8% to 60.8%; the best agent+grasp combination reached 83.2% in simulation (hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 13-16, hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 2-4, hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 1-2). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2610.36420 (**preprint**, arXiv 2026).

### 11. Laboratory Manipulation Tasks

**MATTERIX.** Darvish et al. (2025) used a Franka Research 3 for lab tasks: beaker pick-and-place achieved 9/12 (75%) success; liquid pouring achieved 9/10 (90%) with 59.6 ± 7.9 mL transferred (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 11-14). DOI: 10.1038/s43588-025-00924-4 (peer-reviewed, Nature Computational Science 2025).

**Mobile robot rack handling.** Burger (2023) reported 464/464 (100%) rack transfers using a KUKA LBR iiwa arm with a Festo parallel gripper over 71 iterations in a 2-hour robustness test (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 62-69). DOI: 10.17638/03087073.

**ABB YuMi vial handling.** Manna et al. (2026) used an ABB YuMi IRB 14000 for end-to-end Drosophila vial handling, achieving 93.85% retention of flies fit for analysis across 1,024 assays versus 83.33% for a human (manna2026endtoendautomationof pages 1-3, manna2026endtoendautomationof pages 3-5). DOI: 10.64898/2026.10.20.758951 (**preprint**, bioRxiv 2026).

**No published evidence was found for a standardized, repeatable vial-into-rack insertion benchmark with trial-level success rates on any industrial arm.** This remains a gap in the literature.

### Summary and Gaps

The benchmarks most immediately replicable on a low-cost arm with a parallel gripper and RGB cameras are: (1) ISO 9283-style pose repeatability using AprilTag tracking in lieu of an OptiTrack system; (2) the Box and Blocks Test with the YCB apparatus; (3) YCB object pick-and-place following SceneReplica scenes; (4) NIST task board peg insertions (especially the larger pegs); (5) SO-100/SO-101 LeRobot tasks (pick-place, stacking, sorting), which are already native to the user's platform; and (6) the Van Wyk peg-in-hole protocol with 3D-printed artifacts.

**Explicit gaps.** No peer-reviewed ISO 9283 positional repeatability values were found for the AgileX PiPER or SO-101. No standardized vial-into-rack benchmark with published trial-level success rates exists. The QBIT and FMB force-based quality metrics require a force/torque sensor not present on budget arms. The Kinova Gen3 and ABB YuMi lack published results on the NIST task board or BBT benchmarks in the retrieved literature, limiting direct cross-arm comparisons for those specific platforms.
