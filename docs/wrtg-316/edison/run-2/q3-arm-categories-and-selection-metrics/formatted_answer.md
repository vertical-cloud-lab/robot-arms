### 1.1 Mobile Manipulators
The highest-profile SDL deployments use mobile manipulators that navigate between laboratory stations. The KUKA KMR iiwa, combining an omnidirectional base with a 7-DOF touch-sensitive LBR iiwa arm (14 kg payload, 0.1 mm repeatability), was used by Burger et al. in the seminal "mobile robotic chemist" that operated autonomously for over 21 hours per day, performing photocatalysis optimization across multiple stations (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52). Lower-cost alternatives combining a MiR mobile base with a UR5 or Franka Panda arm were noted as producing "similar dexterous mobile-robot functionality at lower purchase cost" (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52).

### 1.2 Collaborative Arms (Cobots)
Stationary collaborative arms dominate SDL implementations. The Franka Emika Panda (8-DOF with gripper, 0.1 mm repeatability) served as a station-level manipulator in MATTERIX, performing beaker placement and liquid-dispensing station setup (darvish2025matterixtowarda pages 22-24). The UR5e is widely used in liquid-handling environments such as the MIR200-based SDL for polymer electronics (licardo2024intelligentrobotics—asystematic pages 24-25). The ABB YuMi (7-DOF per arm, 0.02 mm vendor repeatability) is described as a dual-arm cobot with "human-like dexterity, precision, safe operation alongside humans, and suitability for intricate small-parts assembly" (licardo2024intelligentrobotics—asystematic pages 24-25). Ferraguti et al. provided a task-oriented comparative methodology benchmarking the KUKA LWR 4+, UR5, and Panda on payload, repeatability, programming effort, hand-guiding force, collision safety, and usability (ferraguti2019amethodologyfor pages 6-6).

### 1.3 Dual-Arm Systems
The Yaskawa MOTOMAN CSDA10F dual-arm robot (7-DOF per arm, ±0.1 mm repeatability, 10 kg payload per arm) was used to automate silica nanoparticle synthesis, demonstrating that "the use of the robot not only increased the synthesis accuracy and reproducibility but reduced the personnel time and costs up to 75%" (dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 6-7, dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 3-6). The Yaskawa SDA10F was similarly deployed for complex sample preparation with analytical instruments (fleischer2016applicationofa pages 3-5, joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 25-28).

### 1.4 SCARA and Low-Cost Arms
SCARA robots such as the Dobot M1 Pro have been evaluated for microplate handling, with an emphasis that "affordable robot arms such as the Dobot Magician and myPalletizer were priced at approximately USD 1,000" (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 15-20). Rupp et al. demonstrated that a low-cost 4-axis Dobot Magician could automate pipetting, autosampling, and bacterial inoculation with no significant difference from trained researchers (rupp2022establishmentoflowcost pages 4-5, rupp2022establishmentoflowcost pages 1-2).

### 1.5 Cartesian/Gantry Systems
Gantry-based systems (OpenTrons OT-2, SPT LabTech Mosquito) are described as "reliable and scalable to laboratory size" for liquid handling, though they lack the anthropomorphic flexibility of articulated arms (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52, tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6).

### 1.6 Open-Source and Ultra-Low-Cost Arms
The SO-100/SO-101 leader-follower arms (~$200 build cost) appear extensively in imitation-learning research using the LeRobot framework but have not yet been validated in published SDL workflows. **No peer-reviewed paper was found deploying AgileX PiPER or SO-101 arms in a materials-science SDL context.** This represents a clear gap in the literature.

## 2. Selection Metrics and Criteria

### 2.1 Mechanical Performance Metrics
Published selection criteria for laboratory arms span: repeatability, accuracy, payload, reach, degrees of freedom, speed/cycle time, footprint, and gripper compatibility (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52, ferraguti2019amethodologyfor pages 6-6, harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 15-20). Burger explicitly evaluated whether the arm could "transport and manipulate vials and racks, handle powder cartridges, load cartridges into the solid-dispensing system, and perform these operations robustly over hundreds of experiments" (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52).

### 2.2 ADePT Framework
The most comprehensive published framework for assessing autonomous laboratory robots is ADePT (Adaptability and Learning, Dexterity, Perception, Task Complexity), introduced by Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia (2026). It provides "a systematic and quantitative framework for assessing the capabilities of autonomous laboratory robots" through four scored dimensions: adaptability (generalizing across tasks, learning from experience), dexterity (contact-rich manipulation, force/tactile sensing), perception (multimodal sensing, transparent glassware detection), and task complexity (multi-step protocols, conditional decisions) (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 7-9, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 4-5, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 2-3).

### 2.3 Cost Categories
Tom et al. (2024), in the most cited SDL review (Chemical Reviews, 1222 citations), categorize hardware cost as: "approximately $1 million" for special-purpose commercial hardware, "approximately $10,000" for general-purpose commercial hardware (with additional software development), and "approximately $100–$2,000" for community-developed open hardware (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). The OSCAR platform reports a total cost of approximately $35,000 USD including a UR3 arm at an academic price of CAD 25,000 (~USD 18,750) (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 8-9, pivin2026oscaramodular pages 9-10). **No published, systematic cost-per-task analysis comparing affordable and industry-standard arms was found; this is a significant gap.**

### 2.4 Safety
Ferraguti et al. applied ISO 10218-1/2 and ISO/TS 15066 collaborative-robot safety standards when benchmarking the KUKA LWR 4+, UR5, and Panda, measuring quasi-static and transient impact forces and comparing them against biomechanical limits (ferraguti2019amethodologyfor pages 6-6).

## 3. Standards and Authorities

### 3.1 ISO 9283:1998 — Robot Performance Testing
ISO 9283 is the foundational standard for manipulating-robot performance criteria. The protocol specifies 30 measurement cycles at each of 5 positions arranged in an ISO cube, with the TCP approaching from one direction, under defined payload, speed, and environmental conditions (shinde2022theperformanceparameter pages 3-8, nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 7-10). Repeatability is calculated as the mean radial deviation from the barycenter plus three standard deviations. Accuracy is the displacement between the commanded position and the barycenter of attained positions. The standard specifies using external measurement systems; implementations have used five-probe LVDT arrays (1 μm resolution) (shinde2022theperformanceparameter pages 3-8), three-axis dial indicators (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 7-10, nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 4-7), calibrated OptiTrack multi-camera vision systems (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 13-19, nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 24-26), and laser trackers (lattanzi2020geometricalcalibrationof pages 11-15).

### 3.2 Manufacturer vs. Independent Measurements
A critical finding is that manufacturer specifications diverge significantly from independently measured values. Chajón et al. (2026) tested a UR3 and found that the manufacturer's ISO 9283 repeatability specification of 0.03 mm contrasted with their photogrammetric camera-measured repeatability of 0.147–0.229 mm in the planar workspace, while a dial-indicator reference measurement yielded 0.0109 mm (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 8-10). The photogrammetric values were "substantially higher than the UR3 manufacturer's ISO 9283 repeatability specification," demonstrating that measurement-chain uncertainty and environmental effects contribute to the gap (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 10-11). Harazono et al. (2024, **bioRxiv preprint**) similarly emphasized that "manufacturer repeatability specifications, including the ISO 9283 approach-based method, may not predict real laboratory performance because they generally test the arm without holding an object" (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 1-6).

### 3.3 ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 — Safety
These standards govern collaborative robot safety and are applied in Ferraguti et al.'s comparative methodology, which measured impact forces during constrained and unconstrained collisions and verified compliance with biomechanical limits (ferraguti2019amethodologyfor pages 6-6).

### 3.4 Key Authorities and Organizations
The leading research groups producing validated SDL robotics include the Cooper group (Liverpool; Burger et al. mobile robotic chemist, Dai et al. autonomous mobile robots, Fakhruldeen et al. ARChemist, Lunt et al. multi-robot integration), the Aspuru-Guzik group (Toronto; MATTERIX, CLAIRify, Tom et al. comprehensive review), and the Thurow group (Rostock; dual-arm laboratory automation with Yaskawa/Stäubli). NIST has contributed to advancing measurement science for assessing robot positional accuracy (shinde2022theperformanceparameter pages 3-8). Standards bodies include ISO (TC 299 for robot standards), and laboratory-specific organizations SiLA and SLAS set interoperability and labware standards.

### 4.1 Low-Cost Photogrammetric Repeatability Test
Of particular relevance for affordable arms, Chajón et al. (2026) demonstrated a Raspberry Pi 4 camera with ArUco markers as a cost-effective method for characterizing robot positioning. Using a compact CharUco board for calibration (RMS reprojection error ~1 pixel), they achieved sub-millimeter accuracy and repeatability measurements following selected ISO 9283 elements, with 30 repetitions at 5 poses (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 6-8, chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 1-3). The two-ArUco-marker configuration produced the lowest mean deviation of 0.290 mm (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 8-10). This test is directly applicable to low-cost arms with wrist cameras and AprilTag-style fiducials.

### 4.2 Station Calibration Test
Burger's 6-point calibration protocol using a 100 × 100 × 50 mm physical cube achieved 0.12 mm translation accuracy and <0.005° orientation error across 100 independent calibrations with ±15 mm initial position noise (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). This directly validates the concept of recalibrating at each station using a known fixture—the exact workflow described for the user's AprilTag-based approach.

### 4.3 Vial Manipulation Endurance
The 29-iteration, 464/464 vial-grasp-and-place success rate test over 2+ hours provides a reproducible endurance benchmark (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73). An equivalent test for an affordable arm would involve repeated pick-and-place of standard laboratory consumables (vials, microplates) over extended periods with logged success/failure at each stage.

### 4.4 Automated-vs-Manual Synthesis Comparison
Dembski et al.'s MOTOMAN CSDA10F benchmark comparing automated and manual nanoparticle synthesis demonstrated that "automated dosing showed significantly lower setpoint deviations than dosing by three laboratory technicians" and produced more consistent silica nanoparticles (206.7 ± 12.0 nm, PDI 5.8%), with personnel costs reduced by up to 75% (dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 6-7, dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 3-6).

## 5. Imitation Learning and Leader-Follower Approaches

The SO-100/SO-101 arms, used in the user's setup, appear in several recent imitation-learning benchmarks. ArmnetBench v0.1 provides a parallel real-world evaluation benchmark for manipulation policies on low-cost arm farms, with 50 teleoperated demonstrations per task released in LeRobot v3.0 (Selvaraj et al., 2026, **preprint**). RIO uses SO-100 arms for bimanual coordination evaluation (Ortega-Kral et al., 2026, **preprint**). SEVO collects teleoperated episodes via "leader–follower control" using SO-100 arms with wrist cameras (Fang et al., 2026, **preprint**). RoboMIND includes 107k demonstration trajectories across Franka Panda, UR5e, and AgileX dual-arm robots (Wu et al., 2024, **preprint**). **However, none of these studies apply imitation learning to SDL-specific tasks (pipetting, weighing, sample transfer, instrument interaction). This represents a clear gap where the user's work would be novel.**

## 6. Explicit Gaps Where No Published Evidence Exists

1. **AgileX PiPER and SO-101 in SDLs**: No peer-reviewed publication was found deploying these specific arms in a materials-science or chemistry SDL context.

2. **Cost-per-task analysis**: No formal, standardized cost-per-task metric comparing affordable and industry-standard arms in laboratory automation has been published. Cost comparisons are limited to aggregate personnel-time reductions (e.g., 75% in Dembski et al.) and broad hardware cost categories (Tom et al.).

3. **Imitation learning for SDL tasks**: While imitation learning using low-cost leader-follower arms is well studied for household manipulation, no published work applies this approach to SDL-specific tasks such as pipetting, powder dosing, or instrument interaction.

4. **AprilTag-based station recalibration in SDLs**: While Burger's 6-point touch calibration is well documented, the specific use of vision-based AprilTag fiducials for station-to-station recalibration in an SDL has not been systematically validated in the peer-reviewed literature, though the construction-robotics literature demonstrates the approach with ArUco markers achieving sub-millimeter accuracy (wilcock2026fusionoffiducial pages 18-19).

5. **Standardized benchmarks for laboratory arms**: The ADePT framework provides a conceptual scoring system but acknowledges that "detailed scoring schemes are located in supplementary materials" and does not define a standardized physical test suite with fixed tasks, objects, and pass/fail criteria (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 13-13). No NIST- or SLAS-endorsed benchmark test suite for laboratory robot arms currently exists.

## 7. Summary and Recommendations

For validating an affordable arm (AgileX PiPER at ~$2K, SO-101 at ~$200) against industry-standard SDL arms, the literature supports a tiered testing approach:

**Tier 1 — Mechanical characterization**: Apply the ISO 9283 protocol (30 cycles × 5 poses) using either the low-cost photogrammetric method of Chajón et al. with a Raspberry Pi camera and ArUco markers, or three-axis dial indicators (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 6-8, chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 8-10). Report both unloaded and loaded repeatability to address the gap identified by Harazono et al. (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 1-6).

**Tier 2 — Calibration accuracy**: Replicate Burger's 6-point calibration cube test with 100+ trials and injected position noise to characterize station-finding accuracy under realistic conditions (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).

**Tier 3 — Task-level benchmarks**: Conduct microplate transfer tests (125+ trials per offset condition), vial manipulation endurance (29+ iterations), and pipetting accuracy comparisons against a trained operator (rupp2022establishmentoflowcost pages 4-5, harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 15-20, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73).

**Tier 4 — Full workflow**: Run complete multi-station SDL experiments and report success rate, failure modes, unattended duration, and intervention count, following the ARChemist approach (fakhruldeen2022archemistautonomousrobotic pages 6-7).

**Tier 5 — Capability scoring**: Apply the ADePT framework to score adaptability, dexterity, perception, and task complexity, enabling cross-study comparison with published systems (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13).

**Tier 6 — Economics**: Track personnel time, consumables, maintenance, and total operating cost to establish the first published cost-per-task figures for affordable SDL arms, addressing a clear gap in the literature (dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 6-7, tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6).
