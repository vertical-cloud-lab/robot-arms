# Independently Measured Performance Data for Low-Cost vs. Industrial Robot Arms in Self-Driving Laboratory Contexts

## 1. Overview and Key Finding

A systematic search of peer-reviewed journals, major conference proceedings (ICRA, IROS, ICLR), and preprints from 2015–2026 reveals a **critical gap**: no published study has subjected any of the low-cost arms commonly discussed for SDL use—AgileX PiPER, SO-100/SO-101, Koch v1.1, or WidowX—to an independent, ISO 9283-style metrological characterization of repeatability, accuracy, backlash, or endurance. All repeatability and accuracy figures available for these arms are manufacturer datasheet values or informal observations embedded in systems papers, not independently verified measurements. By contrast, industrial collaborative arms—particularly the Universal Robots UR5—have been tested under the ISO 9283 standard in peer-reviewed studies that fully confirm the manufacturers' claims. The following sections detail what is known, what has been measured, and what protocols exist for anyone seeking to close this gap.

## 2. Low-Cost Arm Specifications and Evidence

### 2.1 AgileX PiPER

The AgileX PiPER is a 6-DOF lightweight arm (4.2 kg) with a manufacturer-specified effective working radius of 626.75 mm and a payload of 1.5 kg. Wojtkowski (2026) states in a thesis: "the repeatability of the arm is within ±0.1 mm, which means that if the arm were to repeat the same motion, its deviation would be incredibly small" and notes the gripper "is specified to open up to 70mm with and accuracy of ±0.5 mm, and has a rated clamping force of 40 N" (pqac-00000005). These values are explicitly reproduced from the manufacturer's documentation; the thesis did not independently measure them. Wojtkowski performed pick-and-place experiments using MoveIt 2 with ROS 2 and evaluated nominal operation, grasp failure, and planner failure scenarios, but reports no ISO 9283 measurement or quantitative repeatability test (pqac-00000003, pqac-00000005). The PiPER costs approximately $2,000 based on project estimates. **No peer-reviewed independent metrological characterization of this arm exists.**

### 2.2 SO-100 / SO-101

The SO-101 is a 3D-printed, open-source arm driven by six Feetech serial-bus servos. Hickok (2026, preprint) reports: "a follower arm costs about $120–150 in parts" against "roughly $4,900 for one ALOHA arm" (pqac-00000010). The LeRobot paper (Cadène et al., ICLR 2026) lists the SO-100/101 manipulator pair at approximately $225 ($550 with parenthetical configuration), and notes that "low-cost, open-source robots like SO-10X and ALOHA cost a fraction of proprietary industrial arms" (pqac-00000024, pqac-00000038). ArmnetBench prices a single-arm SO-101 cell at approximately $359 including cameras, Raspberry Pi, and smart plug, and a bimanual cell at $477 (pqac-00000026).

Critically, **no paper reports any ISO 9283, dial-indicator, or laser-tracker measurement of SO-101 repeatability or accuracy**. What the literature does report is extensive task-level benchmarking. Hickok (2026, preprint) notes that "servo offsets differ between arms" and that "one arm farm reports a misaligned camera and objects that wore down during evaluation" (pqac-00000010), indicating non-trivial variability across nominally identical builds. ArmnetBench (Selvaraj et al., 2026, preprint) collected 2,518 policy rollouts across a managed fleet of SO-101 cells, noting that "whether absolute rates agree across nominally identical cells remains untested" (pqac-00000025).

### 2.3 Koch v1.1

LeRobot lists the Koch v1.1 bimanual manipulator at approximately $670 ($1,346 for an extended configuration) (pqac-00000038). ManiSkill3 (Tao et al., 2024, preprint) describes it as a "low-cost $300 Koch v1.1 robot arm." **No published study has independently measured the Koch v1.1's repeatability, accuracy, backlash, payload, or endurance.**

### 2.4 WidowX (Interbotix)

REPLAB (Yang et al., ICRA 2019) built a complete benchmarking cell around a WidowX arm at approximately $2,000, noting that "a single REPLAB cell costs about 2000 USD" (pqac-00000007). The WidowX has 6-DOF with a 1-DOF parallel-jaw gripper (1–3 cm opening). REPLAB reports that after control-noise compensation, "the end-effector positions are within 2 cm of the target over the 35 x 40 cm workspace floor, and usually smaller near the center" (pqac-00000007). This is a **system-level positioning figure** (arm + camera + controller), not an ISO-style arm repeatability measurement. Regarding endurance, Yang et al. state: "REPLAB cells proved to be quite robust. With software constraints in place to avoid arm collisions with the boundaries of the REPLAB cell, we encountered no major breakages over more than 100,000 grasp attempts. No servos needed to be replaced. Repair maintenance work was largely limited to occasional tightening of screws and replacing frayed cables" (pqac-00000007). **No ISO 9283 or equivalent independent arm-level repeatability measurement exists for the WidowX.**

## 3. Industrial Arm Specifications and Independent Measurements

### 3.1 Universal Robots UR5

Pollák et al. (2020, peer-reviewed journal) performed the most detailed independent ISO 9283 measurement found in this review. The protocol used five measurement points (P1–P5) arranged on the diagonals of an ISO cube placed in the robot's workspace. The robot's TCP was moved to each point in 30 measurement cycles at three speed settings (10%, 50%, 100% of rated speed) and two load conditions (50% and 100% of the 5 kg payload). Position was measured using six Heidenhain MT 25/MT 12 linear incremental displacement sensors (stated accuracy: 0.004 mm) with six VRZ 401 evaluation units (pqac-00000017).

Key results: At 100% load and 10% speed, the measured unidirectional pose accuracy was AP = 0.02223 mm and repeatability RP = 0.03292 mm (pqac-00000031). At 100% load and 50% speed, AP = 0.02517 mm (pqac-00000031). At 100% load and 100% speed, the calculated RP = 0.05325 mm (pqac-00000033). Across all conditions and measurement points (Table 10 in the original), RP values ranged from 0.015 mm to 0.075 mm—all below the manufacturer's specified ±0.1 mm. The authors concluded: "the robot's repeatability in the observed workspace is identical with the repeatability specified by the manufacturer" and "the robot COMPLIES" (pqac-00000034). The manufacturer does not state unidirectional pose accuracy in the UR5 catalogue; only pose repeatability (±0.1 mm) is specified (pqac-00000032).

### 3.2 Universal Robots UR3

Chajón et al. (2026, peer-reviewed journal) proposed a low-cost photogrammetric characterization method using a Raspberry Pi 4 camera, ArUco markers, and OpenCV, following selected elements of ISO 9283:1998, with 30 repetitions at five target poses (pqac-00000035). The UR3 manufacturer specifies a positioning repeatability of 0.03 mm per ISO 9283:1998. An independent dial-indicator reference measurement yielded "a mean deviation of 0.0109 mm" (pqac-00000036). The camera-based measurement yielded positioning repeatability of RPp = 0.147–0.229 mm for linear approaches and 0.195–0.265 mm for axial approaches (pqac-00000036). The authors note that "the repeatability deviations obtained through the photogrammetric measurement are higher than the manufacturer's specification and the independent dial-indicator reference measurements" but attribute this to "the combined effect of the robot's actual positioning behavior and the uncertainty of the external photogrammetric measurement chain" (pqac-00000037). This paper is particularly relevant because it describes a **low-cost, repeatable measurement method** (Raspberry Pi camera + ArUco markers) that anyone with an affordable arm could replicate.

### 3.3 UR5e in SDL Context

Sulaiman et al. (2026, peer-reviewed journal) describe a mobile manipulator for a self-driving chemistry laboratory built on a Clearpath Ridgeback platform with a UR5e arm and Robotiq Hand-E gripper. They report that "the UR5e arm provides 6 degrees of freedom (DOF), high positional accuracy, and integrated force-torque sensing, making it suitable for delicate manipulation tasks" (pqac-00000022). However, **no ISO-style arm-level measurement was reported**; the study focused on kinematic modeling, vision-based pose estimation, and grasping success rates.

### 3.4 Franka Emika Panda, ABB YuMi, Kinova, KUKA

The Franka Panda is frequently mentioned as the high-end comparison platform for low-cost arms. LeRobot characterizes SO-10X and ALOHA as costing "a fraction of closed-source, industrial-grade robots such as the Franka Emika Panda arm" (pqac-00000024). However, **no peer-reviewed study in the reviewed SDL or robot-learning literature independently measures Panda repeatability or accuracy**. The Franka Panda manufacturer specifies ±0.1 mm repeatability (datasheet), but this was not independently verified in any paper found in this search. Similarly, no independently measured repeatability, accuracy, or endurance data for ABB YuMi, Kinova, or KUKA arms used specifically in SDL contexts were found in the literature reviewed, though these arms appear in SDL descriptions (e.g., the Cooper group's mobile robotic chemist uses a KUKA-based system).

### 5.1 ISO 9283 Repeatability Measurement (Pollák et al., 2020)

**Protocol**: Place an imaginary ISO cube in the robot's workspace. Define five measurement points (P1–P5) on the cube diagonals. The robot TCP approaches each point from one direction, 30 times per condition. External sensors (linear incremental sensors with 0.004 mm accuracy) record the achieved position. Compute unidirectional pose accuracy AP (deviation of barycentre from programmed position) and repeatability RP (radius of ball enclosing attained positions at ±3σ). Test at 100% speed with the rated payload. Optionally repeat at 50% and 10% speed, and at 50% payload. Compare measured RP with the manufacturer's specification (pqac-00000015, pqac-00000017).

### 5.2 Photogrammetric Characterization (Chajón et al., 2026)

**Protocol**: Mount a universal 3D-printed tool attachment on the robot. Attach a known-size ArUco marker (15 mm edge). Use a Raspberry Pi 4 camera at a fixed position, calibrated with a CharUco board (RMS reprojection error ~1 px). Command the robot to 5 planar poses, 30 repetitions each, using linear or axial motion strategies. Compute planar accuracy APp and repeatability RPp following ISO 9283 equations. This method is explicitly designed for "self-built and resource-constrained systems" and costs under $100 in measurement equipment (pqac-00000035, pqac-00000036).

### 5.3 REPLAB Grasping Benchmark (Yang et al., ICRA 2019)

**Protocol**: 20 objects scattered on a 35×40 cm workspace floor. Each episode: 60 grasp attempts. Object must remain in gripper while arm moves to a standard pose and holds for 2 seconds. Report cumulative success rate (CSR) curves. Cross-cell reproducibility is tested by running the same grasping baseline on two independently built cells and comparing CSR curves. Over 50,000 training grasps were collected. Total cost per cell: ~$2,000 (pqac-00000006, pqac-00000007, pqac-00000008).

### 5.4 SO-101 Bench (Hickok, 2026, preprint)

**Protocol**: Digital twin of SO-101 in Isaac Lab. Four language-conditioned tasks (bin, next-to, between, move) scored as five task families. 138 objects in three generalization splits (seen, unseen-instance, unseen-class). 916 evaluation episodes. Each learned policy fine-tuned on 3,629 simulated demonstrations under equal 36h×4×A100 compute budget. Automatic scoring from simulator state with geometric thresholds that must hold continuously for 5 seconds. Sim-to-real ranking validated with Spearman ρ = 1.00 across five policies run on the physical arm (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000013).

### 5.5 ArmnetBench v0.1 (Selvaraj et al., 2026, preprint)

**Protocol**: Managed fleet of SO-101 cells. 12 tasks (8 single-arm, 4 bimanual). 7 policies each trained on 50 demonstrations per task. 30 rollouts per task-policy pair. Three-way human scoring (successful/suboptimal/failure). Active operator time per rollout ~10 seconds. Per-cell cost: $359 (single-arm) or $477 (bimanual) (pqac-00000025, pqac-00000026).

## 6. Where No Published Evidence Exists

The following specific comparisons have **no published evidence** in the literature reviewed:

1. **Independent ISO 9283 repeatability/accuracy measurements for any low-cost arm** (AgileX PiPER, SO-100/101, Koch v1.1, WidowX). All available numbers are manufacturer datasheets or system-level observations.
2. **Backlash measurements** for any arm in this review, low-cost or industrial, in the SDL context.
3. **Endurance and failure-rate data** beyond the REPLAB observation of >100,000 grasp attempts without servo replacement on the WidowX (pqac-00000007). No systematic mean-time-between-failures (MTBF) or accelerated-life testing has been published for any of these arms.
4. **Speed benchmarks** (cycle time, maximum TCP velocity under load) compared head-to-head between low-cost and industrial arms in lab tasks.
5. **Cost-per-task metrics**: No study computes a cost-per-task figure normalizing arm price against task throughput or success rate. The price data exist (Table 1), and task success rates exist (ArmnetBench, SO-101 Bench), but no published work combines them.
6. **Independent metrological data for Franka Panda, ABB YuMi, or Kinova** in the specific SDL context.

## 7. Implications for Validating Affordable Arms

For researchers seeking to place a low-cost arm (such as the AgileX PiPER or SO-101) in the published literature and validate it against industry-standard SDL arms, the following tests can be performed using published protocols:

**Arm-level metrology**: The Chajón et al. (2026) photogrammetric method requires only a Raspberry Pi 4, an ArUco marker, and OpenCV—hardware already present in a typical low-cost SDL setup. Following ISO 9283 elements (30 repetitions at 5 poses, linear and axial approaches), this method can produce repeatability and accuracy values directly comparable with the UR3 (0.03 mm spec) and UR5 (±0.1 mm spec) results in the literature (pqac-00000035, pqac-00000036).

**Task-level benchmarking**: The REPLAB, SO-101 Bench, and ArmnetBench protocols provide well-defined task suites with published baselines. SO-101 Bench and ArmnetBench are directly applicable to SO-101 arms with no hardware modification (pqac-00000010, pqac-00000025). For other arm types (e.g., PiPER), adapting the REPLAB grasping protocol (~$2,000 cell, 60-attempt episodes, CSR metric) would provide comparable data (pqac-00000006).

**Cost-per-task**: Given published arm prices (SO-101 follower: ~$150; PiPER: ~$2,000; UR5: ~$25,000–35,000; Franka Panda: ~$30,000–50,000) and task success rates from the benchmarks above, a cost-per-successful-task metric can be computed, but this has not yet been done in the literature (pqac-00000038).

## 8. Summary

The literature on robot arms for self-driving laboratories reveals a two-tier landscape. Industrial arms (UR5, UR5e, UR3) have manufacturer-specified repeatability in the range of 0.03–0.1 mm, and at least one (UR5) has been independently confirmed at RP = 0.015–0.075 mm across conditions via a full ISO 9283 protocol (pqac-00000031, pqac-00000033). Low-cost arms (SO-101 at ~$150, PiPER at ~$2,000, Koch at ~$670, WidowX at ~$2,000) have been extensively used in robot-learning benchmarks and SDL prototypes, but **none has been subjected to an independent metrological characterization**. The PiPER claims ±0.1 mm repeatability on its datasheet (pqac-00000005), which if confirmed would place it at the same specification as the UR5, at approximately 1/15th the cost—but this claim remains unverified. For the SO-101, even the manufacturer does not publish a repeatability specification, and inter-unit variability (servo offsets, build quality) is explicitly noted in the literature (pqac-00000010). The photogrammetric method of Chajón et al. (2026) offers a directly applicable, low-cost test protocol (Raspberry Pi + ArUco markers) that any lab with these affordable arms can use to generate the missing independent metrological data (pqac-00000035).
