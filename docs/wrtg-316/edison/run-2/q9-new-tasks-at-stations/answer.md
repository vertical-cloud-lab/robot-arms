# Tests and Benchmarks for Robot Arms as Automated Manual Manipulators in Self-Driving Laboratories: A Literature Review

## 1. Robot Arms Performing Multiple Hands-On Tasks at Different Stations

The most comprehensive demonstration of a single arm taking over several hands-on laboratory tasks at different instruments is the mobile robotic chemist by Burger et al. (2020), which used a KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF arm on a KUKA Mobile Robot (KMR) platform. Over eight days it autonomously performed 688 experiments across multiple stations encompassing weighing and dispensing insoluble solids, dispensing liquid solutions, transporting sample vials in racks, capping vials under nitrogen, sonication for catalyst dispersion, photolysis, and transferring samples to a head-space gas chromatograph (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3). In robustness tests the system achieved 464/464 successful vial grasps and placements and 400/400 successful cartridge grasps and placements at both the hotel and the Quantos dispenser, over 29 and 25 iterations respectively (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73). The cartridge cavity was only 0.5 mm larger than the cartridge, implying a placement tolerance of approximately 0.25 mm (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73). The arm had a stated repeatability of 0.1 mm, and after six-point touch-sensitive calibration against a reference cube, the station frame was located to within 0.12 mm with orientation residuals below 0.005° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).

Jiang et al. (2023; DOI: 10.1039/D3DD00075C) demonstrated an ABB YuMi dual-arm robot performing biomimetic solid dispensing. Tasks included opening and closing an analytical-balance door, moving vials and hoppers, selecting and manipulating spatulas of different sizes, scooping powder, dispensing through a funnel, and closed-loop weighing (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 3-4). The system dispensed thirteen different solids at target weights down to 20 ± 2 mg. For non-challenging solids at 200 mg, the mean dispensing error was 0.07%; across all solids the mean failure rate was 23.1% over 120 runs, where failure was defined as non-completion of one dispensing procedure (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11). Failure causes included blocked funnels, deliquescent material, and robot errors when the balance showed no weight change during continuous dispensing (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10).

Lunt et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1039/D3SC06206F) presented a multi-robot integration for a 12-step PXRD workflow using an ABB YuMi and KUKA KMR. The YuMi transferred vials from transport racks to a grinding station, carried out grinding-related handling, unscrewed vial caps, and placed caps into a plate at the sample-preparation station (lunt2024aroboticworkflow pages 87-95). The KMR provided mobile base precision of ±10 mm and orientation below ±2.5°, with fine calibration achieved via force-feedback six-point calibration cubes (lunt2024aroboticworkflow pages 49-57). No task-level success rate was reported for this system.

ORGANA (Darvish et al., 2025; DOI: 10.1016/j.matt.2024.10.015) used a Franka robot to perform solubility screening (7 steps, 25.63 min), recrystallization (8 steps, 44.80 min), pH measurement (6 steps, 3.85 min), and electrochemistry including electrode polishing (114 steps, 130 min) (darvish2025organaarobotic pages 14-15). No positioning tolerance or success rate was reported.

OSCAR (Pivin et al., 2026; DOI: 10.1021/acssynbio.5c00733) used a UR3 arm to perform pipetting (1–200 µL), tip handling, PCR thermocycler programming via a stylus tool, gel electrophoresis loading, Petri-dish manipulation, spreading, and colony picking across three multistep biology protocols. The same protocols were successfully replanned for UR5 and Franka Panda arms, with average planning time of approximately 5 s across all three arm models (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 7-8). No positioning tolerance or task-level success rate was reported.

Lim et al. (2021; DOI: 10.1109/TASE.2020.3036055) used a Denso VS-060 6-DOF arm to automate a Michael reaction, performing syringe-pump liquid transfer, mixing on a magnetic stirrer, silica/cotton filtration, and vial transfer to a GC-MS autoinjector rack. The robot achieved a yield of 34%, which was stated to be "comparable to that obtained by a junior chemist (undergraduate student in Chemistry)" (lim2021developmentofa pages 1-2). No positioning tolerance was reported; yields rather than manipulation success rates served as the outcome metric.

## 2. Positioning Tolerances by Task

The literature reveals a hierarchy of positioning demands:

- **Cartridge/hopper placement into powder dispensers**: ~0.25 mm, as the cavity clearance is only 0.5 mm larger than the cartridge (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73).
- **Syringe injection into a GC port**: ≤1.50 mm needle-tip error, as the injection port radius is 1.50 mm (angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 2-3, angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 4-6).
- **Vial grasping and placement**: The KUKA system achieved 0.12 mm calibrated accuracy sufficient for 100% success on 10 mL GC vials (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).
- **Solid dispensing (mass tolerance rather than position)**: ±2 mg at 20 mg targets; 0.07% mean error at 200 mg for non-challenging solids (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 2-3).
- **Mobile base coarse positioning**: ±10 mm and ±2.5° before fine arm calibration (lunt2024aroboticworkflow pages 49-57).

**Explicit gap**: No study reports the positioning tolerance required for swapping vial carriers on a pipetting gantry, loading well plates into plate readers, or changing augers/hoppers on a powder doser as a reproducible benchmark with a defined tolerance threshold. These tasks appear in descriptions of SDL workflows but without quantified manipulation requirements.

## 3. Station Recalibration Methods

Three distinct approaches to recalibration at each station have been published:

**Touch-sensitive calibration cubes (Burger et al., 2020)**: The KUKA LBR iiwa touches six points on three mutually perpendicular faces of a calibration cube at each station, and the recorded contact points define the station coordinate frame relative to the robot. With initial position noise of up to ±15 mm and orientation noise of ±2.5°, the cube was located to within 0.12 mm and orientation offsets were reduced below 0.005° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). This is the closest published analog to an AprilTag-based calibration square, but uses force feedback rather than vision.

**AprilTag coarse localization plus visual servoing (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; DOI: 10.1109/IROS55552.2023.10341743)**: An AprilTag brought the end effector near the injection target, then two fixed cameras and a Keypoint-RCNN model detected the syringe needle and iteratively servoed to submillimeter alignment. This achieved 100% injection success over 63 trials, while the AprilTag-only approach achieved only 9.52% (2160p) and 4.76% (1080p) (angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 7-8, angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 4-6). This demonstrates that AprilTag-only localization is insufficient for millimeter-precision tasks and must be supplemented with closed-loop visual refinement.

**Semantic object models with precision fixtures (OSCAR, Pivin et al., 2026)**: Each piece of labware is characterized in a Semantic Object Description Format (SODF) encoding 3D shape, interaction points, and forces. Objects are anchored with precision shoulder screws and 3D-printed supports; the system then uses position and force control to interact with them (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 3-5). This is a teaching-agnostic approach where new layouts require re-fixturing rather than re-programming.

**ALVAR fiducial tags (Joshi, 2021)**: ALVAR tags attached to objects supported object detection and pose estimation in a dual-arm system using cameras, Kinect, and force/torque sensors (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 35-39). This is the most direct precedent for AprilTag-based fiducial-guided motion in a laboratory manipulator context.

**Explicit gap**: No publication describes a system that uses AprilTags (specifically) as calibration squares at each station in a materials-science SDL to recalibrate a low-cost arm before each task, as the user describes. The Angelopoulos approach is the closest, but uses AprilTags only for coarse approach, not as the primary calibration target.

### 4.1 Teach Pendant and Scripted Waypoints

The dominant approach in published SDL work is deterministic programming via teach pendants or scripted waypoints. Burger et al. used station-specific programs with six-point tactile recalibration (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). Joshi (2021) described the ABB YuMi's RS-2 system where "job teaching" stores motion elements, labware-specific gripper indices, and reference points, explicitly rejecting path planning as "unsafe, difficult to reconfigure, computationally demanding" (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 66-69). OSCAR's approach generates robot behaviors from digital models of labware and a calibrated workspace map rather than from device-specific waypoint recording (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 3-5). This yields extremely high reliability where tested (464/464, 400/400), but the programming time per new task is not measured in any study.

### 4.2 Imitation Learning Policies on Low-Cost Arms

The most directly relevant study for the user's setup is Cowling (2026; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2610.37170; **preprint**), which evaluated ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA, and π0.5 on an SO-101 leader/follower arm (approximately $200 in parts) using LeRobot's implementations. The task was picking up a green ball while ignoring two red distractors and placing it in a basket, with success defined as delivery within 30 seconds. One operator recorded 225 demonstrations by leader-follower teleoperation at 30 fps; each demonstration lasted approximately 17 seconds. Nested training sets of 12, 25, 50, 100, and 200 demonstrations were evaluated on 10 fixed placements (cowling2026from12to pages 5-7, cowling2026from12to pages 7-9).

Key results (cowling2026from12to pages 9-12):
- **ACT from scratch**: 1/40 (2.5%) at 12 demos → 37/40 (92.5%) at 100 demos, with an odds ratio of 3.89 per doubling of data. This is the recommended LeRobot baseline for ≥100 demonstrations.
- **π0.5 expert-only fine-tuning**: 16/40 (40%) at 12 demos → 28/40 (70%) at 25 demos, then stable around 68–75%. This provides a large advantage with scarce data but saturates below ACT's peak.
- **Diffusion Policy (trimmed)**: Peaked at 25/50 (50%) with 50 demonstrations but was non-monotonic. Untrimmed data yielded only 3/110 successes; removing a median 2.8 s idle lead-in was essential.
- **SmolVLA**: Peaked at 21/40 (52.5%) at 50 demos and declined with more data.
- **π0.5 LoRA fine-tuning**: 4/110 total successes; this recipe largely failed.

The study's evaluation protocol is reproducible: each block comprised one checkpoint run once on each of 10 placements in order, with a 30-second limit per episode and a 20-second reset. All 1,340 main-comparison episodes were scored and audited against recordings (cowling2026from12to pages 7-9).

**Explicit gap**: No published work has applied ACT, Diffusion Policy, or any LeRobot-style imitation-learning policy to a laboratory instrument task (e.g., capping, pipetting, weighing, plate loading) on a low-cost arm. The Cowling study used a pick-and-place task with distractor objects, not a chemistry workflow. Extending these results to the constrained, precision-demanding tasks of an SDL requires new experiments.

### 4.3 Reinforcement Learning

Pizzuto et al. (2024; DOI: 10.1109/CASE59546.2024.10711291) demonstrated model-free RL (TQC with Hindsight Experience Replay) for vial scraping on a Franka Emika Panda. The policy used proprioceptive feedback and force/torque readings; no human demonstrations were required. The real-robot case study succeeded with two vial sizes and two scraper lengths, with each full-vial scrape taking approximately 90 minutes across 24 regions (pizzuto2024acceleratinglaboratoryautomation pages 6-7). Curriculum learning was essential for generalizing across starting poses (pizzuto2024acceleratinglaboratoryautomation pages 4-6). No repeated-trial success percentage was reported; the only non-recoverable failure was a stuck scraper requiring human reset (pizzuto2024acceleratinglaboratoryautomation pages 7-8).

Lei et al. (2026; DOI: 10.1126/scirobotics.aed6267) achieved 1000/1000 successful episodes across eight manipulation tasks by combining diffusion-policy imitation learning with offline and online RL. Per task, approximately 115 demonstrations (1.8 hours of human teleoperation), 566 offline RL rollouts (6.5 hours), and 434 online RL rollouts (5.6 hours) were needed—approximately 13.9 hours total (lei2026performantroboticmanipulation pages 12-15). This was not demonstrated in an SDL context, but the protocol establishes a reproducible benchmark for deployment-grade manipulation learning.

## 5. Cost Considerations

**No published cost-per-task metric exists** for comparing robot arms in SDL applications. The literature provides only broad cost ranges:

- Commercial articulated robots (KUKA, ABB, UR, Franka) are described as generally costing approximately $20,000–$40,000 for the arm alone (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-24). Complete mobile platforms such as the KUKA KMR are substantially more expensive (generally considered over $100,000).
- Burger's thesis notes that combining a MiR mobile base with a UR5 or Franka Panda provides "a dexterous collaborative mobile robot similar to the KMR at a lower purchase price" but gives no figures (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52).
- 3D-printed and open-source robotic arms range from $50 (DeltaZ) to $700 (anthropomorphic arm with vision-based manipulation) (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-25).
- The SO-101 arm used in the Cowling (2026) study costs "a few hundred dollars in parts" (cowling2026from12to pages 1-3).

**Explicit gap**: No study compares the cost per task—or cost per successfully completed laboratory operation—between industry-standard arms (UR5e at ~$35k, Franka Panda at ~$30k, ABB YuMi at ~$40k) and low-cost alternatives (AgileX PiPER at ~$2k, SO-101 at ~$200). Such a metric would need to account for arm cost, integration time, programming or training time per task, success rate, cycle time, and maintenance. This represents a significant gap that the user's work could fill.

## 6. Reproducible Test Protocols Identified

Based on the literature, the following tests can be reproduced by anyone validating an affordable arm for SDL use:

1. **Vial grasp-and-place (Burger protocol)**: Grasp 10 mL GC vials from a rack, navigate to a target station, and place into a receiving rack. Repeat for N iterations. Measure: success/failure (vial placed without dropping); number of trials ≥400; success criterion: vial seated in well; measuring tool: visual inspection or force-feedback contact confirmation (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73).

2. **Cartridge/hopper insertion (Burger protocol)**: Place a cartridge into a cavity with 0.5 mm clearance. Measure: successful insertion (cartridge seated); number of trials ≥400; success criterion: cartridge detected in position; measuring tool: force-feedback confirmation (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 69-73).

3. **Syringe injection alignment (Angelopoulos protocol)**: Align a syringe needle with a 1.50 mm radius target. Measure: XY planar distance between needle tip and target center after alignment; number of trials ≥63; success criterion: needle tip within 1.50 mm of target center; measuring tool: two calibrated machine-vision cameras with Keypoint-RCNN detection (angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 4-6, angelopoulos2023highaccuracyinjectionusing pages 6-7).

4. **Solid dispensing accuracy (Jiang protocol)**: Dispense 13 different solids at multiple target weights (1000, 500, 200, 20 mg). Measure: percentage dispensing error = (dispensed − target)/target × 100; number of runs: 120 total; success criterion: completed procedure within mass tolerance; measuring tool: analytical balance; failure criterion: non-completion of one procedure (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 7-8).

5. **Imitation-learning data-efficiency scaling (Cowling protocol)**: Train ACT/Diffusion Policy/foundation models on nested demonstration sets of 12, 25, 50, 100, 200 episodes. Evaluate each checkpoint on 10 fixed placements with a 30-second time limit. Measure: successes/episodes with 95% Wilson score intervals; criterion: target object delivered to goal; measuring tool: operator scoring audited against camera recordings (cowling2026from12to pages 5-7, cowling2026from12to pages 7-9, cowling2026from12to pages 9-12).

6. **Six-point calibration accuracy (Burger protocol)**: Touch six points on three perpendicular faces of a calibration cube with known initial noise. Measure: residual position error (mm) and orientation error (degrees); measuring tool: force-feedback contact detection; success criterion: frame located within specification (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).

## 7. Explicit Gaps in the Published Literature

The following specific gaps were identified where **no published evidence exists**:

1. **No study has used imitation learning (ACT, Diffusion Policy, or any LeRobot policy) for a laboratory instrument task** such as capping, pipetting, weighing, or plate loading on any arm, let alone a low-cost one.

2. **No cost-per-task metric** has been published comparing industry-standard and affordable arms for SDL operations.

3. **No study has used AprilTags as the primary calibration target** (calibration square) at each station for a robot arm in a materials-science SDL. The Angelopoulos AprilTag + visual-servoing approach and Burger's touch-calibration-cube approach are the closest analogs.

4. **No study has benchmarked an AgileX PiPER or SO-101 arm** on any laboratory instrument task. The SO-101 has been evaluated only on a pick-and-place task with distractor objects.

5. **No study reports positioning tolerance requirements for swapping vial carriers on a pipetting gantry**, loading/unloading augers or hoppers on a powder doser, or performing tool changes in a materials-science context as reproducible benchmarks.

6. **Task-level success rates are rarely reported** with sufficient statistical rigor: Burger (100%, ≥400 trials), Angelopoulos (100%, 63 trials), and Jiang (76.9%, 120 runs) are exceptions. Most SDL papers describe workflow demonstrations without systematic success/failure counting.

7. **Setup time and programming time per new task** are not measured in any SDL robotics paper. This information is essential for comparing the value proposition of teach-pendant programming against imitation learning but is entirely absent from the literature.
