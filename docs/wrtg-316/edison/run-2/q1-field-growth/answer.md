## Robot Arms as Automated Manual Manipulators in Self-Driving Laboratories: A Literature Review of Benchmarks, Calibration, and Cost

### 1. The Self-Driving Laboratory Landscape and Growth

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) integrate robotic hardware, automated instruments, and AI-driven experiment selection into closed-loop discovery platforms. The field has grown rapidly since approximately 2019, driven by declining robotics costs, broader AI accessibility, and open-source designs (bayley2024autonomouschemistrynavigating pages 1-3). Tom et al. (2024), in the most comprehensive review to date (1,222 citations), categorize SDL hardware into three price tiers: "special-purpose commercial systems cost approximately $1 million, general-purpose commercial hardware approximately $10,000, and open hardware approximately $100–$2,000" (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). No peer-reviewed source provides a definitive count of operating SDLs or a market-size estimate; the literature instead characterizes growth qualitatively through publications, demonstrated workflows, and cost reductions (tobias2025autonomousselfdrivinglaboratoriesa pages 12-14, bayley2024autonomouschemistrynavigating pages 1-3). One economic reference notes that establishing a physical laboratory may require approximately $800,000 in equipment, while cloud-lab subscriptions start at approximately $50,000/month (tobias2025autonomousselfdrivinglaboratoriesa pages 12-14).

#### 2.1 The Defining System: Burger's Mobile Robotic Chemist (KUKA LBR iiwa)

The landmark system is Burger et al. (2020, *Nature*, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2442-2, 2,300 citations). A KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF arm on a KUKA Mobile Platform (KMR) operated autonomously in an otherwise unmodified chemistry laboratory. The arm had "a 14 kg maximum payload, an 820 mm reach," and the mobile base navigated using SLAM-like laser scanning to "better than ±10 mm positional and less than ±2.5° orientation precision" (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6). Because this was insufficient for manipulating vials, the robot performed a **six-point touch calibration** at each station: "it touched six points on a station-associated cube to determine the cube's position and orientation relative to the robot. This improved accuracy to ±0.12 mm and ±0.005°, broadly comparable to a human operator" (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). The system performed over 600 experiments over eight days, covering solid dispensing, gravimetric liquid dispensing, capping under nitrogen, photolysis, and gas chromatography (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 6-12). It reportedly improved photocatalyst hydrogen evolution "up to 1,000 times faster than a human researcher" (carruthers2025amobilerobotic pages 24-27).

#### 2.2 Universal Robots UR5e

The UR5e (6-DOF, ±0.03 mm vendor repeatability, 5 kg payload) is the most widely cited arm in SDL publications. It has been used for: mobile SDL sample handling with a Clearpath Ridgeback base and Robotiq Hand-E gripper (sulaiman2026kinematicanalysisand pages 6-9); automated *in situ* XRD experimentation (Yoo et al. 2026); retrosynthetic flow-reactor assembly (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 3-4); digital pathology slide handling with a custom manipulator requiring 24 distinct laboratory tasks (kucarov2025mappingmanuallaboratory pages 25-27); and robotic chemical experiment automation with dual demonstration methods (Sasaki et al. 2025). No single published cross-system calibration or benchmarking protocol was identified for the UR5e in SDL contexts; each study employed study-specific taught frames, vision-based estimation, or fiducial registration.

#### 2.3 Universal Robots UR3 and OSCAR

The OSCAR platform (Pivin et al. 2026, *ACS Synthetic Biology*, DOI: 10.1021/acssynbio.5c00733) uses a UR3 arm (6-DOF, ≤0.1 mm repeatability, 3 kg payload, academic price CAD 25,000) with a custom dual-function end effector combining a micropipette (1–200 μL) and gripper. It demonstrated a complete plasmid-cloning workflow: "PCR amplification, Gibson assembly, bacterial transformation, plating, colony picking, PCR validation, and agarose-gel electrophoresis" (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 2-3). Tip insertion was demonstrated at "±0.5 mm precision" for picking colonies "approximately 1 mm in diameter" (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 2-3). The total OSCAR implementation cost was "approximately $35,000 USD" (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 8-9). Protocols were also demonstrated on UR5 and Franka Panda arms with minimal modification (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 7-8).

#### 2.4 Franka Emika Panda

The Panda (7-DOF, ~±0.1 mm repeatability, 3 kg payload) appears primarily in benchmarking contexts. Kirschner et al. (2021, IROS, DOI: 10.1109/IROS51168.2021.9636329) proposed tactile performance and safety benchmarks validated on the UR5e, UR10e, and Panda, complementing ISO 9283 motion tests. OSCAR demonstrated protocol portability to the Panda, though one accessory required relocation due to joint constraints (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 8-9). The RoboMIND dataset (Wu et al. 2024, preprint) includes the Panda alongside the UR5e and AgileX dual-arm robot for imitation-learning teleoperation data.

### 3. Calibration Methods: Touch-Based versus Vision-Based

Two paradigms dominate station-level calibration:

**Touch-based calibration** was pioneered by Burger et al. The LBR iiwa arm touched six points on a physical cube at each station; "three points defined one plane, two points defined a perpendicular second plane, and a final point defined the third plane," establishing a station coordinate frame (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). This achieved ±0.12 mm and <0.005° from initial errors of ±15 mm and ±2.5° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). This approach requires force-torque sensing and physical fixtures but delivers sub-millimeter accuracy.

**Vision-based calibration using fiducial markers** (ArUco, AprilTag) is an alternative. Wienbruch et al. (2025, *Scientific Reports*, DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-05670-1) used ArUco markers on laboratory devices with a Raspberry Pi camera mounted on a Horst600 arm. They achieved a mean 3D reconstruction deviation of 15 mm (axis means: 12 mm X, 10 mm Y, 23 mm Z) and applied a 50 mm safety margin for collision avoidance (wienbruch2025facilitatinglaboratoryautomation pages 7-8, wienbruch2025facilitatinglaboratoryautomation pages 4-7). Their display-reading pipeline achieved a 1.69% error rate (wienbruch2025facilitatinglaboratoryautomation pages 1-2). In contrast, Michaux et al. (2024) used ChArUco boards for robotic PIV calibration and reported positioning uncertainty of approximately 2.5 mm at 2 m working distance (michaux2024automaticsetupand pages 9-12). The Hydra marker-free calibration method (Huber et al. 2025, preprint) achieved approximately 5 mm task-space accuracy using RGB-D cameras on serial manipulators.

**Gap identified:** No published study directly compares AprilTag-based station recalibration accuracy on a low-cost arm (such as the PiPER or SO-101) against the touch-based calibration of industry-standard arms in an SDL workflow. This is a primary validation test the user should design and publish.

#### 4.1 The REPLAB Grasping Benchmark

REPLAB (Yang et al. 2019, ICRA, DOI: 10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794390) is the closest published analog to what the user proposes. It uses a ~$2,000 WidowX 6-DOF arm in a 70×40×60 cm self-contained cell. The grasping benchmark protocol: 20 objects are scattered using a standardized procedure (box filled, shaken, inverted); 60 grasp attempts per episode; success requires the object to remain in the gripper for 2 seconds; cumulative success rate (CSR) is plotted against attempts, averaged over 3 runs (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 3-4, yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 4-6). Random grasping succeeded in approximately 21–23% of attempts; the best learned method (pinto2016) cleared all 20 objects in every run (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 4-6, yang2019replabareproducible pages 4-5). Cross-cell calibration errors were 0.87 cm and 1.52 cm (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 4-6). The dataset exceeds 92,000 grasp attempts collected at ~2,500 per day per cell (yang2019replabareproducible pages 4-5). This benchmark is directly adaptable to the PiPER and SO-101.

#### 4.2 The Volk–Abolhasani SDL Metrics Framework

Volk and Abolhasani (2024, *Nature Communications*, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-45569-5, 161 citations) proposed a multi-metric framework for SDL evaluation: degree of autonomy, operational lifetime (demonstrated vs. theoretical, assisted vs. unassisted), throughput (demonstrated vs. theoretical), experimental precision (from unbiased replicates), material usage (total, high-value, hazardous), accessible parameter space, and optimization efficiency (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 3-5, volk2024performancemetricsto pages 1-2). Critically, their literature audit found that "71% of studies did not quantify the precision of their automated experimental systems, and none of the reviewed literature provided quantitative information on the accessible parameter space" (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 6-7). This gap means that most SDL arms lack the basic replicate-precision data needed for fair comparison.

#### 4.3 The ADePT Capability Framework

Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia (2026, *Communications Chemistry*, DOI: 10.1038/s42004-026-01932-9, 18 citations) introduced ADePT, assessing laboratory robots across four dimensions: Adaptability and Learning, Dexterity, Perception, and Task Complexity (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 4-5, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 7-9). Many current systems "remain systems integrators: effective in controlled settings but limited in perception, adaptation, error recovery and generalisation" (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 4-5). The framework is not a physical test but a capability-rating rubric that can contextualize where a low-cost arm falls relative to industry platforms.

### 5. The Low-Cost and Open-Source Movement

The movement toward affordable laboratory arms is accelerating. Tom et al. (2024) note that open-hardware SDL systems cost approximately $100–$2,000, though they "generally require additional software development and hardware building" (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). Doloi et al. (2025, *Digital Discovery*, DOI: 10.1039/d4dd00411f, 53 citations) catalog 3D-printed robotic arms from $50 (DeltaZ, basic pick-and-place) to $700 (anthropomorphic arm for vision-based manipulation) (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-25). Commercial articulated robots are described as costing approximately $20,000–$40,000, making them inaccessible for many SDL groups (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-24). The Minimum Viable Option (MVO) liquid-handling system costs under $50 and reportedly achieves "less than 0.1 mm linear-motion accuracy with pipetting performance comparable to commercial systems" (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-24).

Rupp et al. (2022, *SLAS Technology*, DOI: 10.1016/j.slast.2022.07.001, 30 citations) demonstrated that a low-cost 4-axis robot with AutoIt scripting achieved "accuracy and precision that did not significantly differ from manual work by a trained professional" for pipetting, autosampling, and bacterial inoculation (rupp2022establishmentoflowcost pages 1-2). Highly professional automation lines "can cost more than €1 million" (rupp2022establishmentoflowcost pages 1-2).

The SO-101 arm (~$200) is appearing in the imitation-learning ecosystem. VLA-REPLICA (Huang et al. 2026, preprint) notes that "LeRobot's SO-101 robotic arm is its low cost and ease of self-assembly." ArmnetBench (Selvaraj et al. 2026, preprint) reports benchmarks on a fleet of SO-101 cells. However, **no peer-reviewed publication validates the SO-101 or the AgileX PiPER in an SDL materials-science workflow**.

### 6. Imitation Learning and LeRobot-Style Approaches

The convergence of affordable arms and imitation learning is an emerging paradigm. Several 2026 preprints reference LeRobot-format datasets for teleoperated manipulation demonstrations on arms including the SO-101 and UR5e. The RoboMIND dataset (Wu et al. 2024, preprint) contains 107,000 demonstration trajectories across the Franka Panda, UR5e, AgileX dual-arm, and a humanoid, using human teleoperation. Chiche et al. (2026, *IEEE Access*) demonstrated hand-shadowing teleoperation "using a second SO-ARM101 as the leader arm (standard LeRobot teleoperation)." These approaches are directly relevant to the user's leader/follower SO-101 setup, but they address general manipulation rather than SDL-specific tasks (pipetting, powder dosing, balance reading, etc.).

### 7. Where Researchers Agree, Disagree, and Leave Gaps

**Consensus areas:**
- Robot arms functioning as automated manual manipulators—moving between stations, handling labware, and operating instruments designed for humans—is a validated paradigm (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3).
- Station-level calibration is essential; coarse mobile-base positioning alone (±10 mm) is insufficient for vial-scale manipulation (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6).
- Multi-metric evaluation is preferred over single throughput numbers (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 3-5, volk2024performancemetricsto pages 1-2).
- Cost is a major barrier to SDL adoption, and open-source/low-cost systems are needed to democratize the field (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6, doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 24-24).

**Areas of disagreement or divergence:**
- Whether mobile manipulators or fixed-station robots are preferable. Burger used a mobile platform, while most other SDLs use fixed arms with conveyor or gantry sample transport. The ADePT framework identifies both "robot-centric" and "end-to-end integrated" configurations as viable (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 9-11).
- Touch-based versus vision-based calibration: touch calibration achieves ±0.12 mm (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56) versus ArUco/camera systems at 15 mm mean deviation (wienbruch2025facilitatinglaboratoryautomation pages 7-8). These are not directly comparable (the former is end-effector positioning, the latter is workspace reconstruction), but the accuracy gap is significant for fine manipulation.

**Published gaps — no evidence found:**
1. **Cost-per-task metrics.** No SDL publication reports a standardized cost-per-task (e.g., cost per sample dispensed, cost per pick-and-place cycle, cost per experiment) that enables arm-to-arm comparison. This metric was not found in any of the retrieved studies.
2. **Low-cost arm SDL validation.** No peer-reviewed study deploys the AgileX PiPER, SO-101, or any sub-$2,000 6-DOF arm in a materials-science SDL workflow with quantified accuracy and throughput. REPLAB benchmarks grasping but not laboratory-specific tasks.
3. **AprilTag station-calibration accuracy on low-cost arms.** No study validates AprilTag-based station recalibration for laboratory manipulation with a low-cost arm and compares it against touch-based calibration.
4. **Replicate precision.** Volk and Abolhasani found that 71% of SDL studies did not quantify automated-system precision (volk2024performancemetricsto pages 6-7). Basic replicate-precision reporting remains absent for most arms.
5. **Cross-arm standardized protocols.** No single standardized physical test protocol exists for comparing arms across different SDL installations. Each laboratory develops bespoke evaluation procedures.

### 8. Recommended Tests for Validating an Affordable Arm

Based on the published literature, the following tests can be repeated to compare a low-cost arm (PiPER, SO-101) against industry-standard SDL arms:

1. **Station-calibration accuracy test** (adapted from Burger 2020): Place a fiducial target (AprilTag or physical cube) at each station. Measure residual position and orientation error after calibration using an external reference (e.g., dial indicator or laser tracker). Report mean and standard deviation over ≥10 calibrations per station. Compare against the ±0.12 mm / <0.005° baseline.

2. **REPLAB-style grasping benchmark** (Yang 2019): Build a ~$2,000 cell. Scatter 20 objects; run 60 grasp attempts per episode, 3 runs. Report cumulative success rate. Compare against the 21–23% random baseline and pinto2016's full-clearance performance.

3. **Pipetting accuracy test** (adapted from OSCAR): Dispense known volumes (10, 50, 100, 200 μL) gravimetrically onto an analytical balance. Report mean error and CV% over ≥10 replicates per volume. Compare against OSCAR's ±0.5 mm tip-insertion precision and manual-pipette tolerance.

4. **SDL workflow endurance test** (adapted from Kucarov 2025): Run the full automated workflow continuously for ≥1 week at maximum load. Record failure events, recovery time, and total successful operations. Compare against the 1,000-slide endurance test.

5. **Volk–Abolhasani metrics reporting:** For any SDL deployment, report: degree of autonomy, demonstrated throughput, operational lifetime, replicate-based precision, material usage, and optimization efficiency.

6. **ADePT self-assessment:** Score the system across Adaptability, Dexterity, Perception, and Task Complexity using the rubrics in the supplementary materials of Salazar-Villacis and Benyahia (2026).
