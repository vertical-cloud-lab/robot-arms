### 2. ISO 9283 Pose Accuracy and Repeatability

The fundamental metrological benchmark for any robot arm is EN ISO 9283:1998, which prescribes commanding the tool centre point (TCP) to five points inside an imaginary ISO cube across at least 30 unidirectional cycles. Pollák et al. (2020) performed this measurement on a Universal Robots UR5 using six Heidenhain MT 25 / MT 12 incremental sensors (stated accuracy 0.004 mm) and six VRZ 401 evaluation units (pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 10-12, pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 1-2). Under 100 % payload at 10 % speed, the measured unidirectional pose accuracy was AP = 0.02223 mm; observed repeatability values ranged from approximately 0.033 mm to 0.065 mm across conditions, well within the ±0.1 mm manufacturer specification (pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 12-14, pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 16-18). Kirschner et al. (2021) adapted the ISO 9283 methodology for force-sensing benchmarking on a Franka Emika Panda, UR5e, and UR10e, using a K6D40 force–torque sensor (500 N range, 0.2 % accuracy) and 30 repetitions per condition (kirschner2021towardsareference pages 3-4, kirschner2021towardsareference pages 2-3). Their results measure force accuracy and repeatability, not Cartesian pose, so they must not be conflated with pose-repeatability specifications (kirschner2021towardsareference pages 1-2).

**Replication guidance for low-cost arms:** A simplified version using an AprilTag fiducial target and an external camera to measure TCP position over 30+ cycles can yield useful pose-repeatability data, but should not be claimed as fully ISO-equivalent without adequate traceability and uncertainty analysis. PiPER, with its higher build quality, is a more suitable candidate than SO-101 for this test.

### 3. NIST-Inspired Task Boards and Peg-in-Hole Assembly

**Lian et al. (2021, IROS)** benchmarked off-the-shelf solutions on NIST Assembly Task Board #1, selecting 12 parts (four round pegs of 4–16 mm, four rectangular pegs, and four connectors including USB and RJ45) and evaluating position control (KUKA iiwa 14), hybrid force/motion control with spiral search (ABB IRB1200 with ATI Gamma F/T sensor), and 3-D vision (UR5e with LMI Gocator 3506 sensors) (lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions pages 1-2, lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions pages 3-4, lian2021benchmarkingofftheshelfsolutions pages 2-3). The protocol injects controlled 3-D pose perturbations and measures completion rate, tolerance area, cycle time, and cost. Per-task success rates were not available in the retrieved text but are reported in the paper's figures.

**Tang et al. (2023, preprint)** presented IndustReal using a Franka Emika Panda with a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D435 camera and reinforcement-learning policies trained in simulation. Over 600 real-world trials on NIST-inspired pegs and gears with 0.5–0.6 mm clearances, success rates ranged from 83 % to 99 %: specifically, pick achieved 158/160 (98.75 %), insertion 100/120 (83.33 %), and complete pick–place–insert 107/120 (89.16 %) (tang2023industrealtransferringcontactrich pages 1-2, tang2023industrealtransferringcontactrich pages 8-10, tang2023industrealtransferringcontactrich pages 2-3).

**So et al. (2024, IEEE RAM)** introduced the Digital Robot Judge (DR.J), a compact electronic task board with six industry-inspired subtasks (button press, key switch, Ethernet-plug insertion, battery extraction, battery recycling, and button press). Across two international Robothon competitions, 10 of 29 selected teams completed all tasks. The board automatically records subtask completion times. The best robot achieved 52 s versus a human mean of 18.9 s and best of 8.1 s. Robots reached only 16 % of best-human speed overall, though one specialized task was completed 9× faster than humans (so2024digitalrobotjudge pages 8-9, so2024digitalrobotjudge pages 11-13, so2024digitalrobotjudge pages 9-10). Platforms included Franka, KUKA iiwa, and Epson VT6 (so2024digitalrobotjudge pages 11-13). The DR.J board is modular and openly documented, making it particularly attractive for low-cost arm validation.

**Van Wyk et al. (2018, IEEE T-RO)** compared compliant-hand and rigid-end-effector peg-in-hole strategies using 3-D-printable pegs at dimensionless clearance c = 0.02, with 60 attempted insertions per system and Gaussian positional offsets of 1 and 2 mm standard deviation (wyk2018comparativepeginholetesting pages 3-5).

### 4. Pick-and-Place and YCB Object Protocols

**Calli et al. (2015, IEEE RAM)** defined the YCB Object and Model Set benchmarking framework, including a Peg Insertion Learning Assessment (60 trials: 40 at the known home position across four time limits of 0.5–5 s, plus 20 trials at unknown 5/10 mm perturbations), a Gripper Assessment, Block Pick and Place, Table Setting, and a Pitcher-Mug pouring task. On the HERB robot, the Pitcher-Mug protocol was completed successfully in 8/10 configurations (calli2015benchmarkinginmanipulationa pages 24-27, calli2015benchmarkinginmanipulation pages 11-13, calli2015benchmarkinginmanipulationa pages 11-13). These protocols are hardware-agnostic and directly replicable.

**Morgan et al. (2020, IEEE RA-L)** adapted the clinical Box and Blocks Test (BBT) for robot benchmarking, defining three protocols: Modified-BBT (16 ordered blocks), Targeted-BBT (16 blocks placed in numbered targets), and Standard-BBT (transfer from 100 randomly configured blocks in one minute). Each protocol is repeated at least five consecutive times. Baselines were provided on a Barrett WAM with a Yale OpenHand T42 gripper and a Franka Emika Panda with extended parallel-gripper fingers (morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 1-2, morgan2020benchmarkingclutteredrobot pages 4-5). The BBT apparatus is distributed with the YCB Object and Model Set, ensuring reproducibility.

**Bottarel et al. (2020, IEEE RA-L)** presented GRASPA 1.0, evaluating five physical grasps per object per layout with a success criterion of lifting 0.15 m and holding for at least 5 s. Example iCub results included mustard bottle and chips can at 100 %, banana and cracker box at 80 %, and foam brick and tennis ball at 20 % (bottarel2020graspa1.0graspa pages 3-4, bottarel2020graspa1.0graspa pages 7-8). The benchmark was also deployed on a Franka Emika Panda with a RealSense D415 camera (bottarel2021wheresmymesh? pages 64-67, bottarel2021wheresmymesh? pages 84-87).

**Heo et al. (2025, IJRR)** presented FurnitureBench, a reproducible long-horizon benchmark using a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda with its default parallel gripper, three RealSense D435 cameras, and AprilTags. Skills were evaluated over 10 trials; example IQL success rates reached 100 % for some desk grasp/place phases. Reproduced environments in 10 newly built setups retained 84 % of original performance (heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 6-7, heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 8-10, heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 3-4). Single-skill components (grasping, insertion) are transferable to low-cost arms, though full furniture assembly may exceed their payload.

**Khargonkar et al. (2024, ICRA)** introduced SceneReplica, using 20 procedurally generated cluttered tabletop scenes with 16 YCB objects, designed for reproducibility without AR tags (khargonkar2024scenereplicabenchmarkingrealworld pages 1-2).

### 5. Laboratory-Specific Manipulation Tasks

**RobInHood (Longley et al. 2026, Digital Discovery)** demonstrated a Franka Emika Panda performing vial transport, capping/decapping (via a fixture-based friction rail), solid dispensing, liquid dispensing, heating/stirring, filtration, and imaging of 20 mL sample vials within a standard fume hood (longley2026robinhoodarobotic pages 3-5). No repeated-trial success rate was reported in the extracted evidence; this is an explicit gap that makes direct quantitative comparison with low-cost arms impossible without establishing such rates.

**Manes et al. (2024, Scientific Reports)** reported a soft cable-loop gripper on a KUKA iiwa for chemistry automation that achieved a failure rate of only 0.8 %, summarized as ≤1 % grasp failures, for cylindrical and prismatic laboratory objects. The exact trial denominator was not available from the extracted evidence.

**MATTERIX (Darvish et al. 2025, Nature Computational Science)** achieved a 90 % success rate in real-world experiments using a KUKA KMR iiwa mobile manipulator for chemistry-laboratory tasks, evaluated through a digital-twin simulation-to-real pipeline. Full protocol details would need to be recovered from the paper before replication.

**LabRobFail (Wang et al. 2026, preprint)** provides a benchmark for robotic failure analysis in chemical self-driving laboratories, with more than 20,000 operation trajectories covering 70+ task scenarios (pick, place, pour, stir, dryer loading), five major failure categories (Perception, Grasping, Motion, Logic, Safety), and 11 fine-grained failure types. Their VLM-based failure-detection system achieved 90.83 % accuracy and improved downstream task success by 4–16 percentage points (wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 2-3, wang2026labrobfailabenchmark pages 1-2).

**ADePT Framework (Salazar-Villacis & Benyahia 2026, Communications Chemistry)** proposes a four-dimensional assessment for autonomous laboratory robots: Adaptability and Learning, Dexterity, Perception, and Task Complexity. Example dexterity tasks include vial manipulation, sealing, pipetting, microwell-plate insertion, and pouring (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 4-5, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 6-7, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 2-3). ADePT is a scoring rubric, not a physical test suite, and must be paired with repeated-trial protocols.

**No published evidence exists** for standardized, repeated-trial benchmarks of vial decapping, microplate loading, or instrument-loading success rates with quantitative denominators on any arm model. The SDL literature describes these tasks qualitatively but does not report manipulation success rates in a form that permits cross-platform comparison. This is the most significant gap identified.

### 6. Low-Cost Arm Benchmarks

**ArmnetBench v0.1 (Selvaraj et al. 2026, preprint)** evaluated seven manipulation policies over 12 tasks (stacking, insertion, deformables, pick-and-place, articulated objects) on SO-101 arms, collecting 2,518 valid policy rollouts with approximately 30 rollouts per task–policy pair. Policy-only strict success was 690/2,518 ≈ 27.4 %, or 30.9 % including suboptimal outcomes (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 3-7, selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 1-3). This is the most comprehensive published benchmark for the SO-101 platform.

**Cowling (2026, preprint)** tested four imitation-learning policies on a pick-and-place task (green ball into basket, two red distractors) using an SO-101 arm over 1,340 physical episodes. ACT achieved 2.5 % (1/40) with 12 demonstrations and rose to 92.5 % (37/40) with 100 demonstrations; fine-tuned π0.5 achieved 40 % at 12 and 70 % at 25 demonstrations (cowling2026from12to pages 1-3).

**LeRobot (Cadène et al. 2026, preprint)** provides the software ecosystem for SO-100/101 arms and reports approximate hardware costs: SO-100/101 at ~$225 ($550 for leader-follower pair), Koch-v1.1 at ~$670, ALOHA at ~$21k, and LeKiwi at ~$230 (cadene2026lerobotanopensource pages 3-5). The paper emphasizes that these platforms cost a fraction of the Franka Panda but does not state an exact Panda price.

### 7. Cost and Cost-Per-Task

**No published cost-per-task metric was found** in any of the reviewed literature. Tom et al. (2024, Chemical Reviews) provide approximate hardware-cost tiers for SDL platforms: special-purpose commercial hardware ~$1 M, general-purpose commercial hardware ~$10,000, and open hardware ~$100–$2,000 (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). Lo et al. (2024, Digital Discovery) describe the "frugal twin" concept and list representative system costs ranging from $300 (Educational ARES) to $15,000 (nanomaterials robot), with the Opentrons OT-2 at ~$7,500 (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 5-6, lo2023reviewoflowcost pages 2-5). These are system-level figures, not isolated arm prices or per-task cost comparisons. This represents a significant gap: there is no published framework for normalizing a robot arm's hardware cost by the number of successful task completions to yield a cost-per-task metric. Developing such a metric (e.g., hardware amortization per successful vial transfer or peg insertion) would be a novel contribution.

### 8. Summary and Recommendations

The table above compiles 18 published benchmarks across six categories. For validating a PiPER or SO-101 as an automated manual manipulator in a self-driving laboratory, the following benchmark stack is recommended, ordered from foundational to application-specific:

1. **Pose repeatability** (simplified ISO 9283 with AprilTag fiducial, 30+ cycles)
2. **Peg-in-hole insertion** (3D-printed NIST-style pegs, 60+ trials per clearance, following Van Wyk et al. or IndustReal geometry)
3. **DR.J electronic task board** (automatic scoring of 6 subtasks including insertion and manipulation)
4. **YCB pick-and-place** (BBT protocol, ≥5 repetitions per condition, YCB block set)
5. **GRASPA grasping** (5 trials per YCB object, 0.15 m lift, 5 s hold)
6. **SDL-specific primitives** (vial pick-and-place, decapping, plate loading — these must be established as new benchmarks with ≥30 trials per primitive, since no published standard exists)
7. **LeRobot/ArmnetBench imitation-learning tasks** (for SO-101 specifically, using the published 12-task suite)

For each test, report the exact arm model, gripper, camera, control method (scripted vs. learned), number of trials, success criterion, and any fixture or environmental aids. Where a low-cost arm cannot match an industrial arm's native force sensing, state the limitation rather than omitting the benchmark.
