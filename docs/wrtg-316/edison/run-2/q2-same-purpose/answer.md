# Tests That Determine the Value of a Robot Arm as an Automated Manual Manipulator in a Self-Driving Laboratory: A Literature Review

## 1. Core Finding: No Publication Exactly Matches the Stated Purpose

After a comprehensive search of peer-reviewed journals, major conference proceedings, and preprints from 2015–2026, **no publication was found whose sole purpose is to define or run tests that evaluate, compare, or select a robot arm for use as an automated manual manipulator in a self-driving or automated laboratory**. No paper directly compares low-cost arms (such as AgileX PiPER or SO-100/101) with industry-standard arms (UR5e, Franka Panda, KUKA LBR iiwa) on laboratory-specific tasks, and no published "cost per task" metric for laboratory robot arms exists.

However, several closely related bodies of work collectively address nearly all aspects of this question. These fall into five categories: (A) SDL system-level validation papers that report arm performance as a by-product; (B) ISO 9283-based arm metrology; (C) simulation and task benchmarks for laboratory robotics; (D) low-cost manipulation benchmarks using imitation learning; and (E) fiducial-marker-based calibration studies applicable to station recalibration. ## 2. Terminology: "Automated Manual Manipulator" Does Not Appear in the Literature

The term **"automated manual manipulator"** was not found in any of the retrieved publications. The literature uses the following alternative terms to describe a robot arm that performs hands-on laboratory work a human would otherwise do (yoshikawa2024towardsautomatedrobotic pages 8-10, chu2016automationstrategiesfor pages 16-18, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52):

- **"Mobile manipulator"** — the most common term in SDL literature, especially for arms on mobile bases (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52)
- **"Mobile robotic chemist"** or **"robotic chemist"** — Burger et al.'s system (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3)
- **"General-purpose robot arm"** — emphasizing flexibility versus specialized hardware (yoshikawa2024towardsautomatedrobotic pages 8-10)
- **"Dual-arm robot"** or **"dual-arm laboratory robot"** — for two-armed systems like ABB or Yaskawa (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 39-42, joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 42-46)
- **"Collaborative robot" / "cobot"** — for arms designed for human co-working (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 28-31)
- **"Robotic laboratory assistant"** and **"laboratory automation robot"** (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 153-156)
- **"Robotic sample-preparation system"** — for systems focused on analytical workflows (joshi2021optimizationandapplication pages 150-153)

Burger's thesis describes systems that "take on the role of the researcher" and perform "complex pick-and-place tasks" at multiple stations (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 49-52). Yoshikawa describes "general-purpose robots" that can "manipulate existing human-oriented laboratory tools, reducing instrument reconfiguration" (yoshikawa2024towardsautomatedrobotic pages 8-10).

## 3. Closest Papers and Their Tests

### 3.1 Burger et al. (2020) — "A mobile robotic chemist" (Nature, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2442-2)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate an autonomous mobile robot performing photocatalysis experiments across a human-scale laboratory. **Arm:** KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF on a KUKA mobile base (specified repeatability ±0.1 mm). **Calibration:** A tactile six-point calibration procedure: the robot touched a calibration cube at six locations across three mutually perpendicular planes, then used the contact points and plane geometry to define each station's coordinate frame. This achieved localization within 0.12 mm and orientation offsets below 0.005° (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). **Results:** The robot completed 688 experiments autonomously over eight days in a 7.3 × 11 m laboratory, with reported positioning precision of ±0.12 mm in x–y and ±0.005° orientation (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3). **How it differs:** This is a capability demonstration, not a benchmark that compares arms. No task-level success/failure rate was published, and no alternative arm was tested. The calibration cube is functionally analogous to the user's calibration-square concept.

### 3.2 Zhou et al. (2025) — "LIRA: Localization, Inspection, and Reasoning" (Communications Chemistry, DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1)

**Purpose:** Add vision-based localization, error detection, and recovery to SDL workflows. **Arm:** KUKA KMR iiwa with Intel RealSense D435i wrist camera. **Calibration protocol:** ArUco fiducial markers at each station; the arm moves to a calibration position, requests an ArUco pose over the network, and updates manipulation frames. Gaussian filtering and buffered-detection refinement reduce transient errors (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 9-10). **Localization accuracy:** A 20 mm ArUco marker produced a mean translation error of 2.42 mm (unfiltered) and 1.51 mm (filtered); a 75 mm marker achieved 1.02 mm; cube-based calibration achieved 0.92 mm translation and 0.90° rotation error (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5). Each visual calibration took approximately 5 seconds versus approximately 53 seconds for tactile calibration (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). **Operational test:** Over 100 workflow loops (~8 hours), 600 pickup and insertion operations, 100% success rate (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5). **Manual placement error test:** 200 tests per workflow, 97.5% success rate, with 5 failures (2 PXRD plate placement, 3 NMR rack) (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). **VLM inspection:** Fine-tuned LLaVA-OneVision 0.5B achieved 97.9% overall inspection accuracy (97.9% placement correctness, 96.8% misalignment direction, 98.9% recoverability prediction) on a 540-image dataset (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 5-7). **How it differs:** Tests the localization and inspection pipeline rather than comparing arms; uses only the KUKA KMR iiwa.

### 3.3 Lan et al. (2025) — "AutoBio" (Preprint, DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2505.14030)

**Purpose:** Provide a simulation benchmark for evaluating vision-language-action (VLA) models on biology laboratory tasks. **Arms:** Simulated (no physical arm comparison). **Tasks:** Pipetting, centrifuge operations, thermal-mixer panel control, thermal-cycler lid manipulation. **Protocol:** Trajectory-concatenation test combined 200 Close/Open thermal-cycler-lid episodes. **Results:** π0 achieved 100.0%, 96.0%, 3.0%, and 87.3% for Close, Open, Close→Open, and Open→Close respectively; RDT achieved 98.7%, 88.0%, 4.3%, and 8.7% (lan2025autobioasimulation pages 8-10). **How it differs:** Tests VLA policies in simulation, not physical arm hardware. Demonstrates that laboratory task benchmarks exist but are not yet used for arm selection.

### 3.4 Ginsburg et al. (2023) — "Exploring Benchmarks for Self-Driving Labs Using Color Matching" (SC '23 Workshops, DOI: 10.1145/3624062.3624615)

**Purpose:** Propose color matching as an accessible, repeatable SDL benchmark. **Arms:** Precise Automation PF400 plate manipulator, Hudson SciClops plate crane, Opentrons OT-2 liquid handler (ginsburg2023exploringbenchmarksfor pages 1-3). **Metrics:** Time without human input (TWH), commands completed without human input (CCWH), time per color (ginsburg2023exploringbenchmarksfor pages 6-6). **Protocol:** Iterative closed-loop optimization of pigment volumes for a target color RGB=(120,120,120), producing 128 mixtures per run. Batch sizes varied over 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 (ginsburg2023exploringbenchmarksfor pages 4-6). **Results:** Best run (B=1): 8 h 12 min TWH, 387 commands without intervention, ~4 min/color (ginsburg2023exploringbenchmarksfor pages 4-6). **How it differs:** Benchmarks whole-workcell autonomy and throughput, not individual arm accuracy or competing arm models.

### 3.5 Selvaraj et al. (2026) — "ArmnetBench v0.1" (Preprint, DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2607.24481)

**Purpose:** Parallel real-world evaluation of manipulation policies on low-cost arm farms. **Arms:** SO-101 (5-DOF, ~$200) in single-arm and bimanual configurations with three cameras and Raspberry Pi edge devices (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 1-3). **Tasks:** 12 tabletop tasks including block_stack, ring_insert, cable_clip, cable_unclip, eye_drops_to_basket, eye_drops_to_shelf, fold_tea_towel, insert_candle, open_lamp_door, transfer_cube, tool_insert, tool_removal (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 3-7, selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 7-9). **Protocol:** Seven imitation-learning policies (π0.5, π0, GR00T N1.7, Diffusion Policy, ACT, MolmoAct 2, SmolVLA) each trained on 50 demonstrations per task. Target: 30 randomized rollouts per task-policy cell, human-labeled as successful, suboptimal, or failure (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 3-7). **Results:** 2,518 rollouts total. π0.5 led at 47.6% strict success (51.5% including suboptimal); SmolVLA lowest at 15.0%. No policy succeeded on cable_clip; best task-policy combinations reached 60–86% (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 7-9). **How it differs:** Directly relevant to SO-101 validation but tests general tabletop manipulation, not laboratory-specific tasks. No industrial-arm comparator.

### 3.6 Ahlquist (2024) — "Improving Self-Driving Labs" (OSTI Report, DOI: 10.2172/2459394)

**Purpose:** Quantify system-level experiment repeatability in the MARS SDL. **Arm:** UR5e on a linear rail with OnRobot and Robotiq grippers. **Tests:** Labware pick-and-place reliability across different labware types, instrument locations, and storage areas; gripper communication failure rate; dry-run operation timing and variance (ahlquist2024improvingselfdrivinglabs pages 1-6). **Results:** With the original OnRobot gripper, 29–43% of experimental campaigns failed before completion due to communication errors; a Robotiq Hand-E replacement produced sixfold fewer communication failures (ahlquist2024improvingselfdrivinglabs pages 1-6). No millimeter-level placement-error distribution was reported.

## 4. ISO 9283: The Principal Standardized Arm Test

ISO 9283:1998 ("Manipulating Industrial Robots—Performance Criteria and Related Test Methods") is the universally referenced standard for robot arm evaluation (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 7-10, bi2020thestateof pages 3-4, bi2020thestateof pages 4-5). Its protocol defines:

- **Five target poses** arranged in an ISO cube within the robot's workspace
- **At least 30 repetitions** per pose, approached from the same direction
- **Measurement of** pose accuracy (deviation from commanded position), pose repeatability (dispersion of repeated arrivals at the same target), path accuracy, velocity characteristics, overshoot, drift, and static compliance
- **Measurement tools:** laser trackers, digital dial indicators, vision-based tracking systems

**UR5 results under ISO 9283:** Nasertorabi (2024) measured the UR5's repeatability as 0.0260 mm unloaded (mean deviation 0.0096 mm, σ 0.0055 mm) using contact-based measurement at a single point over 30 trials. Under a 4 kg payload at five ISO-cube positions, repeatability ranged from 0.0269 mm to 0.2628 mm, with P5 identified as a workspace-dependent outlier (nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 24-26, nasertorabi2024repeatabilityassessmentof pages 19-24). Pollák et al. (2020) established the UR5 test methodology using six linear incremental sensors (accuracy 0.004 mm) and 30 cycles at five points with a 5 kg load, but the retrieved pages did not expose the article's final numerical values (pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 1-2, pollak2020measurementofunidirectional pages 10-12).

**Photogrammetric approach for low-cost arms:** Chajón et al. (2026) developed a Raspberry Pi camera-based method using ArUco markers and selected ISO 9283 elements, specifically designed for self-built and resource-constrained robots. They achieved sub-millimeter planar accuracy and repeatability using a two-marker ArUco configuration, with CharUco calibration at ~1 px RMS reprojection error (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 3-4). This method is directly transferable to PiPER or SO-101 validation.

**No ISO 9283-equivalent for laboratory tasks** exists: Bi et al. (2020) emphasized that "standardized, reproducible, and repeatable methods are lacking" for integrated robotic systems, and that standards should eventually cover hardware, software, and interfaces rather than robot hardware alone (bi2020thestateof pages 3-4, bi2020thestateof pages 4-5).

## 5. Fiducial-Marker-Based Station Calibration

The user's approach of recalibrating at each station using a calibration square (AprilTag or similar fiducial) is validated by multiple published systems:

- **LIRA** uses ArUco markers at each station on a KUKA KMR iiwa in an SDL, achieving 0.92–2.42 mm translation error depending on marker size and filtering, with a tenfold reduction in calibration time compared to tactile methods (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 2-3).
- **Hernández-del-Valle et al. (2026)** fuse ArUco fiducial-marker augmented reality with YOLOv11-based perception for a polymer SDL, using ArUco tags to identify stations and perform pick–transport–place between devices with a UR5 and Ufactory 850 arm.
- **Burger et al. (2020)** used a tactile calibration cube (not fiducial markers) for sub-millimeter station registration (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56).
- **Chajón et al. (2026)** validated ArUco-based external robot characterization following ISO 9283 elements (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 3-4).
- **Korthals et al. (2019)** compared ARToolKit, AprilTag, and ArUco for robot benchmarking, establishing that all three fiducial families are viable for multi-camera tracking and camera registration in laboratory settings.

## 6. Cost Categorization

Tom et al. (2024) provide a broad cost taxonomy for SDL hardware: special-purpose hardware ~$1 million, general-purpose commercial hardware ~$10,000, and open hardware $100–$2,000 (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). The low-cost SDL review by Lo et al. (2024) catalogs 25 low-cost SDL systems with total costs ranging from $50 to $30,000, though most of these use liquid-handling devices rather than general-purpose robot arms (lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 1-3, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 3-4, lo2024reviewoflowcost pages 4-5). **No published cost-per-task comparison between low-cost and industrial robot arms for laboratory work was found.**

## 7. Imitation Learning on Low-Cost Arms

While no paper applies LeRobot-style imitation learning specifically to laboratory automation tasks, several benchmarks provide transferable evaluation protocols:

- **ArmnetBench v0.1** (Selvaraj et al. 2026, preprint) tests 7 imitation-learning policies on SO-101 arms across 12 tabletop tasks with 50 demonstrations each and 30 rollouts per evaluation (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 3-7, selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 7-9).
- **Dong et al. (2025, preprint)** evaluate diffusion policies on the SO-100 with 10 tasks, 20 demonstrations, and 10 trials per task (dong2505conditioningmatterstraining pages 13-16).
- **Cowling (2026, preprint)** systematically studies how ACT, Diffusion Policy, SmolVLA, and π0.5 scale from 12 to 200 demonstrations on a low-cost arm.

These protocols (number of demonstrations, number of evaluation rollouts, success/suboptimal/failure labeling) are directly applicable to validating imitation-learning-based laboratory tasks on PiPER or SO-101 arms.

## 8. Explicit Gaps in the Published Literature

Based on this review, the following gaps are confirmed:

1. **No head-to-head comparison** of low-cost versus industrial robot arms on laboratory tasks has been published.
2. **No standardized laboratory-task benchmark** exists for arm selection (analogous to ISO 9283 for positioning). The closest are AutoBio (simulation only) and the color-matching SDL benchmark (workcell-level, not arm-level).
3. **No "cost per task" metric** has been published for any laboratory robot arm.
4. **No paper tests the AgileX PiPER** in any laboratory or manipulation benchmark context.
5. **No paper applies imitation learning** (LeRobot-style or otherwise) specifically to laboratory tasks such as pipetting, weighing, or vial handling on physical hardware.
6. **No standard exists** for evaluating mobile manipulators or station-recalibrating arms in SDL contexts; Hifi et al. (2025) explicitly noted this gap (hifi2025aquantitativeinvestigation pages 3-4).

## 9. Recommended Repeatable Tests

Based on the published literature, the following tests could be assembled into a validation protocol for an affordable arm in an SDL:

1. **ISO 9283-based positioning repeatability** at five workspace positions, 30 cycles each, using the Chajón et al. photogrammetric method with ArUco markers and a Raspberry Pi camera (chajon2026photogrammetriccharacterizationof pages 3-4).
2. **Station recalibration accuracy** using ArUco/AprilTag markers of varying sizes, following the LIRA protocol: measure mean translation and rotation error with and without Gaussian filtering (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5).
3. **Pick-and-place reliability** for laboratory-relevant objects (vials, well plates, racks), following the LIRA operational stability test: count successes over ≥100 consecutive operations (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5).
4. **Imitation-learning task success rate** on laboratory-relevant tasks, following the ArmnetBench protocol: 50 demonstrations, 30 randomized rollouts, human-labeled outcomes (selvaraj2607armnetbenchv0.1parallel pages 3-7).
5. **System-level autonomy metrics** following the Ginsburg color-matching benchmark: time without human input (TWH), commands completed without human input (CCWH), and time per task (ginsburg2023exploringbenchmarksfor pages 6-6).
6. **Communication and gripper reliability** following Ahlquist: measure campaign failure rate over extended runs (ahlquist2024improvingselfdrivinglabs pages 1-6).

No single published protocol covers all of these dimensions, and assembling them into a unified benchmark for affordable SDL arms represents an open contribution to the field.
