# Station-to-Station Calibration of Robot Arms in Self-Driving Laboratories: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction

This review identifies published systems in which a single robot arm moves between laboratory stations and recalibrates at each one, the positioning accuracy achieved, the protocols used to measure it, and the tolerances required by downstream tasks. It then synthesizes these findings into a set of repeatable tests suitable for validating low-cost arms such as the AgileX PiPER (~$2k) and SO-101 leader/follower arms (~$200) against industry-standard platforms (KUKA iiwa, UR5e, Franka Panda, etc.) used in self-driving laboratories (SDLs).

### 2.1 The Mobile Robotic Chemist (Burger et al., 2020)

The foundational SDL mobile manipulator is the KUKA KMR system described by Burger et al. (2020, *Nature*). The robot uses a KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF arm on an omnidirectional mobile platform that navigates a 7.3 × 11 m laboratory using laser scanning. At each station, it performs a **six-point (6P) touch calibration** against a rigid rectangular cube (100 × 100 × 50 mm). Three contact points define one plane, two define a perpendicular plane, and one defines the third, establishing the cube's coordinate frame relative to the robot (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). Testing with 100 separate calibrations, with initial-position noise of up to ±15 mm in x/y and ±2.5° in orientation, showed that "the cube was recovered within 0.12 mm, while orientation offsets were reduced to less than 0.005°" (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). The robot achieved "an x–y positioning precision of ±0.12 mm and an orientation precision of ±0.005 degrees" enabling manipulations comparable to human researchers (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3). No vision system was used; the robot operated using laser scanning and touch feedback, allowing operation in complete darkness (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3). The robustness test required "navigation to the station followed by 6P calibration before manipulation" at every iteration (burger2023amobilerobotic pages 62-69). DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2442-2.

### 2.2 LIRA: Vision-Based ArUco Localization (Zhou et al., 2025)

Zhou et al. (2025, *Communications Chemistry*) introduced LIRA, which replaces the tactile 6P method with ArUco fiducial markers mounted on laboratory instruments. The evaluation protocol defined a reference position 300 mm from the calibration marker; the robot was commanded to that position after each calibration, and "translational and rotational deviations were calculated, and the procedure was repeated 100 times for statistical reliability" (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 2-3). Key results: a 20 mm ArUco marker without filtering produced 2.42 mm mean translational error and 3.46° rotational error; with Gaussian and mean filtering this improved to 1.51 mm and 1.54°; a 75 mm marker yielded 1.02 mm and 1.01°; and the tactile cube comparator achieved 0.92 mm and 0.90° (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5). Critically, visual calibration took approximately 5 seconds per station versus approximately 53 seconds for the tactile method, and "LIRA reduced total arm-manipulation time by 36.1% (873 seconds versus 1367 seconds)" (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). DOI: 10.1038/s42004-025-01770-1.

### 2.3 Mobile Robotic Process Chemist (Brass et al., 2026 — Preprint)

Brass et al. (2026, *ChemRxiv* preprint) describe a mobile robot that works between a fume hood, filtration system, weighing station, and UHPLC-MS. The system "used visual fiducial markers to compensate for mobile-base positioning errors and to localize the robot end effector," replacing the earlier 6P method and achieving "±0.41 mm translation precision and ±1.014° rotational precision" (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 7-10). The specific fiducial marker type and size are not reported in the extracted text. DOI: 10.26434/chemrxiv-2025-bsfvz.

### 2.4 Manufacturing Mobile Manipulator with AprilTag Docking (Bavelos et al., 2021)

In a manufacturing analogue directly relevant to SDL station-to-station workflows, Bavelos et al. (2021, *Applied Sciences*) describe an omnidirectional mobile manipulator that navigates between workstations, then performs "virtual docking" using fiducial markers (AprilTag and Alvar families tested). A stereo Intel RealSense camera detects the marker, establishes the station reference frame, and a "closed-loop PID controller then commands the omnidirectional wheels until the error reaches an acceptable threshold, achieving a maximum localization error of 1 cm" (bavelos2021enablingflexibilityin pages 7-9). This demonstrates the general pattern: coarse navigation followed by vision-based fine re-localization against a station fiducial. DOI: 10.3390/app11093985.

### 2.5 Other SDL Systems

**Sulaiman et al. (2026)** describe a UR5e on a Clearpath Ridgeback omnidirectional base for SDL operations, using SIFT/FLANN/RANSAC-based vision for object localization, though no numerical station-calibration accuracy is reported (sulaiman2026kinematicanalysisand pages 6-9, sulaiman2026kinematicanalysisand pages 3-6). DOI: 10.1007/s41315-025-00518-3.

**Dai et al. (2024, *Nature*)** extend the Cooper group's mobile robot approach to exploratory synthetic chemistry with multiple mobile robots sharing equipment with human researchers, but the extracted text does not provide calibration accuracy numbers. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-08173-7.

**Self-Building Labs (Bai et al., 2026 — preprint)** use wrist-mounted cameras and iterative digital-twin refinement to establish robot-to-device transforms, noting that "grasping and insertion require millimetre-scale precision" (bai2026selfbuildinglabs pages 4-7). DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2610.23826.

Key findings:

- **Close range, filtered, controlled conditions (LIRA):** A 75 mm ArUco marker at ~300 mm yields 1.02 mm mean translational error and 1.01° rotational error over 100 trials, approaching the 0.92 mm tactile reference (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5).
- **Close range, robotic arm (Franchini et al., 2023):** AprilTag/TagSLAM at <1 m achieves "translational positioning accuracy better than 1 cm" and "rotational accuracy better than 0.5 degrees" (franchini2023mechatronicdesignand pages 6-7). DOI: 10.1109/metroaerospace57412.2023.10190044.
- **Hand-camera bias (Wilcock & Iuorio, 2026):** An end-effector-mounted camera showed a mean distance bias of +14 mm for AprilTag, versus +69 mm for a tripod-mounted global camera (wilcock2026fusionoffiducial pages 8-9). DOI: 10.1007/s41693-026-00183-3.
- **Marker size matters:** Yu et al. (2021) showed AprilTag mean Euclidean error decreasing from 41.8 mm (67.5 mm marker) to 17.8 mm (126 mm marker), and ArUco error decreasing from 62.0 mm to 7.0 mm across corresponding sizes (yu2021applicationofa pages 16-18). Camera resolution also matters: at 2048×2048 pixels, AprilTag relative error dropped to 0.18% mean (yu2021applicationofa pages 16-18). DOI: 10.3390/s21051829.
- **Dynamic conditions degrade accuracy severely:** Lvov et al. (2023) report AprilTag RMSE of 5.11 cm static at 0.9 m, worsening to 8.86–9.16 cm under angular motion at 1 m (lvov2023mobilemocapretroreflector pages 4-5, lvov2023mobilemocapretroreflector pages 5-6). DOI: 10.1109/case56687.2023.10260562.

**Practical implication for low-cost arms:** At typical wrist-camera working distances of 0.2–0.4 m, with a 50–75 mm AprilTag, a Raspberry Pi camera at ≥1280×960 resolution, and temporal filtering (median of N frames), sub-5 mm translational accuracy is achievable. This is sufficient for vial handling (tolerances ~2 mm) but may be marginal for microplate seating without mechanical compliance aids.

## 4. Benchmarks for Robot Arm Accuracy in Laboratory Automation

### 4.1 Microplate Handling Repeatability (Harazono et al., 2024 — Preprint)

Harazono et al. constructed a reusable platform with dial gauges to measure microplate transfer repeatability on a Dobot M1 Pro SCARA arm. At 100% speed over 125 transfers, the interquartile range was "0.020 mm in x and 0.006 mm in y," while at 50% speed it was "0.034 mm in x and 0.014 mm in y" (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 11-15). Lid-placement success "declined sharply above Δy = 2.190 mm" (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 11-15). The study emphasizes that "repeatability can be affected by the payload, attached end effector, and temperature-related drift, and that manufacturer repeatability specifications may not represent actual loaded microplate handling" (harazono2024evaluationofmicroplate pages 1-6). DOI: 10.1101/2023.12.29.573685.

### 4.2 REPLAB: Low-Cost Arm Benchmark (~$2,000)

Yang et al. (2019, ICRA) introduced REPLAB, a standardized ~$2,000 work cell with an Interbotix WidowX 6-DOF arm. Calibration uses a checkerboard for camera registration and a 5×5 floor grid to fit an affine correction model (q = αp + β). "End-effector positions were within 2 cm of targets across the 35×40 cm workspace and within 1 cm near its center" (yang2019replabareproducible pages 2-3, yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 2-3). The benchmark includes >50,000 grasp attempts with standardized task definitions, evaluation protocols, and performance measures (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 1-2). DOI: 10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794390.

### 4.3 Nanoparticle Synthesis Platform (Dembski et al., 2023)

Dembski et al. used a dual-arm Yaskawa MOTOMAN CSDA10F for automated nanoparticle synthesis, benchmarking against three laboratory technicians each performing triplicate measurements. "Automated dosing achieved relatively small deviations and was reported to be at least as accurate as manual dosing" with "personnel costs estimated to decrease by 75% per synthesis" (dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 6-7, dembski2023establishingandtesting pages 3-6). DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-38535-6.

### 4.4 OSCAR: Open-Source Lab Robot with UR3

The OSCAR platform uses a UR3 arm (academic price ~CAD 25,000) with a Robotiq 2F-140 gripper, force-torque sensor, and Intel RealSense D415 camera. The authors note that "calibration is required for high-Cartesian-accuracy tasks such as pipet-tip attachment and colony picking" and that "robust calibration can take a few hours through trial-and-error" (pivin2026oscaramodular pages 9-10). DOI: 10.1021/acssynbio.5c00733.

## 5. Low-Cost Arms and Imitation Learning for Laboratory Tasks

### 5.1 SO-101 with ACT/LeRobot for Vial Handling (Johns & Gongora, 2026)

The most directly relevant work for the user's configuration is Johns and Gongora (2026), who used an SO-101 6-DOF leader/follower arm with a custom cylindrical-fit gripper and two RGB cameras, trained using the LeRobot framework. For laboratory vial handling, "the ACT policy achieved 34 successful placements in 36 fixed-start physical trials, corresponding to a 94.4% task success rate" while "SmolVLA achieved 31/36 successes (91.1%)" (johns2026towardautonomousrobots pages 5-8, johns2026towardautonomousrobots pages 8-9). Key design insights included that "a 45-degree oblique camera view was found preferable to a top-down view because it preserved depth, contact, height, tilt, and alignment cues" (johns2026towardautonomousrobots pages 5-8). No formal geometric calibration protocol or positioning accuracy measurement was reported; reliability came from closed-loop visual policies and mechanical gripper design rather than kinematic precision (johns2026towardautonomousrobots pages 1-5). DOI: 10.2172/3684119.

### 5.2 Placing PiPER and SO-101 in Context

**No published work was found on the AgileX PiPER arm in SDL or laboratory contexts.** The SO-101 appears only in the Johns & Gongora report. For comparison: REPLAB's WidowX arm (~$2,000) achieved ~2 cm position accuracy with open-loop commands (yang2019replabareproducible pages 2-3); industry-standard arms achieve 0.01–0.1 mm repeatability (manufacturer specifications for UR5e: ±0.03 mm; KUKA iiwa: ±0.1 mm); and the LIRA system shows that vision-based correction with ArUco markers can bring effective accuracy to ~1 mm regardless of the underlying arm kinematic error (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5).

## 6. Where No Published Evidence Exists

Several gaps in the literature should be noted explicitly:

1. **No published station-to-station calibration test has been performed on any arm costing less than ~$2,000.** The REPLAB benchmark evaluates grasping but not station recalibration. Johns & Gongora evaluate task success but not calibration accuracy.
2. **No published work reports AprilTag or ArUco localization accuracy specifically from a Raspberry Pi camera at robot-arm working distances (0.2–0.4 m).** The LIRA results use an unspecified camera; Franchini et al. do not specify camera model; published Raspberry Pi camera evaluations are from UAV contexts at longer ranges.
3. **No published cost-per-task metric exists for SDL robot arms.** Dembski et al. report 75% personnel cost reduction but not cost per successful manipulation. No study compares amortized hardware cost per task across arm price tiers.
4. **No published protocol combines station-to-station navigation, visual recalibration, and manipulation success into a single repeatable benchmark for any arm class.**
5. **Calibration time is rarely reported.** Only LIRA provides a direct comparison (~5 s visual vs. ~53 s tactile per calibration) (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8).

## 8. Summary and Recommendations

The literature on station-to-station calibration in SDLs is dominated by the Cooper group's KUKA-based mobile robotic chemist and its successors. The progression from tactile 6P calibration (±0.12 mm, ~53 s) to ArUco vision-based calibration (1.02 mm with 75 mm markers, ~5 s) demonstrates that visual fiducial calibration is now competitive with touch-based methods for most SDL tasks (zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 3-5, zhou2025localizationinspectionand pages 7-8). For a low-cost arm like the PiPER or SO-101, the most promising approach is to combine AprilTag-based station re-localization (using markers ≥50 mm at ≤400 mm distance with temporal filtering) with closed-loop imitation learning policies that compensate for residual kinematic errors through visual feedback, as demonstrated by Johns & Gongora's 94.4% vial-placement success rate on the SO-101 (johns2026towardautonomousrobots pages 5-8).

The critical missing piece is a published, repeatable benchmark that integrates station-to-station navigation, fiducial recalibration, and task success into a single protocol applicable across arm price tiers. The seven-test suite proposed in this review is designed to fill that gap.
