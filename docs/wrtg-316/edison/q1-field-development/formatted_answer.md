# Robot Arms in Self-Driving Laboratories and the Low-Cost Manipulation Frontier: A Literature Review with Repeatable Benchmarks

## 1. Field Overview: Robot Arms in Automated and Self-Driving Laboratories

### 1.1 Defining the Field

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) integrate robotic automation with artificial intelligence to accelerate scientific discovery. The most comprehensive review is Tom et al. (2024), which provides "an in-depth analysis of the state-of-the-art in SDL technology, its applications across various scientific disciplines, and the potential implications for research and industry" (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). That review distinguishes three hardware tiers for SDL automation: special-purpose systems costing approximately $1 million, general-purpose robotic-arm systems at roughly $10,000, and open hardware at $100–$2,000 (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6). The two principal arm architectures used in SDLs are six-axis articulated arms (6-DOF) and SCARA arms; six-DOF arms provide "largely unconstrained positioning and greater spatial flexibility, analogous to a human arm" and are used to "connect instruments into a coordinated automated workflow" (moran2026theriseof pages 1-4).

Moran et al. (2026, peer-reviewed in *npj Robotics*) provide a more recent survey focused specifically on robotics in SDLs, identifying six-DOF arms and SCARAs as the two dominant architectures, and noting that robotic subsystems may be commercial, bespoke, or open-source (moran2026theriseof pages 1-4). The ADePT framework (Salazar-Villacis & Benyahia, 2026, peer-reviewed in *Communications Chemistry*) introduced four core dimensions—Adaptability and Learning, Dexterity, Perception, and Task Complexity—for systematically evaluating autonomous laboratory robotics, moving the field beyond simple task-completion metrics toward a holistic assessment of capability proficiency (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 12-13, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 4-5, salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 1-2).

### 1.2 Landmark Systems

**Burger et al. (2020, *Nature*; DOI: 10.1038/s41586-020-2442-2)** defined the field with the first mobile robotic chemist, using a KUKA LBR iiwa 7-DOF arm (14 kg payload, 0.1 mm repeatability) mounted on a KMR mobile base. The system achieved positioning accuracy of "±0.12 mm" translation and "±0.005°" orientation after a touch-sensitive six-point calibration method, and "ran 43 batches of 16 samples, totaling 688 experiments" autonomously over eight days (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56, burger2020amobilerobotic pages 1-3).

**Dai et al. (2024, *Nature*; DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-08173-7)** demonstrated autonomous mobile robots for exploratory synthetic chemistry, integrating mobile robots with LC-MS and benchtop NMR, showing that "a synthesis laboratory can be integrated into an autonomous laboratory by using mobile robots that operate equipment and make decisions in a human-like way" (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6).

**UR5e-based systems** have been widely adopted: a six-DOF UR5e (0.03 mm repeatability, 850 mm reach) was used for modular flow-reactor assembly for retrosynthetic planning (salazarvillacis2026theadeptframework pages 3-4), for chemical experiment automation with demonstration-based programming (sasaki2025roboticsystemfor pages 6-8), and as the manipulation platform in mobile SDL configurations (sulaiman2026kinematicanalysisand pages 6-9). The AutoBio simulation benchmark also uses UR5e with Robotiq 2F-85 parallel gripper for biology laboratory tasks (lan2025autobioasimulation pages 13-16).

**Franka Emika Panda** (7-DOF, ~0.1 mm repeatability, 3 kg payload) serves as the standard manipulation research platform in FurnitureBench and RAMP (heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 3-4, collins2024rampabenchmark pages 1-2).

The "frugal twin" concept proposed by Lo et al. (2024, peer-reviewed in *Digital Discovery*; DOI: 10.1039/d3dd00223c) explicitly advocates low-cost surrogates of high-cost experimental platforms, establishing the philosophical foundation for democratizing SDL hardware (tom2024selfdrivinglaboratoriesfor pages 5-6).

### 1.3 The Low-Cost and Open-Source Movement

The movement toward low-cost manipulation hardware accelerated from 2019 onward with several key contributions:

- **REPLAB (Yang et al., 2019, ICRA; DOI: 10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794390)**: A ~$2,000 standardized work cell (70×40×60 cm) built around a Trossen WidowX arm, RGB-D camera, and defined workspace, providing a "task definition, evaluation protocol, performance measures, and a dataset of over 50,000 grasp attempts" (yang2019replabareproducible pages 1-2, yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 1-2). **Peer-reviewed.**

- **Blue (Gealy et al., 2019, ICRA; DOI: 10.1109/ICRA.2019.8794236)**: A 7-DOF quasi-direct-drive arm with "prototype bill-of-materials cost of $3,328" and an estimated consumer price near $5,000, providing ~4 mm repeatability and 2 kg burst payload at full extension (gealy2019quasidirectdrivefor pages 6-8). **Peer-reviewed.**

- **ALOHA (Zhao et al., 2023; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2304.13705)**: A bimanual leader-follower teleoperation system costing ~$20,000 (later described as "roughly $4,900 for an ALOHA arm"), paired with Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) for end-to-end imitation learning (zhao2023learningfinegrainedbimanual pages 7-8, hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 1-2). **Preprint (not yet peer-reviewed as a journal article).**

- **LeRobot (Cadène et al., 2026; DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2602.22818)**: An open-source library for end-to-end robot learning, explicitly supporting "low-cost, open-source robots like SO-10X and ALOHA" that "cost a fraction of proprietary industrial arms." The SO-100 and SO-101 are among the most adopted platforms, with 319,586 downloads and 58,299 episodes for SO-101 on the Hugging Face hub (hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 1-2, cadene2026lerobotanopensource pages 13-16). **Preprint.**

- **Open Robot Hardware (Patel et al., 2023, *IEEE RA Magazine*; DOI: 10.1109/MRA.2022.3225725)**: A comprehensive review of 80+ open-hardware projects noting that open hardware "lowers the barrier of entry to new technologies and can further accelerate innovation in robotics." **Peer-reviewed.**

### 3.1 Protocols Most Directly Relevant to the User's Setup

**ALOHA ACT (preprint).** The original ALOHA study used 50 teleoperated demonstrations per task (except 100 for Thread Velcro) and evaluated each task over 25 physical trials with one training seed. The six real-world tasks—Slide Ziploc, Slot Battery, Open Cup, Thread Velcro, Prep Tape, and Put On Shoe—yielded final-task success rates of 88%, 96%, 84%, 20%, 64%, and 92%, respectively (zhao2023learningfinegrainedbimanual pages 7-8, zhao2023learningfinegrainedbimanual pages 8-10). Success was judged by binary task completion with intermediate milestones scored as well. This protocol can be adapted to a single PiPER arm (substituting single-arm tasks) or to SO-101 leader-follower pairs.

**Mobile ALOHA (preprint).** Fu et al. extended ALOHA with a mobile base and co-training on static ALOHA data, reporting that "co-training can increase success rates by up to 90%" on mobile manipulation tasks. Whole-task co-trained success rates ranged from 40% (Cook Shrimp, 20 demonstrations) to 95% (Wipe Wine, 50 demonstrations) (fu2401mobilealohalearning pages 5-7).

**SO-101 demonstration-scaling study (Cowling, 2026; preprint).** This study is the most directly comparable benchmark for SO-101 arms. A single pick-and-place task (pick green ball, ignore two red distractors, place in basket) was evaluated with nested demonstration budgets of 12, 25, 50, 100, and 200. ACT was evaluated over 40 episodes per budget on ten fixed placements with a 30-second limit. ACT success scaled from "1/40 (3%) at 12 demonstrations to 37/40 (93%) at 100 demonstrations" (cowling2026from12to pages 9-12, cowling2026from12to pages 3-5). SmolVLA peaked at "21/40 (53%) at 50 demonstrations" then declined; Diffusion Policy performed poorly without trimming idle demonstration lead-in frames (cowling2026from12to pages 9-12). **This protocol is immediately repeatable on SO-101 hardware with the LeRobot stack.**

**SO-101 Bench (Hickok, 2026; preprint).** A simulated, language-conditioned spatial-manipulation benchmark using an Isaac Lab digital twin of a real SO-101, with 916 evaluation episodes across four task families (Bin, Next-to, Between, Move). Success must "persist for 5 seconds" and tasks impose geometric constraints (e.g., "the target must move at least 1 inch, remain untouched at the end, and non-target objects must not move more than 1 inch") (hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 6-8). The best learned policies achieved approximately 61% mean success, while "five policies trained without real data preserve their exact ranking on the physical SO-101" (Spearman ρ = 1.00), though "simulation overestimated absolute success by 9–31 percentage points" (hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 1-2, hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 2-4).

**REPLAB grasping (peer-reviewed, ICRA 2019).** A test set of 20 toys is grasped with a cumulative-success-rate metric; "the protocol terminates after success or after 10 unsuccessful attempts, followed by sweeping the arm over the workspace floor to perturb objects" (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 4-5). The dataset of labeled grasps (23% baseline success) provides ground truth for training grasping policies. This benchmark's $2,000 cell design is comparable to the PiPER system cost.

**RAMP (peer-reviewed, IEEE RA-L 2024).** Uses "AprilTags attached to beams and pegs" with "wrist-mounted and bird's-eye cameras" for state estimation (collins2024rampabenchmark pages 5-7). Each assembly is attempted five consecutive times, and "a successful insertion requires the peg to enter the correct hole with no stem visible, sit flush with the joint surface, and pin the required two joints" (collins2024rampabenchmark pages 3-4). This benchmark is directly relevant to setups using AprilTags and wrist cameras.

**FurnitureBench (peer-reviewed, IJRR 2025).** Uses a Franka Panda with "three Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D cameras, including wrist/front views" and measures assembly-phase completion across eight 3D-printable furniture pieces. A reproducibility study showed that "10 participants independently built new environments" and a trained policy "achieved an average of 84% of the original performance" (heo2025furniturebenchreproduciblerealworld pages 3-4). While designed for Franka, the 3D-printable furniture pieces and phase-completion metrics could be adapted to any 6-DOF arm with parallel gripper.

### 3.2 Protocols Using Industrial Arms (for Comparison Baselines)

**Burger et al. mobile robotic chemist (peer-reviewed, *Nature* 2020).** The KUKA LBR iiwa completed 688 chemistry experiments with calibrated positioning accuracy of ±0.12 mm, tested with 100 calibration trials (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6, burger2023amobilerobotic pages 52-56). This establishes an upper bound for laboratory manipulation precision.

**AutoBio biology laboratory benchmark (preprint, 2025).** Nine biology-laboratory tasks using UR5e and ALOHA arms, including pipetting, centrifuge-tube manipulation, and thermal-cycler operation. Scoring is generally binary: "success requires satisfying pose and contact requirements at checkpoints and terminal states; exceeding the time limit is failure" (lan2025autobioasimulation pages 13-16).

## 4. The Imitation Learning Pipeline: From ALOHA to LeRobot

The imitation learning approach used in the user's setup has a clear lineage:

1. **ACT (Zhao et al., 2023; preprint)** introduced "Action Chunking with Transformers," a generative model over action sequences that "allows the robot to learn 6 difficult tasks in the real world, such as opening a translucent condiment cup and slotting a battery with 80-90% success, with only 10 minutes worth of demonstrations" (zhao2023learningfinegrainedbimanual pages 7-8).

2. **Mobile ALOHA (Fu et al., 2024; preprint)** demonstrated that "co-training with existing static ALOHA datasets boosts performance on mobile manipulation tasks" with "50 demonstrations for each task" (fu2401mobilealohalearning pages 5-7).

3. **LeRobot (Cadène et al., 2026; preprint)** unified the software stack, providing teleoperation-to-deployment pipelines for SO-100/101 arms. The SO-100/101 became the most popular community platform with over 78,000 episodes contributed to the hub (cadene2026lerobotanopensource pages 13-16).

4. **Cowling (2026; preprint)** provided the first controlled scaling study on SO-101, showing that ACT trained from scratch overtook pretrained VLA policies at approximately 100 demonstrations, achieving 93% success on a pick-and-place task (cowling2026from12to pages 9-12).

5. **SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025; preprint)** demonstrated a small, efficient VLA model that "achieves performance comparable to VLAs that are 10x larger," evaluated on SO-100 (hickok2026so101benchaccessible pages 1-2).

## 5. Where No Published Evidence Exists

The following gaps were identified in the peer-reviewed literature:

- **AgileX PiPER**: No peer-reviewed paper was found reporting independent verification of PiPER's repeatability, payload, workspace, or manipulation benchmarks. The arm's specifications remain manufacturer claims.

- **SO-101 metrological characterization**: No published work provides a calibrated repeatability value (e.g., ISO 9283 pose repeatability) for the SO-100 or SO-101. Task success rates from preprints exist, but no positional accuracy assessment using a measurement instrument (e.g., laser tracker, CMM) has been published.

- **Low-cost arms in chemistry/biology SDL workflows**: No published system was found that uses SO-100/101 or PiPER-class arms for actual chemistry or biology experimentation in a self-driving laboratory. All published SDLs use industrial arms (KUKA, UR, ABB) or specialized liquid handlers. The "frugal twin" concept exists in review form (Lo et al., 2024) but has not been demonstrated with sub-$2,000 general-purpose arms for scientific experimentation.

- **Head-to-head comparisons**: No published paper directly compares PiPER, SO-101, or similar low-cost arms against UR5e or Franka on identical laboratory tasks with a controlled protocol.

- **AprilTag-based calibration for laboratory precision**: While RAMP uses AprilTags for assembly localization (collins2024rampabenchmark pages 5-7) and Cooper's LIRA module uses "fiducial markers attached to each station" (brass2026amobilerobotic pages 7-10), no published study quantifies how AprilTag-based calibration affects manipulation accuracy on low-cost servo-based arms specifically.

## 6. Recommended Tests for Your Setup

Based on the literature review, the following tests can be repeated on PiPER (6-DOF, ~$2k) and SO-101 leader/follower (~$200) arms:

**Tier 1 — Immediately repeatable with existing protocols:**
1. **Cowling pick-and-place scaling test** on SO-101: collect 12–200 demonstrations of a ball-to-basket task, train ACT via LeRobot, evaluate 40 episodes per budget on 10 fixed placements with 30 s timeout. Compare against published ACT curve (3%→93%) (cowling2026from12to pages 9-12).
2. **REPLAB-style grasping**: construct a work cell, define 20 test objects, measure cumulative success rate over grasp attempts with stop-after-10-failures protocol (yang2019replabareproduciblea pages 4-5).

**Tier 2 — Adaptable with minor modifications:**
3. **ALOHA ACT tasks** (single-arm variants): adapt Slide Ziploc, Slot Battery, or Open Cup tasks to PiPER; collect 50 demonstrations and evaluate 25 trials per task (zhao2023learningfinegrainedbimanual pages 7-8).
4. **RAMP assembly** with AprilTags: 3D-print beam and peg components, attach AprilTags, use wrist and fixed cameras for localization, run 5 consecutive assembly attempts (collins2024rampabenchmark pages 3-4, collins2024rampabenchmark pages 5-7).

**Tier 3 — Novel contributions (filling literature gaps):**
5. **ISO 9283-style positional repeatability test**: command the arm to a set of predefined poses across the workspace, measure achieved position with a calibrated measurement tool, report RP (pose repeatability). No such test has been published for PiPER or SO-101.
6. **Laboratory vial-transfer benchmark**: measure grasp-and-place success rate for standard laboratory vials (e.g., 10 mL GC vials), comparing against Burger et al.'s ±0.12 mm calibrated positioning (burger2020amobilerobotic pages 4-6).

## 7. Summary

The field of robot arms in self-driving laboratories has evolved from expensive, proprietary industrial systems (KUKA, UR5e, ~$35k–$100k+) toward increasingly affordable platforms. The "low-cost, open-source" movement, catalyzed by ALOHA (2023) and LeRobot (2026), has produced platforms costing $120–$200 (SO-101) to ~$2,000 (PiPER, REPLAB). However, the literature reveals a critical maturity gap: industrial arms used in SDLs have peer-reviewed metrological characterization and demonstrated scientific workflows, whereas ultra-low-cost arms have only preprint-level task-success benchmarks and no demonstrated SDL applications. Filling this gap—by running repeatable benchmarks on low-cost arms and comparing directly against published industrial-arm baselines—represents a clear opportunity for new contributions to the field.
