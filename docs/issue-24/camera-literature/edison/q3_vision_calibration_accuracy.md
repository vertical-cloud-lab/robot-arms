## Query

Setup: a tabletop 6-DOF robot arm (AgileX PiPER, about 0.63 m reach, fingertips reach about 0.76 m at table height) on a 1.29 x 1.36 m table against a wall. We want fixed RGB cameras (Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 Wide, 102 x 67 deg field of view, 4608 x 2592 px) that (a) triangulate the gripper fingertips to check the arm's joint-angle kinematics (detect drift of a few mm), (b) show whether the workspace is clear, and (c) may later feed learned visuomotor policies. Current proposal: one camera about 1.2 m above the table looking straight down, one about 0.8 m from the arm base past the table end the arm faces at joint 1 = 0, about 0.42 m up and tilted 19 deg down, and a third low camera on the room-side table edge looking toward the wall. For checking or calibrating the kinematic accuracy of a low-cost serial robot arm with fixed external cameras and a fiducial (ArUco, AprilTag or ChArUco board, or a sphere) on the end effector, what camera distances, viewing angles and numbers of cameras have given the best end-effector position accuracy, what accuracies (mm) were achieved, and how does the viewing angle of a planar fiducial (frontal vs oblique) affect its pose and depth accuracy? Does the evidence favour two or more cameras at large angular separation over a single camera?

## Evidence relevant to the camera layout

**Yes—two calibrated, substantially separated views are preferable to one for an independent three-dimensional check of the PiPER fingertips.** A third view is valuable primarily for surviving occlusion and improving coverage; it does not automatically improve precision. The studies below do not establish a universally optimal camera distance or crossing angle, nor do their best reported errors directly predict accuracy on this table. In particular, distinguish *camera-measurement error* from *robot error measured using cameras* and from *target-registration error after fitting a reference frame*. (sideris2026larkalowcost pages 12-13, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 13-14, wang2023separationandcalibration pages 21-23)

The closest direct robot-calibration example is Wang and colleagues’ binocular measurement of three ceramic spheres on an ABB six-axis arm. At approximately **0.5–1.0 m** camera-to-end-effector distance, the mean discrepancy between commanded and vision-measured movements fell from **0.7873 to 0.2604 mm** after kinematic calibration; the post-calibration maximum was **0.7107 mm**. These are discrepancies *relative to the camera system*, not an independently established 0.26-mm accuracy for its sphere localization, and the industrial arm and optics differ from PiPER and Camera Module 3 Wide. (wang2023separationandcalibration pages 10-12, wang2023separationandcalibration pages 15-17, wang2023separationandcalibration pages 19-21, wang2023separationandcalibration pages 21-23)

For a controlled camera-count comparison, Sideris and colleagues placed **five** Raspberry Pi global-shutter cameras around a **72-cm-diameter** fixture approximately **1 m above** the measurement volume and tracked ArUco-marked three-dimensional fiducials. Their cropped Table 1 shows improving target-registration performance overall from one to five views, particularly on a three-dimensional phantom, but an intermediate three-camera triangulation configuration was *worse* than some two-camera configurations on the planar grid. Their approximately **0.64-mm** five-camera grid figure is a median *individual point* error; the corresponding **0.7477-mm** value in Table 1 is a median of trial/configuration RMSEs. Neither should be read as demonstrated fingertip accuracy across PiPER’s entire workspace. (sideris2026larkalowcost media 0630648e, sideris2026larkalowcost media 76d359f3, sideris2026larkalowcost media 54ca3ecb, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 3-4, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 9-10, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 12-13, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 13-14)

## Why viewing angles matter

There are **two different angles** to optimize. First, the *angle between rays from different cameras to the fingertip* should be substantial: nearly coincident cameras provide little independent depth information. For a conventional parallel stereo pair, depth uncertainty scales approximately as **Z²σd/(fB)**, where *Z* is range, *f* focal length in pixels, *B* baseline, and *σd* disparity uncertainty. This is a geometric approximation, **not** a measured error for the proposed cameras. A roughly orthogonal overhead–side pairing near the working region can therefore be useful, provided **both cameras actually see the same identifiable feature**. A wide baseline that loses joint visibility or produces severe marker foreshortening is counterproductive; LARK’s non-monotonic subset results illustrate that qualification. (sideris2026larkalowcost pages 10-12, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 12-13, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 13-14, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 8-9)

Second, consider the angle **between each camera’s sightline and a planar marker’s normal**. A nearly face-on tag can have excellent apparent area yet weak perspective cues for its *tilt*, permitting competing near-mirror pose estimates; an extremely oblique tag loses projected area and detectable corners. In a particularly relevant **single fixed-camera**, approximately **220-mm-range** experiment, a 35-mm planar AprilTag gave **2.18° ± 0.80°** frontal orientation error, versus approximately **0.15–0.6°** across oblique cells with commanded tilts of **15–30°**. Its positional RMSE over translations made *while held frontally* was **1.50 mm**, compared with **0.17–0.20 mm** for non-coplanar four-tag markers occupying the same footprint. Those position figures combine coordinate directions, including commanded depth changes: the study does **not** establish a separate 1.50-mm depth error. Crucially, its reference was robot-commanded *relative* motion with an alignment fitted on the evaluated measurements—not external absolute ground truth. Moderate obliquity helps the planar tag’s **orientation conditioning**, but is not a guarantee that its **depth translation** improves monotonically. (wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 12-18, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 18-22, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 10-12, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 36-42, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 42-44)

A separate HoloLens/ArUco evaluation found millimetre-scale, view- and distance-dependent *depth-axis biases*, demonstrating why tiny frame-to-frame variation is not evidence of accurate depth. But its marker was held perpendicular to the camera ray while sample locations moved from −30° to +30° in bearing; those results **cannot** be interpreted as a controlled comparison of frontal and tilted marker planes. At some extreme locations detection itself failed. (costa2024assessmentofmultiple pages 9-10, costa2024assessmentofmultiple pages 15-17)

## Recommendation for the proposed PiPER setup

1. **Keep the overhead camera for workspace clearance and policy context, but do not rely on it alone for fingertip height.** From the stated 102° × 67° field of view, an ideal camera pointed vertically down from **1.2 m** covers about **2.96 × 1.59 m** at table height—enough for the stated tabletop if its axes are suitably oriented and the table is centred. At the stated full resolution, that is roughly **0.64 mm per horizontal pixel** and **0.61 mm per vertical pixel** at the table plane; subpixel feature detection is possible, but these figures are *sampling pitches*, not calibrated 3D accuracy. Verify coverage at the **highest fingertip and arm positions**, where the footprint contracts, as well as at table height.

2. **Use the end-facing camera and a genuinely lateral room-side camera as the principal triangulating pair; also fuse the overhead view wherever the same fiducial is visible.** Aim for complementary, substantially separated rays through the *actual fingertip work volume*, rather than prescribing one universally optimal angle at the arm base. The proposed end camera’s **19° downward** aim and **0.42-m** height may yield a shallow sightline across the far tabletop and arm, while the low edge view may be blocked by objects or fingers. Test visibility and ray crossing at the near, far, high and low extremes before permanently mounting them. If the lateral camera cannot see the *same* feature as the end camera at those poses, move or raise it; three installed cameras can otherwise act like only one useful camera.

3. **Track a rigidly attached feature with a known transform to the fingertip tool-centre point (TCP).** A tag or ChArUco board supplies identified corners for cross-camera matching and calibration; a sphere avoids dependence on marker facing direction, but requires reliable centre detection and identity if multiple spheres are used. A rigid, multi-face or otherwise non-coplanar marker helps maintain identifiable corners as the wrist turns. Place the fiducial near the fingertips, characterize its mounting offset, and check that any orientation error does not become a large position error through that lever arm. LARK calibrated both camera extrinsics with a ChArUco board and the three-dimensional marker-corner geometry, then rejected inconsistent corner observations rather than trusting all views indiscriminately. (sideris2026larkalowcost pages 3-4, wang2023separationandcalibration pages 10-12, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 8-9, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 4-5)

4. **Validate the measurement system separately from the kinematics.** Calibrate each camera’s intrinsics and wide-lens distortion at its final focus and resolution; estimate inter-camera extrinsics with a measured board seen across the three-dimensional working volume, and securely mount cameras independently of the moving arm. Establish the board/table/robot-base transform and marker-to-TCP offset; then compare visually triangulated positions against encoder-based forward kinematics at *held-out poses spanning height and reach*. Report repeatability, systematic error and worst-case error separately, including fingertip poses with partial occlusion. To claim that a detected few-millimetre discrepancy is true robot drift, verify camera/TCP accuracy against an **independent** measured target or metrology reference—not merely the same robot commands used for calibration. Static image capture reduces blur and timing concerns; for motion, synchronize observations. (sideris2026larkalowcost pages 5-7, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 12-13, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 12-18, maghami2024visionbasedtargetlocalization pages 14-17, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 4-5)

**Bottom line:** the evidence supports **at least two well-calibrated cameras with complementary, appreciably separated sightlines**, and preferably a third for this cluttered, moving tabletop. It does **not** show that three cameras must always outperform two, that exactly 90° is optimal, or that one wide, overhead monocular image can independently verify few-millimetre *3D* fingertip drift. A single close camera with a well-designed **3D fiducial** can nevertheless provide useful relative pose measurements; that is a different, more marker- and calibration-dependent claim than robust multiview triangulation. (sideris2026larkalowcost pages 12-13, sideris2026larkalowcost pages 13-14, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 18-22, wakiuchi2610quartetmarkera pages 10-12)
